# Part 3 Episode 5 — When the heavy-rain map and the watershed map overlapped, the role of the soil auxiliary wheels finally became visible

## Foreword

In Part 3 Episode 4, we looked at both the world’s El Niño and marine heatwaves around Japan as a world map and as the sea underfoot.

In Episode 5, the viewpoint shifts further toward land.

Heavy-rain warnings.

Landslides.

River flooding.

A rain-cloud radar on television and watershed lines drawn on a map.

This episode has the protagonist imagine one river somewhere in Japan, overlap heavy-rain news with the condition of upstream soil, and begin counting the auxiliary wheels that can move here.

## Section 3 — Soil auxiliary wheels change how water moves before it reaches the river

“Soil auxiliary wheels…”

I traced the watershed map with my finger.

“They are not flashy technology like deep-sea aeration.”

‘No.’

“They are more like quiet technologies that change how water moves.”

‘Yes.’

“For example…”

“Making field soil act more like a sponge.”

“Leaving understory vegetation in forests.”

“Using more permeable pavement in parts of residential areas.”

“Increasing small retention basins.”

“Returning concrete channels slightly toward soil and stone.”

‘Yes.’

“When heavy rain falls…”

“Instead of everything rushing into the river at once…”

“Some of the water enters the soil once, then comes out slowly.”

‘Yes.’

“That becomes an auxiliary wheel that lowers the peak water level a little.”

‘As we saw in Part 2.’

“It is not conspicuous, but it is very important.”

‘The quieter auxiliary wheels can sometimes be the most important in social implementation.’

“There you go saying things like that again.”

## Section 5 — The list of auxiliary wheels that can move in this river

I opened my notebook and wrote: auxiliary wheels that can move in this river.

“For example, what seems realistic in this watershed is…”

“Attempts to increase cover crops and compost in upstream fields.”

“Efforts to increase permeable pavement and small rain gardens in midstream residential areas.”

“Forest management that shifts a little from only cutting to also leaving.”

“Revisiting existing retention ponds and rice fields as temporary flood-storage functions.”

“Redeveloping parts of the riverside as soft banks rather than only hard revetments.”

‘Realistic.’

“None of them changes everything immediately.”

‘Yes.’

“Only some plots.”

“Only some blocks.”

“Only some slopes.”

‘Yes.’

“But even so, the way water moves can change.”

‘It can.’

“The peak water level may drop by even a few centimeters.”

“Soil outflow may decrease a little.”

“The river’s muddiness may become slightly better.”

‘Those are auxiliary-wheel effects.’

“It is not a flashy success.”

‘No.’

“But as something better, it is fairly realistic.”

## Afterword

Thank you for reading.

In Part 3 Episode 5, we brought the hard mode of sea and sky — the world’s El Niño and marine heatwaves — down to the story of heavy rain and flooding in one river in Japan.

What this episode did was to look again at heavy-rain news and rain-cloud radar not only as how much rain will fall, but as how much water will flow into which river’s watershed.

Beneath the red areas on a rain-cloud radar, there is always a river.

That river has an upstream area.

It has a middle reach.

It has a downstream area.

There are forests, fields, housing areas, roads, and human lives there.

In Episode 5, the protagonist spreads that watershed map in his mind, sees upstream fields and forests, midstream housing and roads, downstream towns and riverbanks as one map, and imagines how water moves inside it.

What became visible there was the soil auxiliary wheel.

Sponge-like soil.

Permeable pavement.

Small retention basins.

Rain gardens.

Soft riverbanks.

Understory vegetation in forests and cover on fields.

These are not flashy technologies like deep-sea aeration.

But during heavy rain, they may slightly change how water moves before it reaches the river, and may slightly reduce peak water levels or sediment outflow.

They are exactly auxiliary wheels that slightly change how water moves.

The protagonist’s own role was also put into words again here.

He is not a flood-control expert.

He is not a politician or a river manager.

But he can be a citizen looking at the watershed map.

Instead of hearing news only as weather information, citizens may begin receiving it as the story of this river.

Placing that possibility was also part of this episode: if such citizens increase even slightly, the premise for moving soil auxiliary wheels may change a little.

Part 3’s overall theme is how far auxiliary wheels can be attached to society. Episode 5 begins pulling that theme down not only to sea and sky, but also to the scale of rivers and soil.

In Part 3 Episode 6, the perspective will move to specific watersheds and municipal efforts.

Upstream soil regeneration and farmland adjustments.

Midstream experiments with rain gardens and permeable pavement.

Downstream reevaluation of soft riverbanks and retention areas.

While interviewing these efforts, the protagonist will draw a more concrete picture of soil auxiliary wheels that are beginning to move in reality.
