# Part 3 Episode 8 — Searching on the same summer map for a city that releases heat and a city that does not push too much heat toward the sea

## Foreword

In Part 3 Episode 7, we placed heavy-rain years and quieter years side by side and looked at the viewpoint of the people who keep soil auxiliary wheels running, along with the difficulty of continuing them.

In Episode 8, the perspective shifts to the air and the city.

Extreme heat.

Heatwaves.

Urban heat islands.

Waste heat from air conditioning.

Places inside a city where heat accumulates, and places where some of it can escape.

Against the background of Japan’s summer news, the protagonist begins searching on one summer map, including the sea, for a city that keeps pushing heat into its surroundings and a city that has mechanisms for releasing it.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading.

In Part 3 Episode 8, against the background of Japan’s extreme-heat news — dangerous heat, heatwaves, and urban heat islands — we placed the heat of the sea and the heat of the city back onto the same summer map.

Here, the heat of the sea means high sea surface temperatures and marine heatwaves around Japan.

The heat of the city means urban heat created by the combination of heat stored in asphalt and concrete, poor ventilation, and waste heat from buildings and air-conditioning equipment.

These two phenomena are not produced only by the same cause.

Nor is urban air-conditioning waste heat itself the main cause of marine heatwaves.

However, the sea, the air, and the city exist inside the same circulation in which heat is received, stored, and moved elsewhere.

Episode 8 revisited that connection without oversimplifying it, beginning from the familiar structure of pushing heat outside the city in order to cool one’s own room.

The first distinction confirmed in this episode was between a city that releases heat and a city that traps it.

Tree shade.

Waterfront spaces.

Wind corridors.

Mist used under suitable conditions.

Ground surfaces that do not store too much heat.

Insulated and heat-shielded buildings.

Efficient air-conditioning systems.

Where those elements are combined, fragments of a city that releases heat can be found.

By contrast, in places with no shade, blocked airflow, heat-storing ground and buildings, and concentrated outdoor-unit exhaust, heat becomes harder to release.

Mist cooling was also not described as equipment that cools any place simply by being installed.

Where humidity is high and wind is weak, evaporation may be insufficient and the effect may be limited.

For that reason, mist needs to be combined with shade, airflow, water supply, hygiene management, temperature, and humidity conditions.

From a station to a hospital.

From a school to an evacuation facility.

From a shopping street to a park.

If cooling equipment combined with shade and airflow can be placed along routes people must still travel during extreme heat, mist can move closer to being part of infrastructure that protects daily life rather than a summer event.

The episode also reframed wind corridors as invisible infrastructure.

Differences in airflow created by building placement and road structure may appear as nothing more than lines on a plan.

But in an actual city, they become differences in human thermal experience: this alley is easier to breathe in, while this intersection feels stagnant.

Wind speed.

Surface temperature.

The hours when shade appears.

Places where residents actually feel extreme heat.

If those layers can be overlaid on a map, then as a watershed map revealed how water moves, it may become possible to visualize where heat is trapped in the city and where it escapes.

The protagonist’s role was also reorganized here.

He is not an urban-planning specialist.

He is not in a position to decide building placement or infrastructure investment.

Even so, he can become a citizen who walks with a heat map.

Which road is scorching?

Which alley has airflow?

Which park can serve as an escape point?

Which road lacks shade?

By gathering those physical sensations, he can stand on the side that asks what auxiliary wheels could enter this city.

Part 3 Episode 8 made a rough catalog of air and urban auxiliary wheels after the sea and soil: mist cooling used under suitable conditions, wind corridors, tree shade, waterfront spaces, permeable or water-retaining ground, building insulation and heat shielding, and the reduction and dispersion of air-conditioning waste heat.

In Part 3 Episode 9, carrying this catalog, the protagonist and the AI will visit cities that actually place mist and shade as heat countermeasures and regions that are trying to integrate wind corridors and green spaces into urban planning.

There, through the air of specific places and the voices of people, the story will show where attempts to expand air auxiliary wheels work and where they stop.
