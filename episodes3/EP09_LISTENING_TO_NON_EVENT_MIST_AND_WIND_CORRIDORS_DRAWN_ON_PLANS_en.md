# Part 3 Episode 9 — Going to hear about mist that is not an event and wind corridors drawn into the plans

## Foreword

In Part 3 Episode 8, we organized mist cooling, wind corridors, tree shade, and waterside spaces as elements of a city that releases heat — a rough catalog of air auxiliary wheels.

In Episode 9, we go to see examples that have actually begun operating as infrastructure.

Mist that does not end as a summer-only event.

Wind corridors that were drawn into the plans before the buildings were constructed.

This episode follows the protagonist as he listens to officials and citizens about where air auxiliary wheels work when they enter a real city, and where they stop halfway.

---

“Isn’t the phrase ‘uchimizu event’ reaching its limit?”

I muttered while looking at a poster near the station.

“Let’s all sprinkle water together.”

“Cool the ground and fight the urban heat island!”

It was not a bad idea.

It was old wisdom, and I understood the principle that water takes heat from its surroundings when it evaporates.

But considering this year’s sea surface temperatures and extreme heat, it honestly felt a little underpowered.

‘Uchimizu is not bad. But it easily ends as a once-a-year ritual,’ the AI displayed in the corner of my phone screen.

“Exactly.”

“In Part 3 Episode 9, I want to see the version that is not a ritual.”

‘Mist and wind that are not rituals?’

“Yes. Mist that is not an event, and wind corridors included in the plans from the beginning.”

## Section 1 — What the person responsible for mist that is installed and removed every year really thinks

The first place I visited was the office of a shopping-street association near the station.

Hoses extended from the ceiling of the arcade.

Mist operated only during summer.

Walking through it felt a little cooler.

At the same time, depending on the location, droplets fell onto your shoulders.

“The mist feels nice.”

When I said that, the association representative smiled.

“We appreciate hearing that, but…”

“There is a ‘but’?”

“There is.”

“Is it the work?”

“It is.”

The representative counted on their fingers.

“Every year, we bring out the hoses and hang them.”

“We inspect the pumps.”

“We clean clogged nozzles.”

“We check for water leaks.”

“When the season ends, we take everything down again.”

“Yes.”

“We also apply for subsidies.”

“Oh.”

“Under the category of creating summer liveliness.”

“So that category allows the budget?”

“It can.”

“But the name is liveliness.”

“Exactly.”

The representative gave a wry smile.

“To be honest, I would like it to be treated as heatstroke prevention, or as equipment that slightly improves the city’s thermal environment.”

“But institutionally, it is categorized as an event.”

“Yes.”

“Doing it this year does not guarantee a budget next year.”

“No.”

“If the person in charge changes, or the burden on the association becomes too large, it may stop.”

“That is possible.”

At that point, I felt I could see part of the boundary between mist as an event and mist as infrastructure.

It was not only whether it felt cool.

Who installs it?

Who manages it?

Who pays for it?

Will it still be there next year?

Only when those things are decided can the auxiliary wheel remain attached to the city.

## Section 2 — The plans for mist with permanent year-round piping

The next person I interviewed worked in a newly redeveloped district in another city.

Fine mist flowed from above a plaza.

“There is mist here.”

“Yes.”

“Is it only for summer?”

“We operate it only when temperature and humidity conditions are appropriate, but the equipment itself is permanent.”

“Oh.”

“The pipes and pumps are fixed, and they were incorporated from the design stage of the buildings and plaza.”

“So it was treated as infrastructure from the beginning.”

“It is classified as shared facility equipment.”

The official showed me the plans.

“This is the plaza, and this is the main airflow.”

“Yes.”

“We determined the mist location and spray direction to match the wind corridor.”

“So it is paired with wind.”

“Yes.”

“Rather than cooling stagnant air here…”

“Yes.”

“The idea is to add evaporative cooling to air that is already moving.”

“That starts from a different concept than the shopping street.”

“I think the shopping-street equipment is also important under its local conditions.”

“Of course.”

The official became a little more serious.

“Ideally, existing shopping streets could also install permanent mist lines like this.”

“But?”

“Who pays the initial cost?”

“Yes.”

“Who covers water, electricity, and inspection costs?”

“Yes.”

“Who repairs it when it breaks?”

“There it is — maintenance cost.”

“That is ultimately the issue.”

The official pointed to the edge of the plan.

“If it is included at the design stage, it is easier to classify it as shared infrastructure for the building or district.”

“And if it is added later?”

“You must decide one by one where to install the equipment, the route for water pipes, the power supply, owner consent, and who will be responsible for maintenance.”

“The difficulty suddenly rises.”

“Considerably.”

## Section 3 — The decisive difference between event mist and infrastructure mist

‘Organize it,’ the AI said.

I sat on a bench and opened my notebook.

“First, the characteristics of event mist.”

Installed every year and removed every year.

Subsidy categories such as liveliness or seasonal events.

A limited group, such as a shopping-street association, carries the work of installation and management.

Operating period and budget may change every year.

“Next, mist as infrastructure.”

Piped in from the design stage of buildings and districts.

Treated as shared equipment for the whole facility.

Maintenance costs are included in management fees or common expenses.

Operated as part of thermal-environment design, including wind, shade, plazas, and pedestrian movement.

‘The difference is clear now.’

“It is.”

“In Part 3 Episode 9, I want to describe this as a difference in how auxiliary wheels enter society.”

‘What do you mean?’

“An auxiliary wheel used as an event tends to depend on the determination of the people who keep it going.”

‘Yes.’

“An auxiliary wheel used as infrastructure depends on institutions, design, and management systems.”

‘Yes.’

“The first is easy to start but difficult to continue.”

‘Yes.’

“The second is difficult to start, but easier to continue once it is incorporated.”

‘That tendency probably exists.’

“This is not about one being righteous and the other being wrong.”

‘Correct.’

“In an existing city, sometimes the event model is the only possible starting point.”

‘Yes.’

“But if we ask how far auxiliary wheels can be attached to society, we need to see this difference.”

‘It connects directly to the theme of Part 3.’

## Section 4 — A city where wind lines were drawn on the plans from the beginning

Wanting to hear more about wind corridors, I met another person involved in urban planning.

“It feels a little cooler walking here.”

The official smiled.

“That was intentional to some extent.”

“I thought so.”

“This street was planned as a wind corridor.”

The official spread out an urban-planning map.

“This side faces the sea.”

“Yes.”

“In summer, when conditions are right, wind enters from this direction.”

“Right.”

“So the buildings are set back slightly, creating a strip that is less likely to block the wind.”

“That is a setback?”

“Yes.”

The official traced the plan with a finger.

“This strip also connects to parks and green spaces.”

“Yes.”

“We also used ground finishes that store as little heat as possible.”

“So it is a set of wind, greenery, and ground.”

“Exactly.”

“Even if buildings let wind through, it helps less if the ground is scorching.”

“That is correct.”

“How did it work after construction?”

“I cannot say everything followed the design exactly.”

“Because actual wind is complicated?”

“It changes depending on surrounding buildings, time of day, and season.”

“Yes.”

“Still, residents do say that this street feels a little cooler.”

“That is significant.”

“It is.”

The official lowered their gaze slightly.

“But it is difficult to apply this design to every district.”

“Because of rebuilding schedules and property rights?”

“Yes.”

“Road width.”

“Yes.”

“Land ownership.”

“Yes.”

“Building height and placement.”

“Yes.”

“Floor area that commercial facilities want to secure.”

“None of that can be surrendered only for wind.”

“Exactly.”

Closing the plan, the official said:

“In a city built from zero, wind corridors can be drawn from the start.”

“Yes.”

“Drawing a thick wind line afterward through an existing city is extremely difficult.”

## Section 5 — The gap between places that can be built anew and places that already exist

‘How does it look?’ the AI asked.

“It is the same structure as the ocean auxiliary-wheel discussion.”

I compared the plan with the actual street.

“With the ocean, there was a difference between sea areas where conditions were relatively easy to organize and areas already entangled in complex interests.”

‘There was.’

“With air and urban auxiliary wheels, the same thing happens between new cities and existing ones.”

‘Yes.’

“In redevelopment areas designed from scratch, wind corridors and mist can be included from the beginning.”

‘Yes.’

“But in an existing city, even removing one parking space is difficult.”

‘Yes.’

“Widening a road.”

‘Yes.’

“Moving a building backward.”

‘Yes.’

“Installing new piping.”

‘Yes.’

“Every one of those collides with someone’s land, cost, and daily life.”

‘Correct.’

“If we ignore that gap and say every city should do this, air auxiliary wheels become another universal-solution theory.”

‘The trap we repeatedly avoided in Part 2.’

“Exactly.”

“So Episode 9 must clearly describe where they can realistically enter and where the strain appears.”

‘A good brake.’

“In new places, attach them from the beginning.”

‘Yes.’

“In existing places, attach them partially, starting with high-priority locations such as school routes, station areas, and hospital surroundings.”

‘A realistic approach.’

“Do not make it all or nothing.”

‘That is an auxiliary wheel.’

## Section 6 — Citizens accurately remember cooler streets and hotter streets

While walking from the shopping street to the redevelopment area, I asked several citizens questions.

“Where around here is the place that feels unbearably hot?”

“The rotary in front of the station.”

The answer came immediately.

“And where is still a little better?”

“The shaded part of this street and the plaza in that new area.”

“The place with mist?”

“Yes. And wind passes through there.”

“Do you change your route on hot days?”

“I do.”

“Instead of the shortest route to the station, I take the road with tree shade.”

“Even if it is a detour?”

“If it is only a few minutes, it is easier.”

Citizens had surprisingly precise heat maps in their memories.

“Do you use those places like evacuation spots?”

“I do not think of them that formally.”

“Yes.”

“But on hot days, I try to go that way.”

‘A citizen walking with a heat map,’ the AI said.

“Exactly.”

“If voices like this are gathered, it may become easier to discuss adding more air auxiliary wheels.”

‘Bodily experience can supplement measurement results.’

“Some things cannot be understood from thermometers alone.”

‘Perception changes with shade, wind, humidity, walking distance, and physical condition.’

“So we look at both numbers and experience.”

‘The same as with soil auxiliary wheels.’

## Section 7 — The deadlines faced by the people trying to expand them

One final remark from the redevelopment official stayed with me.

“Thermal-environment design tends to be postponed.”

“I thought so.”

“Disaster prevention.”

“Yes.”

“Crime prevention.”

“Yes.”

“Transportation.”

“Yes.”

“Commerce, housing, and landscape.”

“They are all important.”

“They are.”

The official nodded.

“And when we then say, let’s consider heat as well…”

“Yes.”

“It easily becomes something to do if there is room.”

“Right.”

“But if summers like this continue, I think ‘if there is room’ will no longer be enough.”

“Does it feel like a deadline is becoming visible?”

“Yes.”

“After the city is completed, it is too late to say there was no wind corridor.”

“Yes.”

“If we realize that roads used by elderly people and children have no shade, we cannot change them immediately.”

“True.”

“If we miss a rebuilding or redevelopment opportunity, the next one may be decades away.”

“So heat design also has a deadline.”

“It does.”

That word, deadline, overlapped with a keyword continuing from Part 2, and it sounded unusually heavy.

Farmers had harvest deadlines.

Municipalities had budget and disaster deadlines.

Companies had financial deadlines.

Cities have deadlines for choices that can only be made before construction.

Wind corridors cannot easily be moved after buildings are built.

Air auxiliary wheels also have a period when they can be attached.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading.

In Part 3 Episode 9, among the air auxiliary wheels organized in Episode 8, we compared mist cooling and wind corridors operating as events with examples beginning to be incorporated as infrastructure.

We looked at where they work, where they stop, and what deadlines and constraints are carried by the people involved.

The first comparison was between event mist and infrastructure mist.

The shopping street’s mist, installed and removed every year, receives subsidies under categories such as liveliness or seasonal events. A limited number of people, such as the shopping-street association, carry the labor of installation, removal, cleaning, and inspection.

It is an important attempt to create cooler places locally, but budget conditions and staff burden mean it may not continue the next year.

This is an auxiliary wheel as an event, supported by human effort.

In contrast, the mist in the redevelopment area was piped in from the design stage of the buildings and plaza and treated as shared facility equipment.

Maintenance costs were incorporated into management fees or common expenses, and it became part of thermal-environment design that included airflow, shade, and pedestrian movement.

This is an auxiliary wheel as infrastructure, supported by institutions and design.

The event model is easier to start, but tends to place a larger burden on those who continue it.

The infrastructure model requires more coordination and cost before starting, but once incorporated, it is easier to continue.

Episode 9 described this as a difference in how auxiliary wheels enter society.

Wind corridors also revealed the difference between a city built from zero and an existing city.

In redevelopment areas, planners can consider sea-breeze direction and surrounding terrain, set buildings back, and plan wind bands connecting parks and green spaces.

By also considering surface materials and shade, they can intentionally create streets that feel a little cooler.

But attempting the same afterward in an existing city encounters many constraints: land ownership, road width, building placement, commercial floor area, piping, and power supply.

The same auxiliary wheel must be attached differently in places built from zero and places that already exist.

Ignoring that reality and saying every city should install the same system would turn air auxiliary wheels into another universal-solution theory.

In existing cities, a realistic approach is to begin partially in high-priority locations: station areas, hospital surroundings, school routes, and streets frequently used by elderly people, adding shade, small mist systems, and resting places.

Citizens’ heat maps also became important material.

Residents remember with surprising accuracy which places feel unbearably hot and which feel slightly better.

On hot days, they choose shaded and windy routes instead of the shortest route.

They pass through plazas with mist.

They accept a small detour to use a cooler road.

These daily choices also provide information about where heat countermeasures are needed in the city.

The need to combine numerical measurements with citizens’ bodily experience mirrors the structure used for soil auxiliary wheels, where farmers’ memories were combined with municipal records.

An urban-planning official also used the phrase thermal-environment design has a deadline.

Urban planning must prioritize disaster prevention, crime prevention, transportation, commerce, housing, and landscape.

Within that list, thermal conditions are often postponed as something to consider if there is room.

But after buildings are built and roads and plazas are arranged, creating new wind corridors is not easy.

If a redevelopment or rebuilding opportunity is missed, the next may be decades away.

Some air auxiliary wheels can only be attached during the design stage.

Part 3 Episode 9 organized what happens when air auxiliary wheels are actually attached to society across three layers: places that can be newly designed, cities that already exist, and citizens who use them.

How far air auxiliary wheels can be attached is determined by the balance among the side that designs, the side that maintains, and the side that uses.
