# Part 3 Episode 6 — Listening to the person who made soil into a sponge and the person who made a rain garden as one watershed story

## Foreword

In Part 3 Episode 5, we overlaid the heavy-rain map and the watershed map, and confirmed that soil auxiliary wheels that change how water moves have a role to play.

In Episode 6, the protagonist goes to listen to the people who are actually moving those auxiliary wheels.

A farmer upstream who made soil into a sponge.

A resident in the middle reaches who made a rain garden.

And a municipal staff member who sees the whole area as a watershed map.

This episode is where small attempts moving separately within the same river’s watershed begin to connect as one story.

---

“There should already be people moving in this river.”

I said that while closing the watershed map.

In Episode 5, I practiced receiving heavy-rain news as the story of a river.

Now it was time to actually go listen to people upstream and in the middle reaches of that river.

‘Will you start upstream or midstream?’ the AI asked.

“Upstream, I think.”

‘Why?’

“Because that is the first soil the rain meets after it falls.”

‘Correct.’

“You praised me instantly for once.”

‘This one was easy to understand.’

“That somehow makes me a little frustrated.”

## Section 1 — In an upstream field, listening to the person who made soil into a sponge

A few days later, I arrived in a small mountain town.

The upper reaches of the river.

A place where several narrow tributaries gather.

Fields on slopes.

Forests above them.

“Good to see you again.”

A farmer I had spoken with a little in Part 2 welcomed me with a smile.

“The rain this year has been incredible.”

“It has.”

Beside the greenhouse, there was a field with a slightly different atmosphere.

Grass remained.

The soil looked soft.

When I stepped on it, it sank a little.

‘A sponge plot,’ the AI said.

“How is this plot doing?” I asked.

The farmer thought for a moment.

“It has both good points and bad points.”

“Could you tell me in detail?”

“The good points show after rain.”

“Oh.”

“In other fields, water collects on the surface and then flows away.”

“Yes.”

“Here, it feels like the soil drinks it once.”

“Drinks it.”

“Even when the rain is strong, it does not turn into muddy runoff right away.”

“That is something the river side would appreciate too.”

“I think so.”

“What about the bad points?”

“Labor.”

The farmer laughed.

“You put in cover crops.”

“Increase compost.”

“Change how you till.”

“Yes.”

“It takes a little more work than the usual way.”

“What about yield?”

“In the first year, it dropped a little.”

“Right.”

“But I feel the difference shows more in years with a lot of rain.”

“Difference?”

“The difference in how things collapse.”

That phrase felt strangely heavy.

---

## Section 2 — The difference in how things collapse may become the difference in river news

‘How does it feel?’ the AI asked.

“Hearing the phrase difference in how things collapse from a farmer, not difference in yield, is pretty big.”

I said that while looking at the field.

‘Why?’

“News talks in terms of whether damage occurred.”

‘Yes.’

“Did the river flood?”

“Did a landslide occur?”

‘Yes.’

“But people in the field are looking at differences in how things collapse.”

‘Yes.’

“This slope collapses easily.”

“This field flows away easily.”

“This forest holds.”

‘Yes.’

“So soil auxiliary wheels are technologies that change the difference in how things collapse.”

‘Exactly.’

“If those differences accumulate, they may become a difference in the news.”

‘Probably.’

“The difference between the river flooded and it barely held.”

‘Yes.’

“And there is someone seeing that in one single field.”

‘That is the upstream auxiliary wheel.’

I crouched at the edge of the field and pressed the soil lightly with my finger.

Soil that does not collapse easily even when it contains water.

Soil that receives rain once before turning into muddy runoff.

It is not flashy.

But beneath a heavy-rain map, increasing this kind of soil one place at a time probably means something.

## Section 3 — In a midstream town, listening to the person who made a rain garden

Leaving the upstream area, I came down to a town in the middle reaches.

A place where the river becomes a little larger.

Residential areas.

A small park.

Roads.

“Here it is.”

I was guided to the front yard of an ordinary house.

But if you looked closely, it was a little different.

The ground was soft.

The center was slightly lower.

Plants that let water pass easily grew along the edge.

“This is a rain garden,” the owner said.

“I saw something like this on the news.”

“Really?”

“A garden that holds rainwater once and then lets it flow slowly, right?”

“Roughly speaking, yes.”

I was a little impressed.

“How was it after you made it?”

“At first, honestly, it was like an extension of a hobby.”

“Yes.”

“I like gardening.”

“Right.”

“But during heavy rain, I saw water come here and stop here once. Then I understood what it meant.”

“Until then, did you think rainwater was something that just flowed away quickly?”

“Yes.”

“It falls from the roof, enters the gutter, and goes to the river.”

“But now it enters the garden once.”

“Yes.”

“One house will not change much.”

“But I thought that if places like this increased, something might change.”

Those words felt connected somehow to the farmer’s phrase: the difference in how things collapse.

One house does not change the world.

One field does not change a river.

But if many small ways of receiving water line up inside the same watershed, the way water moves may change a little.

## Section 4 — The upstream sponge and the midstream rain garden stand together inside the same watershed

‘How does it look?’ the AI asked.

“The sponge field upstream and this rain garden…”

I opened the watershed map on my phone as I spoke.

“On the map, they are inside the same line.”

‘Yes.’

“Inside the ring of this river’s watershed.”

‘Yes.’

“Upstream, soil drinks water.”

“In the middle reaches, a garden holds water.”

‘Yes.’

“And that changes the way water moves before it reaches the river, just a little.”

‘Yes.’

“It is not only upstream or only midstream.”

‘No.’

“They stand together.”

‘That is the image of Part 3 Episode 6.’

“As an image, it is plain.”

‘Plain, but structurally strong.’

“Structure again.”

‘It is Part 3.’

“That convenient phrase is working today too.”

I placed my finger on the watershed map.

The upstream field.

The midstream rain garden.

The river flowing between them.

During heavy rain, raindrops do not know the lines on a map.

But humans can see those lines.

Inside those lines, where is water received, and where does it rush away at once?

Trying to see that is probably the first step of the soil auxiliary wheel.

## Section 5 — Soil auxiliary wheels do not need to demand coordination immediately

“When I talk about this…”

I said with a small wry smile.

“I immediately want to say, let us coordinate.”

‘You probably would.’

“The upstream farmer, the midstream resident, the municipality, companies…”

‘Yes.’

“I want to connect everything and turn it into a big project.”

‘That habit appeared in Part 2 too.’

“But at Episode 6, I feel we do not have to ask that much yet.”

‘Why?’

“Because first, it is more important to find the fact that there are people like this in the same watershed.”

‘Yes.’

“If I suddenly wave a flag saying let us coordinate, something will probably break somewhere.”

‘It would break easily.’

“So in Episode 6, I want to keep it at the level of warm-up exercises for coordination.”

‘Warm-up exercises?’

“Write down once that upstream in this river there is this person, midstream there is this person, and the municipality is thinking about this.”

‘That itself has meaning.’

“Before connecting them, first find them.”

‘Yes.’

“Find them, give them names, and place them on the same map.”

‘That is the job of the person writing the story.’

## Section 6 — The municipal staff member had a map surrounded by lines

Finally, I stopped by the town hall in the midstream town.

“May I ask a little about watershed flood control?”

When I asked the staff member, they showed me several documents.

“This is the watershed of this river.”

There was a line drawn on a large sheet of paper.

River.

Tributaries.

Mountains.

Town.

“This is the most dangerous area during heavy rain.”

“Yes.”

“This slope has a high landslide risk.”

“Yes.”

“These rice fields can be used as flood-storage areas.”

“Oh.”

“This area has room to improve the permeability of residential land.”

“That is the place that came up in the rain garden conversation.”

“Yes.”

The staff member continued.

“To be honest, we cannot do everything.”

“Of course.”

“We do not have enough budget, time, or people.”

“I understand.”

“But even just mapping what is happening inside this line and where there is room to act changes how we talk.”

“How you talk?”

“Not only, please watch out for landslides.”

“Yes.”

“We can say, please watch this slope and this area beside the river.”

“That is big.”

“It is.”

The staff member traced the watershed line with a finger.

“When there is a map, fear becomes a little more concrete.”

“Yes.”

“And at the same time, what can be done becomes a little more concrete.”

With those words, I felt I understood a little.

A watershed map is a map of danger, and at the same time, a map for finding auxiliary wheels.

## Section 7 — The protagonist is on the side of writing the story inside the line

Leaving town hall, I walked along the river.

The upstream sponge field.

The midstream rain garden.

The watershed map at town hall.

All of them were very quiet.

They were different from the flashiness of news captions.

Different from the red heavy-rain radar.

‘How will you close this?’ the AI asked.

“Part 3 Episode 6…”

I said while looking at the river.

“It was an episode about writing the story inside the line.”

‘Line?’

“The watershed line.”

‘Yes.’

“Inside this line, there is the upstream farmer.”

“There is the midstream resident.”

“There is the municipal staff member.”

“There is the river.”

“There are forests and soil.”

‘Yes.’

“Those people are each moving soil auxiliary wheels a little in their own places.”

‘Yes.’

“I write that as one story.”

‘That is your role.’

“Coordination can come next.”

‘Next is fine.’

“First, record that people like this are inside the same line.”

‘That alone has meaning.’

I looked at the flow of the river.

Rain swallowed by the upstream field.

Rain that stopped once in the midstream garden.

Rain that ran along roads.

Rain that entered gutters.

Rain that headed for the river.

Even when it is the same rain, its movement changes depending on where it meets the ground.

And within the same watershed, there are people trying to change that movement just a little.

Part 3 Episode 6 ends here.
