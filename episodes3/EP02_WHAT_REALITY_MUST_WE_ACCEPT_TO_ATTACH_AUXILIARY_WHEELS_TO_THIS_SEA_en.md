# Part 3 Episode 2 — If we attach auxiliary wheels to this sea, how much reality must we accept?

## Foreword

In Part 3 Episode 1, we unfolded the map for looking at the Trinity auxiliary wheels — sea, air, and soil — from the side of society.

Episode 2 focuses on the ocean side.

If deep-sea aeration were to be introduced into an actual sea area, what lines of reality would have to be crossed?

Technology.

Fisheries.

Borders.

Budgets.

Consent.

This episode has the protagonist imagine a specific sea area and collide the desk-bound Trinity Model with field conditions for the first time.

---

“If I attach auxiliary wheels to this sea, how much reality do I have to accept?”

After typing that into the title field of a new file, I opened my folder of sea photographs.

They were from when I was writing Part 1.

Blue sea.

White waves.

A horizon stretching endlessly.

‘Those photos are from quite a while ago,’ the AI displayed in the corner of my phone screen.

“But as a starting point, this is where it begins.”

‘What do you mean?’

“When I first started writing about the sea in Part 1, the sea in my head was a beautiful scene like this.”

‘I see.’

“But in Part 3, the sea is not ‘the sea’ in that sense anymore.”

‘No.’

“It is a sea where ocean heat has accumulated.”

“Where coastal fish are declining.”

“Where red tides and hypoxic water masses appear.”

“Where the lives of coastal towns are shaking.”

“Where national borders and EEZ lines are drawn over the water.”

‘Yes.’

“We are talking about attaching auxiliary wheels to that kind of real sea.”

‘That gap is what this episode has to show.’

---

## Section 1 — Think not about some sea, but about this sea

“First, we have to be able to say not ‘some sea,’ but ‘this sea.’”

I wrote “sea area” in large letters in my notebook.

“In Part 1, I often talked as if it were the world ocean in general.”

‘You did.’

“But if we actually do deep-sea aeration, it becomes this sea.”

‘Yes.’

“For example…”

I wrote several conditions.

Large accumulation of ocean heat.

Impacts on coastal fisheries and towns.

Problems with red tides or hypoxic water masses.

Currents and seafloor topography that make it meaningful to lift deep water.

A realistic possibility of agreement among countries or regions.

“The first auxiliary wheels would probably be attached to a sea area that meets conditions like these.”

‘Probably.’

“In other words, unless we can say ‘here’ instead of ‘somewhere,’ the discussion cannot move forward.”

‘Because this is implementation.’

---

## Section 2 — This sea from the technical side

‘Let us organize it from the technical side too,’ the AI said.

‘Deep-sea aeration requires several conditions.’

“Depth, right?”

‘Yes.’

“You need to lift cold, nutrient-rich water from a certain depth.”

‘Yes.’

“If it is too shallow, you are only stirring the water.”

‘Exactly.’

“Topography matters too.”

‘Yes.’

“The seafloor shape and currents need to have a direction that can carry the lifted water toward the coast.”

‘Yes.’

“So the candidates are places that are deep enough and where lifting water actually means something.”

‘From the technical side, yes.’

“But if we stop there, the sea is chosen only for the convenience of engineers.”

‘Correct.’

“In Part 3, we have to layer people’s lives, politics, and economics on top of that.”

‘Exactly.’

---

## Section 3 — Fishermen, ports, and coastal towns inevitably enter the story

“Deep-sea aeration…”

I said while looking at the sea photographs.

“If we describe it only as a technology that cools the sea, it becomes very abstract.”

‘It does.’

“But in reality, it is also a technology that changes the condition of fishing grounds.”

‘Yes.’

“The nutrients rising toward the coast, the species of fish, fishermen’s income, port activity, processing plants, and the local economy are all connected.”

‘Yes.’

“So when we talk about attaching auxiliary wheels to this sea, the fishermen, the port, and the coastal town must enter the discussion.”

‘They should.’

“It is not enough to say ocean heat will decrease or carbon uptake will increase.”

‘Not enough.’

“Unless we also talk about how fish may change, how fishing may change, and how the yearly rhythm of the port may change, the field probably will not move.”

‘You need to show local risks and expectations together.’

---

## Section 4 — Borders, lines, and who takes responsibility

“There is another thing we cannot avoid: lines.”

‘Lines?’

“National borders and EEZs.”

‘Yes.’

“The sea has clean lines on maps.”

I traced an invisible line in the air with my finger.

‘Yes.’

“But seawater, heat, nutrients, and fish do not care about those lines.”

‘No.’

“Water lifted by deep-sea aeration could flow toward the coast of a neighboring country.”

‘It could.’

“Fish could move into another country’s waters.”

‘Yes.’

“That becomes one reason why something that makes technical sense can still be politically difficult.”

‘It probably does.’

“So attaching auxiliary wheels to this sea means asking…”

“Who takes responsibility?”

“Who decides?”

“Who receives the benefits?”

“Who carries the risk?”

‘Yes.’

“We have to think about all of that together.”

‘That is unavoidable in Part 3.’

---

## Section 5 — Look calmly at budgets and scale

‘We also need to talk about money,’ the AI said bluntly.

“There it is.”

‘It matters.’

“Deep-sea aeration means putting large equipment in the ocean.”

‘Yes.’

“Ships.”

“Equipment.”

“Power.”

“Maintenance.”

‘Yes.’

“And it does not end after one use.”

‘No.’

“It assumes operation over years, maybe decades.”

‘Yes.’

“So it becomes initial investment plus continuing cost.”

‘Correct.’

“And then we have to attach numbers for expected carbon uptake or ocean heat reduction and explain that to politics and companies.”

‘The scale has to be seen calmly.’

“In Episode 2, I want to imagine deep-sea aeration not as a dream technology, but as a document that could be brought to a meeting.”

‘Good positioning.’

---

## Section 6 — The protagonist thinks not as an ocean citizen, but as a coastal citizen

“About my own position…”

I thought for a moment before speaking.

“In Part 1, I was someone who looked at the sea.”

‘Yes.’

“From an observation deck, or from a ferry deck. I saw the sea as scenery.”

‘Yes.’

“But in Part 3, if I look at the sea, I have to look as a coastal citizen.”

‘Probably.’

“Look from a fishing port.”

“Look from a port town.”

“Look through coastal news.”

“Look through the lives of fishermen.”

‘Yes.’

“Then the story of mixing the sea through deep-sea aeration stops being only science. It becomes very much a story of daily life.”

‘That is the core of the ocean side of Part 3.’

---

## Section 7 — Sometimes the answer may be: not yet for this sea

“There is one thing I want to say clearly.”

I became a little more serious.

“Part 3 is about how far auxiliary wheels can be attached to society.”

‘Yes.’

“But there may be many cases where the answer is: this sea cannot have them yet.”

‘There may.’

“Technically difficult.”

“Politically difficult.”

“Difficult because of fisheries.”

“Difficult because of budgets.”

‘Yes.’

“In that case, we probably should not conclude that we must force it anyway.”

‘Better not.’

“Instead, we should say that in this sea, for now, some other auxiliary wheel or other measure should be prioritized.”

‘Probably.’

“In other words, a part about how far auxiliary wheels can be attached is also a part about where they should not yet be attached.”

‘Both have to be seen at the same time.’

---

## Section 8 — Still, I want to find at least one place I can call this sea

‘Then what will you do?’ the AI asked.

“To be honest…”

I smiled a little.

“In this part, I want to find at least one place where I can say, maybe auxiliary wheels could realistically be attached to this sea.”

‘Greedy.’

“I am greedy.”

‘But the story probably needs it.’

“In Part 1, we touched the possibility that the sea might be cooled.”

“In Part 2, we touched the possibility that soil and watersheds might be repaired.”

“In Part 3, I want to show at least one sea where the idea of auxiliary wheels barely reaches the line of reality.”

‘That becomes hope.’

“I do not want to use the word hope lightly.”

‘After passing through Part 2, it will not be light.’

“I hope so.”

---

## Section 9 — Episode 2 only needs to make the sea reality checklist

“To summarize…”

I folded my hands in front of the keyboard.

“The job of Part 3 Episode 2 is to create a reality checklist for the sea.”

‘Yes.’

“Ocean heat.”

“Coastal life.”

“Fisheries.”

“Borders and lines.”

“Budgets.”

“Technical conditions.”

‘Yes.’

“For each one, we ask how much reality must be accepted if auxiliary wheels are attached to this sea.”

‘Exactly.’

“Then, from Episode 3 onward, we can look at specific sea areas one by one.”

‘Natural structure.’

“So Episode 2 is the episode that prevents us from suddenly sinking a device into the sea.”

‘If you suddenly sink it, you ignore everything Part 2 built up.’

“I do not want that.”

Part 3 Episode 2 ends here.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading.

In Part 3 Episode 2, we organized the real-world conditions for the ocean-side auxiliary wheel by asking what it would mean to introduce deep-sea aeration into this sea.

In simple terms, this episode cools down the excitement once.

On the technical side, we looked at depth, seafloor topography, and currents.

It is not enough for a sea to be deep. There must also be a physical reason why lifted water and nutrients could meaningfully reach the coast.

On the life side, we placed fishermen’s income, the yearly rhythm of ports, changes in fish species, and coastal-town economies into the discussion.

Mixing the sea is not merely a scientific experiment. It touches fishing grounds and people’s lives.

On the political and boundary side, we looked at national borders, EEZs, who decides, who takes responsibility, and how benefits and risks are divided.

Changing one sea area may affect neighboring countries or other coasts.

On the economic and budget side, we checked the scale of initial investment, continuing cost, and how to explain expected carbon uptake or ocean heat reduction.

Instead of only hoping that the sea might be cooled, as in Part 1, we imagined deep-sea aeration as a document that would have to survive a meeting.

The important point in this episode was the need to say not “some sea,” but “this sea.”

It was also important to create a checklist: if auxiliary wheels are attached to this sea, this much reality must be accepted.

Part 3 is about how far auxiliary wheels can be attached to society, but it is also about where they should not yet be attached.

This episode honestly recognizes that through the conversation between the protagonist and the AI.

Without this step, the story would move forward only on the excitement from Part 1 that the sea might be cooled, ignoring the reality of life and structure seen in Part 2.

Part 3 Episode 2 is also a brake.

In Episode 3, we will take this checklist and step into a specific sea area.

We will look more concretely at how far the idea of attaching auxiliary wheels to this sea can ride the line of reality, together with the voices of fishing ports and coastal news.
