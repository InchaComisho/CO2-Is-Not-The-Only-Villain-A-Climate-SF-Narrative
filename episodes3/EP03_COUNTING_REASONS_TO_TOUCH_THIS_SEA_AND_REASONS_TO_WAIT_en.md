# Part 3 Episode 3 — While watching fishing-port news, counting the reasons to touch this sea and the reasons to wait

## Foreword

In Part 3 Episode 2, we organized the real-world conditions needed for the ocean-side auxiliary wheel by asking what would be required if deep-sea aeration were introduced into this sea.

In Episode 3, the protagonist carries that checklist and imagines a more specific sea. From fishing ports, news, and coastal life, he counts the reasons to intervene and the reasons to wait.

Even if a sea looks technically meaningful, it will not move unless it crosses the lines of daily life, politics, and budgets.

This episode looks at the idea of attaching auxiliary wheels to this sea for the first time as a sea that appears in the news.

---

“I want to count properly the reasons to touch this sea and the reasons to wait.”

Looking at the checklist from Part 3 Episode 2, I said that.

Depth.

Topography.

Currents.

Fisheries.

Borders.

Budgets.

There was one sea in my head whose conditions seemed almost lined up.

A sea whose name I had heard in the news.

A sea where red tides had appeared along the coast after heavy rain.

A sea where declining catches had become a topic.

A sea where, every summer, news captions said the sea surface temperature was higher than usual.

‘It is a good step to talk while imagining a specific sea,’ the AI displayed in the corner of my phone screen.

“In Part 1, I lumped everything together under the word sea.”

‘You did.’

“In Part 3, unless I get to the words this sea, I probably cannot touch reality.”

‘Probably.’

---

## Section 1 — This sea that appears in news headlines

“Hey.”

I opened a news app as I spoke.

“Headlines about changes in the sea have increased recently, haven’t they?”

‘They have.’

“High water temperatures.”

“Poor fish catches.”

“Red tides.”

“Hypoxic water masses.”

“Record sea-level rise.”

‘Yes.’

“All of these are also items on the checklist for seas that could become candidates for auxiliary wheels.”

‘Very much so.’

“Large accumulation of ocean heat.”

“Impacts on coastal fisheries and towns.”

“Problems with red tides or hypoxic water masses.”

‘The conditions listed in Episode 2.’

“So a sea that appears in the news can also become a candidate for discussing auxiliary wheels.”

‘That follows.’

“Conversely, attaching auxiliary wheels to a sea that does not appear in the news may be politically very difficult.”

‘Probably.’

“When a problem is not visible, budgets and attention do not gather easily.”

‘That is also reality.’

---

## Section 2 — Ocean heat does not appear in morning conversations at the fishing port

I closed the news and opened my folder of fishing-port photos.

A morning port.

Trucks loaded with fish.

Containers filled with ice.

A fisherman with a cigarette in his mouth.

A port cat.

“The term ocean heat probably does not appear in these people’s conversations.”

‘Probably not.’

“What appears is…”

“There aren’t many sardines this year.”

“The fish seem small somehow.”

“We have to repair the nets more often.”

“Fuel costs are getting painful.”

‘Yes.’

“Ocean heat is a currency in the world of news and researchers, but at the fishing port, it appears in units of fish.”

‘That gap is important.’

“In Part 3 Episode 3, I want to look at that gap properly.”

‘What do you mean?’

“It is not enough to say that if auxiliary wheels are attached to this sea, ocean heat will decrease by some percentage.”

‘Yes.’

“Unless we can also put into words how the fish species and catches at this port might change several years later, the field probably will not move.”

‘Exactly.’

“Unless numbers are translated into the language of daily life, the story of the sea probably will not reach the port.”

‘A very Part 3 kind of task.’

---

## Section 3 — The list of reasons to intervene

‘Then make a list of reasons to touch this sea,’ the AI prompted.

I opened my notebook and started writing.

“The reasons to intervene are roughly these.”

To reduce accumulated ocean heat even slightly.

To stabilize coastal fish species and catches over the long term.

To reduce red tides and hypoxic water masses.

To ease abnormal summer heat in coastal towns a little.

To support the sea’s function as a carbon sink.

‘Yes.’

“In other words, attaching auxiliary wheels to this sea means…”

“We want to cool the sea.”

“We want fish to return.”

“We want to reduce red tides.”

“We want to make coastal summers a little easier.”

“We want the sea to absorb a little more carbon.”

‘Those are the directions of the reasons.’

“So the Part 1 idea of cooling the sea gets translated in Part 3 into a set of daily-life and carbon reasons.”

‘Yes.’

“Ocean heat, fisheries, red tides, coastal summer, carbon uptake.”

‘Yes.’

“They look separate, but as reasons to touch this sea, they sit in the same table.”

‘That is important.’

---

## Section 4 — The list of reasons to wait

‘Now list the reasons to wait,’ the AI continued.

I picked up my pen again.

“The reasons to wait are probably like this.”

Impacts on fisheries are uncertain and difficult to read.

Ripple effects on neighboring countries or neighboring coasts are unclear.

Initial investment and maintenance costs are large, and the project may lose in budget priorities.

Impacts on the seafloor and ecosystems cannot yet be fully evaluated.

There is an argument that other measures, such as reducing watershed-side loads, should be done first.

‘Yes.’

“In other words, attaching auxiliary wheels to this sea means…”

“It may bring risks into fisheries.”

“It may bring sparks into relations with neighboring countries or coasts.”

“It may take budget away from other measures.”

“It may place unexpected stress on the seafloor and ecosystems.”

“It may get the priorities wrong.”

‘Those are the directions of the reasons.’

“When writing the ocean-side auxiliary wheels in Part 3, I have to show the reasons to intervene and the reasons to wait together.”

‘If you write only one side, you forget what Part 2 taught you.’

“So hope alone is not enough, and anxiety alone is not enough either.”

‘Yes.’

“Put both on the same page.”

‘That is the checklist.’

## Section 5 — Who decides whether auxiliary wheels are attached to this sea?

“And…”

My voice became a little more serious.

“Who decides whether auxiliary wheels are attached to this sea?”

‘Good question.’

“Researchers.”

“Politicians.”

“Fishermen.”

“Coastal municipalities.”

“National governments.”

“International organizations.”

‘Yes.’

“It is not something one person decides.”

‘Absolutely not.’

“That is an extension of Part 2, where we let go of the habit of searching for the single climate solution.”

‘What do you mean?’

“It is not a structure where one hero decides everything.”

‘Correct.’

“It is not only the government deciding.”

“It is not only companies deciding.”

“It is not only scientists deciding.”

‘Yes.’

“It becomes a multiplayer cooperative game of who takes on what.”

‘That structure does not change in Part 3.’

“So in Episode 3, I want to leave the question of who decides somewhat blurred, and instead describe who takes on what.”

‘That is more accurate.’

“If the story is only about decision-making authority, readers will probably feel it is far away.”

‘Yes.’

“But if it becomes what fishermen take on, what municipalities take on, what the state takes on, and what citizens understand, it becomes a little closer.”

‘That perspective matters.’

‘Yes.’

“From the fisheries side, there is a line where people do not want to bet everything, but it may be worth trying.”

## Afterword

Thank you for reading.

In Part 3 Episode 3, based on the ocean-side auxiliary-wheel checklist made in Episode 2, we imagined a real sea through news headlines, morning conversations at fishing ports, coastal-town summers, borders, and EEZ lines. Through the conversation between the protagonist and the AI, we organized the reasons to intervene in this sea and the reasons to wait.

The first important point was that words like ocean heat and carbon uptake appear in fishing ports and coastal life as fish species, catch volume, and port economies.

The auxiliary-wheel discussion needs to proceed while recognizing the gap between the language of technology and research and the language of daily life.

That was the first confirmation of this episode.

Deep-sea aeration has reasons to intervene.

Cooling the sea.

Bringing fish back.

Reducing red tides.

Making coastal summers a little easier.

Having the sea absorb more carbon.

At the same time, there are reasons to wait.

Risks to fisheries.

Relations with neighboring countries.

Competition over budgets.

Uncertain impacts on the seafloor and ecosystems.

Cautious arguments that other measures should be prioritized first.

In Part 3, it is important to place both of these on the same page.

Whether auxiliary wheels should be attached to this sea cannot be decided only by researchers, politicians, or fishermen.

It is a multiplayer cooperative structure involving many roles, not a story where one hero decides everything.

The protagonist himself also stands not on the deciding side, but on the watching-and-writing side.

Episode 3 is a chapter that, without yet naming a specific sea, assumes a sea seen in the news and from a fishing port, and carefully counts the reasons to touch this sea and the reasons to wait.

In Part 3 Episode 4, carrying this organization forward, we will imagine one sea area where an auxiliary-wheel discussion has begun to move or is being debated, and the protagonist and the AI will pick up fragments of the discussion there: support, opposition, and caution.

It will be the first step into the field where the ocean-side auxiliary wheel changes from a desk-bound Trinity Model into the story of a sea appearing in the news.
