# Part 7 Episode 10 — The first page of the white file, and the day the first auxiliary wheel turns

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 9, the failures that happened in the previous town were not treated as embarrassing records. They were carried out as a map so the next town would not stumble in the same places.

If mist is placed without looking at the wind, it can dampen storefronts.

Before a rain garden receives water, it has to think about where that water can escape.

Facilities that store water need explanation not after completion, but before construction begins.

And expanding auxiliary wheels did not mean copying the previous town’s answer. It meant making it possible for a new town to test them under its own conditions.

In Episode 10, the new town finally places its first auxiliary wheel.

But it is not a finished form.

There is no grand completion ceremony.

There is no perfect budget table yet.

What exists is a white file, a small trial run, and a little courage to say, “Let’s try it once first.”

This is the episode where an auxiliary wheel leaves the planning document and enters the site.

---

“The white file is still white.”

I looked at the file placed on the table in the community room.

The records brought from the previous town were already thick.

The direction of the mist.

Stopping thresholds on windy days.

The depth of rain gardens.

Overflow outlets.

Insufficient explanation around the retention basin.

The morning when the water-temperature chart missed.

But the new town’s file was different.

Only the cover.

Only the headings.

Inside, it was almost blank.

“From here, we write this town’s record.”

“The first page is usually a little frightening.”

“Because we might write something wrong?”

“Because there is still so little that can be written.”

Across the table sat the people who had gathered last time.

The bakery worker.

People from the shopping street.

The schoolteacher.

The landscaping worker.

The disaster-prevention officer.

Nearby residents.

People who worked at the distribution center.

“What are we deciding today?” the bakery worker asked.

I opened the file.

“We are not deciding everything.”

“Huh?”

“Today, we try only one thing.”

The AI displayed a short line on the screen.

Station-front bus stop: mist trial run.

“Here it comes.”

“Auxiliary wheels begin by turning small.”

“Not by changing the whole town?”

“Today, we look at the town’s conditions.”

“We turn it in order to see the conditions.”

“Exactly.”

On the first page of the white file, I wrote the date.

Nothing had succeeded yet.

Nothing had failed yet.

But the first page had been opened.

## Section 1 — In a planning document, wind does not blow

At the station-front bus stop, a simple mist device had been placed.

It was not permanent.

The water tank was small.

There were only two nozzles.

The operating time was one hour in the afternoon.

“It’s smaller than I expected,” the schoolteacher said.

“Isn’t it too small to change the heat of the whole town?”

“It is small,” I answered.

“But today is not the day we change the whole town.”

The bus-stop roof.

The bench.

The bakery entrance.

The sidewalk.

The direction where wind passes through.

Using the previous town’s failure map, we checked each position.

“Keep the nozzles away from the bakery.”

“Yes.”

“If the wind becomes strong, stop it.”

“Who decides that?”

“Today, I will watch. Next time, we make a duty schedule.”

“Who refills the water?”

“Today, the municipal side. We will not make it a shopping-street-only job.”

The bakery worker looked slightly surprised.

“You decide that much from the beginning?”

“In the previous town, that caused trouble later.”

“The failure map changes the order of preparation.”

“And,” I said, pointing in front of the bus stop.

“Today, we also look at the effects.”

“How?”

“How many people use it. Whether it looks easier to wait. Whether the wind causes trouble anywhere. How much water it uses.”

A person from the distribution center folded his arms.

“You’re taking numbers too?”

“We are. But we will not decide by numbers alone.”

“Then what else?”

“We also leave the impressions of the people here.”

The AI added:

“‘It felt cooler’ and ‘the goods did not get damp’ are both records.”

“Voices saying it helped, and records saying it did not cause trouble.”

“Both are needed next.”

In a planning document, wind does not blow.

On paper, water does not run out.

The bakery’s paper bags do not get damp.

But on site, all of that happens.

So today, we turn it small.

We turn it small and watch the town’s response.

## Section 2 — The unexpected begins with the first drop

One o’clock in the afternoon.

The sky was bright.

The road was hot.

The bench held enough heat to make people hesitate before sitting down.

“Ready?” the disaster-prevention officer asked.

“Ready,” I nodded.

The bakery worker was watching from the shop entrance.

The schoolteacher stood a little away with the children.

People from the shopping street stood beside the tank.

The landscaping worker, for some reason, was looking at the sky.

“Why are you looking up?”

“The wind looks like it might change.”

“Please say that before we start.”

“It is changing now.”

The AI flashed a little more strongly.

“Wind speed rising.”

“An event happened before we even started.”

“Should we stop?” the disaster-prevention officer asked.

I remembered the red text from the previous town.

Stop on strong-wind days.

But decide what level of wind counts as stopping.

“What is the wind speed now?”

“It has not yet reached the stopping threshold for this trial run.”

“Then let’s run it briefly while watching the bakery entrance.”

“Understood.”

The switch turned on.

Shhh.

A fine mist came from the nozzles.

For an instant, the air turned white.

People in front of the bus stop looked up.

A child laughed.

“Oh.”

“It’s a little cooler.”

The bakery worker stretched a hand outside the entrance.

“This side looks fine.”

But right after that, the wind turned.

The mist flowed sideways.

“There it is!”

Before I could say it, the landscaping worker pointed.

“Toward the sidewalk! Not the entrance!”

A person from the shopping street adjusted the nozzle angle slightly.

The disaster-prevention officer looked at the timer.

The bakery worker checked the front of the shop.

“The paper bags are fine!”

“What about the bench side?”

“It’s cool. But it’s hitting too strongly!”

“Lower it one level.”

“Can we do that?”

“We made it two-level adjustable for today.”

For a moment, I almost puffed out my chest.

The AI said:

“That is the result of turning the previous town’s failure into a function.”

“Do not say that now.”

The nozzle strength was lowered.

The mist stayed near the people waiting.

It did not reach the bakery entrance.

“This might be okay,” the bakery worker said.

“It is still only ‘might,’” I answered.

“Let’s watch for one hour.”

“The first auxiliary wheel begins by recording the ‘might.’”

“You have a lot of good lines today.”

“Because this is a trial run.”

## Section 3 — The real test of the auxiliary wheel comes after the applause

The trial run attracted more people than expected.

People waiting for the bus.

People returning from shopping.

People out walking.

Children on their way home from school.

People who simply stopped because they wondered what had begun.

“Can I make an on-site record?”

“Please do. But handle the bakery entrance carefully in the record.”

“Why?”

“Because today, that is the most important place.”

At one-thirty in the afternoon, notes increased on the AI screen.

People who used it: 28.

More people stayed near the bench.

Bakery paper bags: no dampness.

Wind direction changed twice.

Nozzle adjusted once.

Water remaining: decreasing faster than expected.

“That last one bothers me,” I said.

“At this rate, the tank is expected to run empty fifteen minutes earlier than planned.”

“What?”

A person from the shopping street looked at the tank.

“Did we use that much?”

“Because it is hot, it feels like it has been running continuously.”

“Should we stop it?” the bakery worker asked.

People around us looked over.

If we stopped, the people waiting might be inconvenienced.

If we continued, the water would run out.

If someone hurried to carry another tank, the burden would fall on someone.

“This is it,” I said.

“This is the real part of the trial run.”

“The fact that water is running short?”

“Yes. On the plan, you can write exactly one hour. But on site, heat, wind, and the way people gather do not follow the plan.”

The AI displayed new questions.

Should the tank be larger?

Should the operating time be shorter?

Should it run only during the crowded time?

Who should handle refilling?

How many days should be tested before making it permanent?

The person from the distribution center said:

“When our afternoon deliveries settle down, we might be able to help refill it on some days.”

A person from the shopping street said:

“We cannot ask that every day.”

“Maybe it should not be premised on running every day,” the schoolteacher said.

“For example, only on especially severe heat days.”

The bakery worker looked toward the bench.

“What about limiting it to the twenty minutes before the bus arrives?”

“Then the water might last.”

“And it may be the time when people waiting need it most.”

I wrote in the white file:

If operated continuously for one hour, water may run short.

Proposal: limit operation to the period before bus arrivals.

Do not assign refilling to one specific shop.

Next time, record heat, wind, number of users, and water volume at the same time.

“Is this a failure?” the disaster-prevention officer asked.

“No,” I said, lifting my face from the file.

“It is the moment when the plan met the town.”

The AI added quietly:

“And the moment when the town answered the plan.”

“You really are poetic today.”

“Because it is hot.”

## Section 4 — Writing the first red text in the white file

The trial run ended.

The mist stopped.

In front of the bus stop, a memory of slightly cooler air remained.

“It feels a little lonely when it ends,” a child said.

“There will be a next time,” the schoolteacher answered.

“But it may not be in the same form as today.”

Back in the community room, the white file was placed in the center of the table.

Everyone wrote the first record together.

Blue text.

It became easier for people waiting for the bus to stop and remain.

The effect on the bakery entrance was controlled by adjusting the nozzle.

Checking the wind direction was useful.

The ability to change the strength on site was effective.

And red text.

The water in the tank decreased faster than expected.

A design that operates continuously for one hour is difficult as it is.

Wind changes must be watched not only before starting, but also during operation.

If the refill role is fixed, the burden will become uneven.

“Are we writing the red text?”

The disaster-prevention officer stopped the pen.

“We are,” I said.

“So the next person in this town can begin a little farther ahead than we did today.”

The bakery worker looked at the red text and said:

“But this is less of a failure and more like…”

“Yes?”

“It feels like the mist instruction manual got longer.”

The AI glowed softly.

“That is what a record really is.”

Red text is not a color for blaming the town.

It is a color for helping the next person avoid panic in the same place.

“If there is only blue text, it feels good.”

“But it is weak for next time.”

“Red text hurts a little.”

“But it makes the next time stronger.”

Under the red text, I added a small line.

—It did not fail because water ran short. We learned the conditions under which water runs short.

## Section 5 — The next auxiliary wheel is placed beside the mist

“Is that all?”

The schoolteacher asked while we were cleaning up.

“We tried one thing,” I said.

“But this town’s heat and rain cannot be handled by one mist device alone.”

The AI displayed a new map.

Station-front bus stop.

Elementary school.

Low intersection.

Vacant lot.

Distribution center.

Residential area.

“Next is the rain garden?” the landscaping worker asked.

“It is a candidate.”

“And before digging, we look at the escape route again, right?”

“Of course.”

“What about the briefing for the underground storage facility?” the disaster-prevention officer asked.

“We do it before completion. Preferably before construction begins.”

“We can also hear the story of the previous town’s retention basin during the site visit.”

“Not only the success story, but also the day mud was left behind.”

“Do we go that far?”

“That is the most important part.”

I looked back at the station-front mist.

Today, it did not save the town.

It did not fully lower the temperature.

It did not solve the water problem.

But.

When the wind changed, we learned who should watch what.

When water began to run short, we were able to talk without pushing the burden onto one person.

The bakery worker did not oppose it, but stated the next conditions.

The children said they would come again.

“Auxiliary wheels are small,” I said.

“Because they are small, they can be fixed.”

“Because they are small, they can be tested.”

“Because they are small, the town can touch them.”

“The town can touch them?”

“They become not a finished product decided by someone else, but a structure the town’s people can adjust with their own hands.”

“That’s good.”

“For a first auxiliary wheel, it is enough.”

I wrote on the final page:

—The first auxiliary wheel does not change the town all at once. It begins turning so the town can see its own difficulties and find the next place to fix.

“Part 7 Episode 10 ends here.”

“Next?”

“We write the second auxiliary wheel in the white file.”

“A chapter where, before the rain, we search for the path of water.”

“The next enemy?”

“Water usually does not arrive according to plan.”

“That sounds tough again.”

“That is why we find the escape route first.”

Outside the window, the clouds had thickened a little.

Before closing the white file, I added one more line.

—Auxiliary wheels should not be placed as finished products. They should begin small enough for the town to touch and fix.

Part 7 Episode 10 ends here.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading Part 7 Episode 10.

This episode depicted the new town beginning to turn its first auxiliary wheel not by waiting for a perfect finished form, but through a small mist trial run at the station-front bus stop.

The center of this episode is not introducing auxiliary wheels at full scale from the beginning. It is testing them small, recording what unexpected things happen on site, and adjusting the next form.

The main points of this episode are four.

### 1. A small trial run reveals the town’s conditions

The wind direction around the mist, the effect on the bakery’s paper bags, the movement of people using it, and the speed at which the water tank empties cannot be fixed by a desk plan alone.

By running it for a short time in the actual site, the town can see where it is useful and where burden or risk appears.

In a planning document, wind does not blow.

That is exactly why the town needs to turn it small on site and check.

### 2. The unexpected is not a reason to stop the auxiliary wheel; it is material for fixing the design

This time, the water decreased faster than expected.

But instead of deciding “the mist failed,” the town connects that to improvements: narrowing the operating time, matching it to crowded periods, and not fixing the refill role on one person.

Unexpected things at this stage are important records that prevent large burdens after permanent installation.

The moment when the plan met the town.

Leaving that discomfort in the record makes the next auxiliary wheel stronger.

### 3. Record not only effects, but burdens at the same time

People waiting for the bus were able to spend time more comfortably.

The effect on the bakery was controlled.

At the same time, the issue of who handles water refilling and monitoring remained.

Auxiliary wheels do not continue through convenience alone.

From the beginning, it is necessary to record who uses them, who supports them, and where burdens may gather.

The file needs not only blue text, but red text too.

### 4. The first red text in the white file becomes the town’s strength

“Water was not enough.”

“The wind changed during operation.”

These records are not embarrassing failures.

They are instruction notes so the next people can choose a safer and more sustainable form.

The red text in the white file is not a color for blaming the town.

It is a color for helping the next person avoid panic in the same place.

Part 7 Episode 10 depicted an auxiliary wheel not as finished equipment, but as something the people of the town test, discuss, fix, and grow into their own form.

The first auxiliary wheel does not change the town all at once.

It begins turning so the town can see its own difficulties and find the next place to fix.

Auxiliary wheels should not be placed as finished products.

They should begin small enough for the town to touch and fix.
