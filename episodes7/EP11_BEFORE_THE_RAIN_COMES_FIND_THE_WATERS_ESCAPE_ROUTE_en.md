# Part 7 Episode 11 — Before the rain comes, find the water’s escape route

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 10, the new town ran a small trial of mist at the station-front bus stop.

The wind changed midway, and the water tank ran out faster than expected.

Even so, the town did not hide the fact that it did not work exactly as planned. It wrote it into the white file for the next improvement.

In Episode 11, the town challenges its second auxiliary wheel — a rain garden.

The stage is a low intersection where water gathers whenever strong rain falls, and a small vacant lot nearby.

The easiest thing to say is, “Let’s store the water here.”

But water does not go only where people tell it to go.

Where does it come from?

Where does it flow?

If it overflows, where does it pass?

And whose front door lies at the end of that path?

The protagonist and the AI begin searching for water’s escape route through the dry town before the rain arrives.

---

“It’s sunny today.”

I looked up at the sky from the middle of the low intersection.

The clouds were thin.

The road was dry.

The gutters were quiet.

The soil in the vacant lot was a little hard.

“This is probably the day when rain feels farthest away, and we’re talking about a rain garden.”

“There are water paths you can only see on sunny days.”

“Really?”

“Because there is no water, you can see the slope.”

“That sounds philosophical if I only listen to the wording.”

“Today, the ground is the teacher.”

At the side of the intersection, the previous members had gathered.

The bakery worker.

People from the shopping street.

The schoolteacher.

The landscaping worker.

The disaster-prevention officer.

Nearby residents.

A person who worked at the distribution center.

And in front of the vacant lot sat the white file.

Its cover now held records from the station-front mist trial.

Blue text.

Red text.

What to try next.

“The second auxiliary wheel, then,” the schoolteacher said.

“A rain garden.”

The landscaping worker looked at the vacant lot.

“But we will not dig yet.”

“Huh?”

“First, we look for where the water comes from.”

The AI drew one blue line on the screen.

Across the road.

Along the edge of the parking lot.

From roof downspouts.

Toward the gutter grates.

To the corner of the vacant lot.

“Water usually walks through town before people do.”

“All right.”

I closed the file.

“Part 7 Episode 11. Water tracking begins.”

## Section 1 — People opening umbrellas on a day without rain

“First, here.”

The disaster-prevention officer pointed to the center of the intersection.

“When heavy rain falls, water collects around this area.”

“How deep?” a nearby resident asked.

“I have seen it reach above the ankle.”

“That much?”

The bakery worker looked surprised.

“On rainy days, I’m inside the shop, so I don’t come this far to look.”

“For us, the worry is whether we can get home.”

The nearby resident’s voice was a little tense.

In a meeting room, the document would probably say this:

Interior flooding occurs near the intersection.

Localized drainage-capacity shortages are a concern.

But on site, it was different.

“This is the crosswalk children use,” the schoolteacher said.

“When school dismissal overlaps with heavy rain, parents start calling.”

The distribution-center worker looked at the edge of the road.

“When trucks pass here, they may push the water even more.”

“That happens too?”

“It does. On rainy days, more things become hard to see.”

The AI displayed a sentence.

Water problems are not only water problems.

“It isn’t just about reducing puddles,” I said.

“People walking. Cars. Shops. Homes. School routes. All of them are involved.”

The landscaping worker stopped in front of the vacant lot.

“That is why, if we are going to make a rain garden, we decide where we do not want water to go before deciding where to put water in.”

“Where we do not want water to go?”

“Water’s escape route.”

The bakery worker gave a small bitter smile.

“You’re teaching water the road?”

“Not perfectly,” the landscaping worker answered.

“But we can at least make it more likely to choose a direction that is not someone’s entrance or a school route.”

“Water does not obey orders. But it obeys lower ground.”

“AI, you’re very much on the side of the ground today.”

“The ground does not betray people. People overlook it.”

“That wording is a little scary.”

“Because this is about water.”

## Section 2 — A blue-chalk river

The landscaping worker began drawing lines on the ground with blue chalk.

The edge of the road.

The gutter grate.

The slope of the parking lot.

The corner of the vacant lot.

The walkway beside the neighboring house.

“What is this?” the schoolteacher asked.

“An imagined map for a rainy day.”

“Can you tell from lines?”

“Not everything. But if you look at the slope of the ground and the traces water leaves, you can see quite a lot.”

We followed the blue lines.

From the center of the intersection to the road edge.

From the road edge to the gutter.

If the gutter became clogged, then farther toward the lower ground.

And finally, to the corner of the vacant lot.

“So it enters here.”

I looked at the vacant lot.

“It may.”

“Then should we dig here?”

“Not yet.”

The landscaping worker extended the blue line toward the neighboring house’s walkway.

“If the vacant lot fills during heavy rain, where do you think the water will go next?”

The nearby resident frowned.

“This way?”

“Yes. If we dig as it is, overflow from the vacant lot may head toward this walkway.”

“That would be a problem.”

“That is why we make an escape route.”

“Where?”

The landscaping worker pointed to the opposite side of the road.

There was an unused planting strip.

“We lower this a little, so if water overflows, it flows along the road edge. We separate it from the walking path with edge height.”

The disaster-prevention officer took notes.

“Do not make the vacant lot hold everything alone.”

“Exactly.”

I remembered the rain garden in the previous town.

The day water overflowed sideways.

The day the garden edge became muddy.

The day people said, “Rain gardens do not fit this town.”

“The previous town focused too much on storing water,” I said.

“This town begins from what happens after it overflows.”

The AI wrote in the white file:

—Think separately about the place that receives water and the place that lets water escape.

“The first red note is already useful.”

“The previous town’s failure changed this town’s blue line.”

“The map is working.”

“It is.”

## Section 3 — What the owner of the vacant lot said

“But.”

A low voice came from behind.

Someone standing at the back of the vacant lot was looking at us.

“Are you planning to turn that vacant lot into a rain garden without permission?”

The air stopped.

The bakery worker took a small breath.

The disaster-prevention officer held the documents closer.

The schoolteacher moved the children a little farther back.

“I’m sorry,” I said.

“We heard that there might be a possibility of borrowing the vacant lot, so we came to look at the site.”

“A possibility, huh.”

The person was the owner of the vacant lot.

“A rain garden is a place where you store water, right?”

“Yes. But it is not only about storing—”

“Mosquitoes appear. Grass grows. Water remains after rain. Neighbors complain. And in the end, who cleans it up?”

The words came one after another.

But all of them were words we had heard in the previous town.

Grass.

Maintenance.

Responsibility.

Anxiety.

Burden.

I did not answer immediately.

The AI quietly displayed:

Opposition is a condition necessary for design.

“Those concerns are reasonable,” I said.

“That is why we did not come today to decide, ‘Let’s make this a rain garden.’”

The owner raised an eyebrow slightly.

“Then why did you come?”

“To see where the water comes from and where it goes.”

I pointed to the blue chalk line.

“If this vacant lot is used, we are thinking about whether we can make a form that does not concentrate water, grass, maintenance, and responsibility only here.”

“Can you?”

“We do not know yet.”

I answered honestly.

“But if we would have to rely on goodwill without being able to do that, then I think we should not do it.”

The owner fell silent for a moment.

“In the previous town, some things did not work well.”

I opened the file.

“Water overflowed sideways. No one had decided who would cut the grass. Someone almost ended up carrying the trouble alone.”

“And?”

“They adjusted it. Not completely, but they began deciding in advance who would maintain it, whom to contact, and what to do when it overflowed.”

“And in this town?”

“We decide from the beginning.”

The landscaping worker continued.

“We separate the places where grass remains and where it is cut. We also consider soil and depth so water is less likely to stay. And we create an escape route for heavy rain.”

The disaster-prevention officer added:

“If we run a trial, the town’s disaster-prevention budget frame and management responsibilities will be made clear. We will not leave it all to the owner.”

The owner looked at the blue line.

“This line leads to our walkway, doesn’t it?”

“It does.”

“Then before you store water here, do something about that.”

I nodded.

“Yes. That comes first.”

“Agreement is not obtaining permission. It is deciding together in what order to handle the things that trouble people.”

“You’re saying good things today.”

“Today is the day to look at the order of water.”

The owner kept their arms folded and compared the vacant lot with the walkway.

“If you are only looking, then look.”

“Thank you.”

“But do not dig without permission.”

“We will not.”

“And if you write things down, write that too.”

I wrote in the white file:

—Do not proceed without the owner’s consent.
—Before the path of water, decide the path of responsibility and maintenance.

## Section 4 — A sudden hose, and sudden confusion

“Shall we actually let a little water flow?” the landscaping worker said.

“Huh?”

The disaster-prevention officer turned around.

“It will not be like heavy rain, but if we run a little water with a hose, we can confirm where the water heads.”

“Are we allowed to do that?”

“If the owner agrees.”

Everyone looked at the owner of the vacant lot.

With arms still folded, the owner sighed.

“Only a little.”

“Thank you.”

The hose was connected.

Water flowed along the road edge.

At first, it was a thin line.

“Oh.”

The children raised their voices.

The water approached the blue chalk line.

It headed toward the gutter grate.

And then—

“Huh?”

The water turned toward the parking-lot corner earlier than expected.

“Not that way!”

The landscaping worker ran over.

“The ground has sunk a little here!”

The water did not enter the vacant lot. It made a small puddle at the edge of the parking lot.

The distribution-center worker said:

“Trucks often turn there, so the ground has been worn down.”

“The chalk line missed.”

I said that.

“The line was not wrong. New information about the ground increased.”

“That is a convenient rephrasing.”

“No, but it really is true,” the landscaping worker said, looking at the parking-lot edge.

“If we had dug without seeing this, water might not have entered the rain garden at all.”

The disaster-prevention officer immediately opened the file.

“Do we write this in red?”

“We write it.”

I answered.

Not only the road, but also parking-lot ruts and ground depressions become water paths.

Before design, confirm flow with a small amount of water.

If the map’s line and the site’s water differ, prioritize the site.

The owner smiled a little.

“So you failed with water before making the rain garden.”

“Yes.”

“But it was good that you failed before digging.”

“That is the point,” I said.

“A small trial exists so we can be wrong before the mistake becomes large.”

The AI displayed a short line:

A trial run is an auxiliary wheel for bringing failure forward.

“Bringing failure forward sounds strange.”

“But it is better than failing after digging.”

“That is absolutely true.”

The hose water had stopped.

But new lines had been added inside the white file.

The vacant lot can be a candidate place to receive water.

The planting strip can be a candidate route to guide overflow safely.

The owner’s concerns should be included as design conditions from the beginning.

Parking-lot ruts and depressions had been overlooked.

Do not proceed before deciding grass, mosquitoes, maintenance, contact points, and costs.

“Right.”

“But we found something before digging.”

“The path of water?”

“That too.”

I looked toward the owner.

“Who would be troubled.”

The bakery worker said:

The owner looked slightly displeased.

“Is that supposed to be praise?”

“It is.”

“We will.”

The disaster-prevention officer closed the file.

“Next, let’s prepare two design options.”

“Two?”

“One option uses the vacant lot more heavily. The other divides the role among the vacant lot, the planting strip, and the road edge.”

The landscaping worker nodded.

“The second one can disperse both water and burden.”

“That feels like a town auxiliary wheel.”

“Do not make one place carry everything.”

“Water or people.”

“Exactly.”

The rain garden had not yet been dug.

But that did not mean it had not begun.

We had seen the path of water.

We had heard people’s anxiety.

We had let a small amount of water flow.

We had written red text.

“So a rain garden begins before digging a hole.”

“Yes.”

## Section 6 — Before the sound of rain

On the way home.

The sky was darker than it had been in the morning.

It was not raining yet.

But the smell of the wind had changed a little.

“The next rain will be the real test,” I said.

“The next rain will not ask our schedule.”

“That’s scary.”

“That is why we looked today.”

“But unexpected things will appear again.”

“They will.”

“Then why did we do this today?”

The AI opened the rain-garden page of the white file.

Blue lines and red text were lined up there.

Water flows more complexly than imagined.

People’s anxiety should be heard before construction, not after.

Do not make one vacant lot carry the whole town’s problem.

Run a small flow and be wrong early.

“It was not to eliminate the unexpected.”

“Then?”

“It was so that when the unexpected appears, no one has to be troubled alone.”

I fell silent for a moment.

The station-front mist.

The water tank that ran short.

The low intersection.

The blue-chalk river.

The voice of the vacant-lot owner.

“I thought an auxiliary wheel meant equipment.”

“Is it different?”

“It is equipment too. But not only that.”

I wrote the final line.

—A rain garden does not begin by digging a hole. It begins by deciding where water will escape when rain comes, and whose trouble will be received first.

Part 7 Episode 11 ends here.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading Part 7 Episode 11.

In this episode, before the new town began considering a rain garden as its second auxiliary wheel, it checked where water comes from, where it flows, and where it should escape if it overflows.

A rain garden is not simply a facility where the ground is dug and rainwater is stored.

It may slow water down and reduce the amount that flows into gutters and roads all at once. But if designed poorly, it may shift water, maintenance, and burden to another place.

The main points of this episode are four.

### 1. Think not only about where to store water, but where overflow escapes

Even if water is led into a vacant lot, if it exceeds capacity during heavy rain and flows into a neighboring walkway or school route, it creates a new trouble.

For a rain garden, the question is not only “how much can it store?” but also “where can the water that does not fit be guided safely?”

Think separately about the place that receives water and the place that lets water escape.

That was the first check in this episode.

### 2. Water paths can be checked even on sunny days

Looking at ground slope, gutters, downspouts, parking-lot ruts, and soil depressions allows a town to predict where rainwater is likely to gather.

In addition, running a small amount of water with a hose can reveal flow patterns and low places that drawings do not show.

The fact that the blue chalk line did not match the actual flow was not a failure.

It was a result: the town gained more information about the ground before digging.

### 3. Opposition becomes design conditions for the rain garden

The vacant-lot owner worried about grass, mosquitoes, remaining water, complaints from neighbors, maintenance, and responsibility.

All of those are real issues.

Rather than setting them aside as the opinion of someone who does not want to cooperate, the town must treat them as conditions for deciding management methods, costs, contact points, where to cut grass, and where water should escape.

Opposition does not always mean a voice that stops a rain garden.

It can also become a condition for reshaping the rain garden into something that can continue.

### 4. Small trials prevent large failures early

In reality, parking-lot ruts and ground depressions had changed the water flow.

Finding that early with hose water, rather than after completing the rain garden, makes it possible to adjust the next design more safely.

Part 7 Episode 11 was not about eliminating all unexpected events.

It was about preparing a structure so that, when unexpected events happen, the burden does not concentrate on one specific person.

A rain garden does not begin by digging a hole.

It begins by deciding where water will escape when rain comes, and whose trouble will be received first.

Letting a small flow run and being wrong early is preparation so that, on a day of heavy rain, one person is not left to struggle alone.

These are the core ideas of Part 7 Episode 11.

In the next episode, Part 7 Episode 12, strong rain finally comes to the town.

How much of the blue-chalk river was correct in the real rain?

And what first question will the unfinished rain garden leave for the town?
