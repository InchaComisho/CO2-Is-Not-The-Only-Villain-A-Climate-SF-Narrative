# Part 7 Episode 12 — Real rain comes to test the blue chalk lines

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 11, before the new town built a rain garden, it walked the low intersection and the small vacant lot.

A rain garden is not simply a facility where you dig a hole and store water.

Where does the water come from?

Where does it flow?

If it overflows, whose walkway or front door will it head toward?

Who will carry the grass, mosquitoes, maintenance, costs, and contact points?

The blue chalk lines drawn on a clear day, and the small amount of water poured from a hose.

They were the town’s first trial run for preparing for real rain.

And now, Episode 12.

The rain garden is still not finished.

The vacant lot has only been slightly shaped.

Along the edge of the road, there is a small temporary line of soil to guide water toward the planting strip.

At the intersection, signs show where people can pass.

Then, strong rain comes to the town.

Real rain does not wait for planning documents, blue chalk, or the town’s convenience.

For the first time in the rain, the protagonist and the AI will see whether the auxiliary wheel can truly support the town.

---

“It’s going to rain.”

The AI said that in the morning.

Outside the window, it was still only dark.

No rain was falling yet.

But the sky was low.

“As forecast?”

“The forecast indicates the possibility of strong rain.”

“Possibility, huh.”

“Rain always begins as a possibility.”

I opened the white file.

The station-front mist page.

The low-intersection page.

The blue chalk river.

The ruts in the parking lot.

The voice of the vacant-lot owner.

And the page that had just been added yesterday.

Rain garden — before temporary trial.

Slightly shape part of the vacant lot.

Create a small temporary soil line along the road edge so water can flow toward the planting strip.

Place a low edge so water does not head toward the neighboring walkway.

During heavy rain, station people at the intersection.

Contact the vacant-lot owner before starting.

“It isn’t finished yet.”

I said that.

“That is why today’s rain is not a completion inspection.”

“Then what is it?”

The AI displayed a short phrase on the screen.

Town first test.

“That sounds scary.”

“A test is not an answer check. It is time to find what should be fixed next.”

Immediately after that:

Plink.

One drop hit the window.

“It’s here.”

## Section 1 — For the first ten minutes, people can still laugh

At ten in the morning, people had already gathered at the low intersection.

The disaster-prevention officer.

The landscaper.

The owner of the vacant lot.

The bakery worker.

The schoolteacher.

The person who worked at the distribution center.

The shopping-street member.

And, a little farther away, children holding umbrellas.

“Did school end early today?”

When I asked, the teacher shook her head.

“Today is Saturday.”

“Oh. Right.”

“Protagonists tend to forget what day of the week it is.”

“Don’t say that now.”

The rain was still ordinary rain.

Small dots increased on the road.

Water entered the gutters.

Drops fell from roofs.

“The blue lines are going to disappear.”

The bakery worker said that.

The chalk that still remained on the ground was already beginning to blur.

“The lines may disappear, but the water paths do not.”

The landscaper answered.

“What we watch today is not the ground, but the water.”

In front of the vacant lot, water was gradually entering the shallow hollow that had been temporarily shaped.

“It’s going in.”

A nearby resident said.

“It’s going in more properly than I expected.”

The water spread across the shallow hollow.

It did not overflow immediately.

It was not heading toward the neighboring walkway yet.

The vacant-lot owner stood with his arms crossed and said:

“For now.”

“That’s right.”

I answered.

“For now.”

The AI displayed numbers on the screen.

Rain intensity.

Water depth.

Gutter flow.

Inflow into the vacant lot.

“Can you see the numbers?”

The disaster-prevention officer asked.

“I can. But we do not judge by numbers alone.”

“That again?”

“Today, we also watch where people can pass.”

The teacher responded at once.

“The crosswalk.”

“Yes. It is where children pass.”

“Then we should watch that too.”

For the first ten minutes, people could still laugh.

The sound of the rain was not loud enough to interfere with conversation.

The blue lines were fading, but the town was still watching where those lines continued.

## Section 2 — The rain gets serious earlier than planned

At 10:20 a.m., the sound of the rain changed.

The sound striking roofs.

The sound hitting car roofs.

The sound falling on umbrellas.

The conversation that had been easy to hear became a little harder to catch.

“It got stronger.”

The distribution-center worker said.

“Earlier than planned.”

The disaster-prevention officer looked at his smartphone.

“The peak is not supposed to have arrived yet.”

“Rain does not read schedules.”

“I know.”

The water running along the road edge thickened.

One fallen leaf stuck to the gutter grate.

Then another.

The water was slightly dammed.

“The grate!”

I shouted.

The landscaper ran over.

The distribution-center worker moved too.

“Don’t touch it. Your footing is slippery!”

The disaster-prevention officer stopped them.

“Do we have a long stick?”

“We do!”

A shopping-street member pulled a stick out of the temporary tool box.

They shifted the leaves slightly.

The water was pulled in with a gulp.

“It’s flowing.”

Someone said.

But while that was happening, water was increasing somewhere else.

The rut in the parking lot.

The place where hose water had unexpectedly turned last time.

“That way!”

Water ran along a path that had not been on the blue chalk line.

The corner of the parking lot.

The entrance of the distribution center.

And then toward the crosswalk at the low intersection.

“Children, this way!”

The teacher raised her voice.

The children stepped back.

“We can’t cross from the road edge!”

“Can we send it toward the planting strip?”

The landscaper shouted.

“It’s about to cross the temporary soil line!”

I looked at the shallow hollow of the rain garden.

Water was entering it.

But it was also coming from another direction beyond the assumption.

“The vacant lot alone is not enough.”

“Not enough.”

“Then what do we do?”

The AI instantly displayed a map.

The parking-lot corner.

The planting strip.

The temporary soil line.

The crosswalk.

The vacant lot.

“Do not stop the water. Move it away from the crosswalk.”

“Cut the edge of the planting strip a little!”

The landscaper nodded.

“Lower it there!”

The vacant-lot owner grabbed a shovel.

“I’ll help!”

“Be careful. It’s slippery!”

A little soil was removed.

The water hesitated, almost wavering.

And then:

Whoosh.

It began to flow toward the planting strip.

“It went!”

“It moved away from the crosswalk!”

The teacher moved the children farther to a safer place.

In the rain, someone breathed out.

But the AI’s screen was still red.

“Vacant-lot water level rising.”

## Section 3 — The vacant lot cannot swallow the whole town

The temporary hollow of the rain garden was receiving water.

From roofs.

From the road.

From the planting strip.

And from the unexpected parking-lot corner too.

The water surface spread.

“I didn’t think this much would come in.”

The vacant-lot owner said.

“Is it still all right?”

A nearby resident asked.

The landscaper did not answer right away.

The edge of the hollow.

The neighboring walkway.

The low temporary edge.

The small outlet for releasing water.

After looking at all of them, she said:

“It has not crossed the edge yet.”

“Yet?”

“But if the rain gets stronger, more water will be released through the outlet toward the planting strip.”

“Then it won’t go to the walkway?”

“With the current shape, it is less likely.”

“Not absolutely?”

“I will not say absolutely.”

In the sound of rain, those words were heavy.

“But we learned that this cannot end with ‘put everything into the vacant lot.’”

I said.

“One hole cannot swallow all the rain of a town.”

The AI continued.

“An auxiliary wheel is not giant magic.”

“Don’t suddenly get so realistic.”

“Reality is more useful for the next design.”

“But because the vacant lot exists, the water at the intersection was delayed a little.”

The disaster-prevention officer said while looking at the records.

“In the first ten minutes, the time before water entered the crosswalk changed.”

“By how much?”

“Compared with the assumption before the temporary shaping, we gained at least a few minutes.”

The teacher said:

“Even a few minutes is big when you need to move children.”

The bakery worker also looked through the rain.

“The water coming toward the shop is later than usual.”

“Even if it cannot make it zero, it creates time.”

I said.

“That is also an auxiliary wheel.”

The vacant lot cannot swallow the whole town.

But it created a few minutes for the town to choose its next action.

Today, those minutes became the movement of children and the decision to move water away from the crosswalk.

## Section 4 — The voice most in trouble is heard in the rain

Before noon, the rain weakened slightly.

Water was still flowing on the road.

The water in the vacant lot would not drain immediately either.

But the crosswalk became passable again.

Just when everyone could move a little, the vacant-lot owner said:

“Am I supposed to watch this even after the rain stops?”

No one answered immediately.

“If water remains, mosquitoes might appear. The grass will grow. Trash might flow in too.”

It was the same voice as last time.

But today, it was a voice after actually seeing the water in the rain.

“If only this place receives the town’s water, that is a problem.”

“That is exactly right.”

The disaster-prevention officer said.

“Today’s record means we can no longer postpone the management discussion.”

“Don’t just write it down and leave it there.”

“We won’t.”

The disaster-prevention officer said it clearly.

“We will write, on the same page as the design, who checks after the water recedes, who collects trash if there is any, how grass is managed, and who to contact after heavy rain.”

The distribution-center worker said:

“We’re nearby too. We can help check the walkway after the water drains.”

The shopping-street member continued:

“If you need a place to keep trash bags or tools, you can use the edge of our storage room.”

The bakery worker also said:

“But you can’t run this on someone’s goodwill every time.”

“Yes.”

I opened the file, blurred slightly by the rain.

“Before relying on goodwill, we decide roles and costs.”

The AI displayed:

Rain-garden auxiliary wheel.

Water outlet.

Management outlet.

Responsibility outlet.

“That’s a lot of outlets.”

“They may be more important than entrances.”

The landscaper said.

“Because water, and trouble too, overflow when they have no exit.”

“That’s today’s quote.”

“Words from the site are strong.”

A rain garden is a place that receives water.

But it is not enough to receive only water.

Trash comes too.

Mud remains.

Grass grows.

Anxiety remains.

That is why outlets are needed.

An outlet for water.

An outlet for management.

An outlet for responsibility.

Outlets so that everything does not flow only into goodwill.

## Section 5 — The blue lines disappeared. But the town’s lines increased

The rain stopped a little after two in the afternoon.

The clouds were still low.

Puddles remained on the road.

In the shallow hollow of the vacant lot, muddy water still sat.

The blue chalk lines were no longer anywhere to be seen.

“They’re all gone.”

I said.

“The lines disappeared. The record remains.”

When we returned to the community room, the white file was slightly damp.

But no one tried to close it.

Blue text.

The shallow hollow of the vacant lot received water from the road.

It slightly delayed the time before water reached the crosswalk.

When an escape route toward the planting strip was made, it became easier to move water away from places where people pass.

It was useful to have people and tools ready to check the gutter grate.

Because the school and the site stayed in contact, the children could be moved earlier to a safer place.

Red text.

The rain intensified earlier than expected.

Fallen leaves blocked the gutter grate.

From the parking-lot ruts, unexpected water headed toward the crosswalk.

The vacant lot alone cannot receive all the water.

Management, cleanup, contact points, and costs after the water recedes were still undecided.

The temporary soil line helped, but it needs to be redesigned into a safer form.

“There’s a lot of red text.”

The teacher said.

“There is.”

I answered.

“But compared with before today’s rain, what does the town know now?”

The teacher thought for a moment.

“Where the water comes.”

“Only that?”

“Who can move. Who is troubled. Who to contact.”

The vacant-lot owner looked into the file.

“Don’t write my name as the management person without asking.”

“We won’t.”

“But call me for the next discussion.”

“We will.”

“From the beginning.”

“From the beginning.”

The AI glowed faintly.

“That is a fairly large agreement.”

“It really is.”

The rain erased the blue chalk.

But it left other lines inside the town.

The contact line between the school and the site.

The line of people who check the gutter.

The line of people who gather after the water recedes.

And the line that says the vacant-lot owner must be called from the beginning.

## Section 6 — Rain tests the auxiliary wheel and connects the town a little

Evening.

The water at the intersection had almost drained.

A little water still remained in the vacant lot.

But the neighboring walkway was beginning to dry.

“Was today’s rain garden a success?”

I asked the AI at the empty intersection.

“In what sense?”

“Don’t ask that.”

“Then let’s separate it.”

The AI displayed three words.

Water.

People.

Next.

“Water?”

“It received part of the water. It delayed the time before water reached the crosswalk. But it could not receive everything.”

“People?”

“The children could be moved. There were people checking the gutter. The owner’s anxiety entered the center of the design.”

“Next?”

“The parts to fix became concrete.”

“So it was neither success nor failure?”

“Today’s rain added pages to the town’s instruction manual.”

I laughed a little.

“An auxiliary wheel’s manual grows thicker each time it is used.”

“If it gets too thick, that’s a problem.”

“Then make it so people can open the troubled part first.”

“That is the failure map.”

“Yes.”

I wrote the final line.

“Part 7 Episode 12 ends here.”

“Next?”

“The true shape of an auxiliary wheel becomes visible more after the rain than during it.”

Outside the window, evening light reflected in the puddles.

Part 7 Episode 12 ends here.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading Part 7 Episode 12.

This episode depicted a still-unfinished rain garden and a temporary escape route for water receiving a real strong rain.

What matters in this episode is that the rain garden is not an “all-purpose facility” that solves the town’s rainwater problem all at once.

The shallow hollow in the vacant lot received part of the water and slightly delayed the time before water reached the crosswalk.

However, conditions that could not be seen from the plan alone also appeared: fallen leaves at the gutter, ruts in the parking lot, and the rain intensifying earlier than expected.

The main points of this episode are four.

### 1. Real rain reveals conditions outside the plan

The blue chalk lines drawn on a clear day were a starting point for thinking about water paths.

In actual rain, clogged gutter grates caused by fallen leaves, parking-lot ruts, and the way rain intensified were added to the situation, and water flowed in places different from the assumptions.

That is why the process must not end with maps and trial runs. The record of real rain has to be reflected in the next design.

### 2. Even if an auxiliary wheel cannot reduce a problem to zero, it can create time and choices

The temporary hollow and planting strip could not receive all the water.

Even so, they delayed the time before water reached the crosswalk, created time to move children to a safer place, and moved water slightly away from places where people pass.

The value of an auxiliary wheel is not only in completely preventing something.

It is also in slowing the speed at which danger approaches and creating room to choose the next action.

### 3. Not only water outlets, but management and responsibility outlets are needed

After the rain stopped, water, trash, and grass-management issues remained in the vacant lot.

If monitoring and cleanup are left only to the vacant-lot owner, the auxiliary wheel will not continue.

Just as an overflow route for water is needed, it is also necessary to decide in advance who will handle management, costs, contact points, and restoration work.

An outlet for water.

An outlet for management.

An outlet for responsibility.

Without considering all three, the rain garden becomes one person’s burden.

### 4. Do not force a binary of success or failure. Think of it as adding pages to the town’s manual

This rain was neither a complete success nor an overall failure.

The town learned concretely what delayed the water, where the water flowed unexpectedly, who could move, and what was missing.

Part 7 Episode 12 depicted how, through actual rain, an auxiliary wheel becomes a town’s strength not as equipment alone, but through repeated observation, records, communication, and role-sharing.

Real rain erased the blue chalk lines.

But the town began drawing new lines: lines of water, lines of people, and lines for calling help.

This is the central sentence of Part 7 Episode 12.

In the next episode, Part 7 Episode 13, the town will face mud, trash, remaining water, and tired people after the rain stops.

Whether the auxiliary wheel truly continues will be decided not only by whether it worked during the rain, but by who cleans up afterward and whose voice is included in the next design.
