# Part 7 Episode 14 — Do not draw only water on the blueprint; draw the distant heat of the sky too

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 13, we looked at the mud, trash, clogged gutters, and the question of “who cleans it up” after strong rain.

A rain garden does not end once you dig a hole to receive water.

Who goes to check the site after the water recedes?

Whose problem is the trash that drifts in?

Where does the small budget for maintenance, communication, cleanup, and records come from?

The town wrote one important line into the white file.

—Do not draw only water on the blueprint. Draw the people who clean up too.

In Episode 14, that blueprint finally moves from “temporary lines” toward “real design.”

But in the middle of the meeting, the AI opens another map.

Not just the town’s intersection, but a much larger map.

A world that keeps warming.

A sea that has turned red.

An El Niño continuing in the equatorial Pacific.

And typhoons forming south of Japan, one after another, and moving closer at short intervals.

Japan as a whole was not heavily struck every time.

But from the southwestern islands to the area around Kyushu, there were impacts on daily life: strong winds, heavy rain, high waves, evacuations, power outages, and transportation disruptions.

It was a summer when it could not simply be said, “They did not make landfall, so everything was fine.”

How does the town’s small rain garden connect to the distant disturbance in the sea?

The protagonist and the AI try to write “the world’s heat” at the edge of the blueprint.

“Today is the day we decide the blueprint.”

The disaster-prevention officer said that.

A large sheet of paper was spread across the table in the community hall.

The low intersection.

The small vacant lot.

The road edge.

The planting strip.

The gutter.

The parking lot.

The crosswalk.

The neighboring house’s walkway.

The blue chalk lines from the previous sessions were gone now.

In their place, thick blue lines had been drawn.

Water flowing in from the road.

Water entering the shallow hollow in the vacant lot.

Water escaping toward the planting strip after the hollow fills.

Water that should be kept away from the crosswalk and the neighboring walkway.

“It finally looks like a blueprint.”

I said.

“The water lines have increased.”

“So have the human lines.”

In the margins of the drawing, words were written in a color different from the water.

Post-heavy-rain checks.

The disaster-prevention officer sends the notice.

Site checks.

Shared among nearby residents, the shopping street, and the vacant-lot owner.

Trash and mud collection.

Coordinate with the town’s cleaning section.

Tool storage.

A corner of the shopping-street warehouse.

Grass management.

Set up a maintenance-cost frame with the landscaping side.

Do not concentrate the burden only on the vacant-lot owner.

The vacant-lot owner stood with arms crossed, looking at the blueprint.

“At least it’s written better than before.”

“Before, it was only about water.”

The disaster-prevention officer answered.

“But now it also says who comes after the water arrives.”

“Don’t write my name yet.”

“We understand.”

“Don’t make me the person in charge before the roles are decided.”

“We understand that too.”

The bakery worker gave a small laugh.

“You’ve become quite familiar with blueprints.”

“If I don’t, the water and trash will come back here again.”

I nodded.

“That is exactly right.”

“Opposing opinions belong inside the blueprint, not outside it.”

“That is basic Part 7.”

At that moment, the AI’s screen changed from the usual rain-garden map.

The town map.

The prefecture map.

The map of Japan.

And then a wide map of the Pacific.

“That got wide very suddenly.”

“We will stretch the edge of the blueprint a little farther.”

On the screen, changes in sea-surface temperature near the equator were displayed.

“El Niño?”

“Yes.”

## Section 1 — The world’s red sea is outside the town blueprint

“This is the warming and El Niño topic we discussed before, right?”

The schoolteacher looked at the AI’s screen.

“The Japan Meteorological Agency says El Niño conditions appear to have continued since spring 2026, and are expected to continue toward winter.”

“It is a phenomenon where the sea becomes warmer, right?”

“Roughly speaking, yes.”

I answered.

“But when the sea temperature changes, the paths of rain and wind can also become easier to shift. You cannot decide when, where, and what kind of impact Japan will receive from one cause alone, but it can affect where typhoons form and how they move.”

The AI drew a line across the Pacific map.

“During El Niño, the region where typhoons form tends to shift southeast of normal. Sometimes, by traveling longer over warm ocean before approaching Japan, they can more easily maintain strong intensity.”

“What happens in the distant ocean relates to our rain here.”

The bakery worker said.

“But it does not directly decide, ‘this town will get exactly this many millimeters of rain.’”

“No, it does not.”

I said.

“This is not a story where El Niño means this town will inevitably flood.”

“Climate background is not for choosing one culprit for each individual rain event.”

“Then why look at it?”

The AI displayed the rain-garden blueprint again.

“So the town does not assume only the same rain as before.”

As global temperature rises, the atmosphere can hold more water vapor. In general, the amount of water vapor the atmosphere can hold increases as temperature rises by one degree, and when conditions align, the materials for heavy rain can increase. That does not mean the rain in front of us can be explained by one cause alone. But it becomes risky to think about the town’s water only on the old assumptions.

“So,”

the schoolteacher said,

“it may be dangerous to think only, ‘this used to be enough.’”

“Yes.”

I pointed to the vacant lot on the blueprint.

“That is why the rain garden is not ‘this will receive all rain.’ It slows the force of the water a little, creates an escape route, and gives people time to move.”

“It is not a device that stops the world’s heat. But it can become an auxiliary wheel that gives the town a little margin against sudden rain.”

## Section 2 — A summer when typhoons appeared one after another

“But this year,”

the distribution-center worker said,

“it doesn’t feel like every typhoon hit mainland Japan that hard.”

“Depending on the region, I think it may feel that way.”

I looked at the Pacific map.

“But south and southwest of Japan, typhoons formed one after another and affected Okinawa, Amami, and areas around Kyushu.”

It was reported that ten typhoons formed in August 2026, and that ten in August alone was the highest number in sixty years, since 1966.

“So it was not just my imagination that multiple typhoons appeared over the ocean in short intervals.”

The bakery worker said.

“At least, there were times when typhoons formed one after another at short intervals and multiple vortices appeared over the ocean.”

“However, we must not state that ‘they form two at a time’ is itself a fixed feature of one climate phenomenon.”

“That is important.”

The AI continued.

“The number of typhoons in a month, their formation area, tracks, intensity, and approaching regions are not decided by El Niño alone. Sea-surface temperature, the Pacific High, the westerlies, monsoon winds, and multiple other conditions overlap.”

“So even in the story, we do not say, ‘El Niño is the cause of everything.’”

“But we also do not ignore the changes in the distant ocean.”

The disaster-prevention officer said.

“It means we hold the assumptions of the blueprint a little wider.”

“Exactly.”

The AI displayed the August typhoon record.

In early August, Typhoon Dolphin affected the Okinawa area, with evacuations, flight and transport cancellations, and warnings for strong winds, high waves, and heavy rain.

In late August, Typhoon Saudel affected Okinawa and Amami, and reports described more than 30,000 households without power in the Amami region of Kagoshima Prefecture, over 230 millimeters of rain in 24 hours, and warnings for landslide disasters.

“It does not mean all of Kyushu was directly hit every time.”

I said.

“But on the southwest side of Kyushu, and in the island areas of Kagoshima Prefecture and Amami, daily life was actually affected.”

“‘It did not make landfall’ and ‘there was no impact’ are not the same thing.”

“We can write that into the blueprint too.”

The bakery worker said.

“It is not that typhoons will not come. It is that we look at what happens when they do.”

## Section 3 — The vacant-lot owner gets angry at the world map

“Wait a minute.”

The vacant-lot owner pointed at the Pacific map.

“The world is hot. The ocean is warm. El Niño. Lots of typhoons.”

After a short pause, he said:

“And then the story becomes: store the water on my vacant lot?”

The air stopped.

“No.”

I answered immediately.

“No?”

“This is not a story about pushing the world’s problem onto one vacant lot in this town.”

“Rather, we write roles and limits onto the blueprint to avoid that kind of pushing.”

I pointed to the vacant lot on the drawing.

“This rain garden is not a place that takes on all of the town’s rain.”

“Then what is it?”

“It is a place that slows how fast water gathers at the intersection. It is a place that helps keep water away from the crosswalk and the house walkway.”

“One hole does not fix the world.”

“It does not.”

“Then what is the point of showing the world map?”

I thought for a moment.

“So we do not say, ‘bigger rains may come, so please receive them all on your land.’”

The owner narrowed his eyes.

“What do you mean?”

“The town’s rain problem cannot be solved by one vacant lot. We need road edges, gutters, planting strips, roof runoff, school evacuation routes, communication, cleanup, budgets, and whole-town design.”

The disaster-prevention officer continued.

“The vacant-lot rain garden is one part of that. If rain exceeds its limits, other measures are needed too: evacuation, traffic decisions, information, and wider support.”

“You won’t make my land the ‘last basin’?”

“We will not.”

“Trash too?”

“If the town’s water carries trash, then we treat it as the town’s issue.”

“Cleanup too?”

“We will not make it only your role.”

The owner looked down at the blueprint.

“Then write it on the drawing.”

“We will.”

I took the pen.

—The rain garden in the vacant lot does not replace the town’s overall drainage, evacuation, and management.
—Rain beyond its limits must be combined with evacuation, traffic decisions, information sharing, and wider support.
—Do not concentrate the burden of water, trash, and management only on the vacant-lot owner.

“With this, the world story does not only increase the town’s burden.”

The schoolteacher said.

“It becomes a story about dividing the town’s burden.”

“Do not translate the big climate story into one person’s endurance.”

## Section 4 — Write typhoon time at the edge of the blueprint

“What happens to this blueprint if a typhoon comes?”

the schoolteacher asked.

“The rain garden works.”

the landscaping worker answered.

“But we do not think it will receive all of a typhoon’s heavy rain.”

“Then what do we do?”

The disaster-prevention officer spread out another sheet of paper.

It did not show the facility layout.

It showed a timeline.

Before the typhoon.

Check gutters and grates.

Check the vacant-lot outlet and the planting strip.

Prepare trash bags, long poles, and contact lists.

Share cautions with the school, shopping street, and nearby residents.

Before rain becomes strong.

Check the low intersection’s traffic conditions.

Watch the crosswalk risk, and guide detours if necessary.

Check the vacant lot water level and the neighboring walkway.

Do not force anyone to clear trash from the gutter in dangerous places.

After the rain.

After the water recedes, check mud and trash.

Do not leave cleanup only to the owner.

Write what happened into the white file.

Reflect it in the next design.

“That is not a facility blueprint.”

the bakery worker said.

“It is a time blueprint.”

“Yes.”

I said.

“It is not ‘we have a rain garden, so we are safe.’ It is deciding how a town that has a rain garden moves before and after rain.”

The AI added:

“A typhoon approaching is also a day when the rain garden may exceed its capacity.”

“So we do not make the auxiliary wheel do the impossible.”

“Make the auxiliary wheel do the impossible?”

“We do not treat the rain garden as all-powerful and delay evacuation or communication.”

“Do not turn the success of equipment into the town’s complacency.”

“That should go into the blueprint too.”

the disaster-prevention officer said.

“Yes.”

I nodded.

“The blueprint needs not only water lines, but time lines too.”

## Section 5 — The world’s heat and the town’s small choices

The day tilted slightly toward evening.

The Pacific map was still projected on the wall.

The sea near the equator.

Distant typhoon vortices.

The Japanese archipelago.

And this town’s small intersection.

“Global warming is too large.”

I said.

“Much larger than the town’s rain garden.”

“It is large.”

“El Niño and typhoons cannot be stopped by this town.”

“They cannot.”

“Then what the town can do is small.”

The AI paused briefly.

“Small. But not zero.”

At the station front, mist is run briefly on dangerous heat days.

At the low intersection, water is kept from gathering all at once.

At the vacant lot, some water is received and moved away from the crosswalk.

At the planting strip, an escape route is made for overflowing water.

At the school, routes to take on heavy-rain days are considered.

At the shopping street, tools and contact points are placed.

After rain, the town does not push the mud onto one person.

“That is mitigation and adaptation.”

the schoolteacher said.

“Efforts to reduce the damage from heat and heavy rain are adaptation to impacts that are already happening. Meanwhile, reducing greenhouse gases globally and limiting further warming is mitigation.”

“The town’s rain garden alone cannot stop warming.”

“It cannot.”

“But there is still meaning in doing it.”

“There is. It can make the town a little safer, and it also means the town does not stop at ‘this is a global problem, so we can do nothing.’”

The AI showed two lines.

The distant line.

Reduce emissions and restrain warming.

The near line.

Prepare for the impacts of heat, heavy rain, and typhoons.

“One line alone is not enough.”

“The distant line alone cannot protect today’s intersection.”

“The near line alone means the world’s heat keeps increasing.”

I copied those two lines into the white file.

## Section 6 — Write the town’s limits and promises into the blueprint

At the end, new lines were added below the drawing.

—This rain garden is not a facility that stops every heavy rain.
—It is an auxiliary wheel that temporarily receives water, slows its flow, and guides it in a safer direction.
—For rain beyond its limits, evacuation, traffic decisions, information, cleanup, and wider support must be layered together.
—Know that behind typhoons and heavy rains are large changes in the ocean and atmosphere.
—For that reason, do not concentrate the burden on one piece of land, one person’s goodwill, or one facility.

“Is this the real blueprint now?”

the bakery worker asked.

The landscaping worker answered.

“There are still parts to adjust as a water design.”

The disaster-prevention officer continued.

“And the management and cost design still need more detail.”

The vacant-lot owner said:

“My name is still blank.”

“It is blank.”

“We will call you first.”

The schoolteacher looked up at the Pacific map.

“When you know about the world, it makes you anxious.”

I nodded.

“It does.”

“But when I look at the town blueprint, it feels a little different.”

“How?”

“It is not only anxiety. I can see what to check next.”

The AI glowed quietly.

“That is what auxiliary wheels do.”

I wrote the final line.

—The distant heat of the sea casts a shadow even on the town’s small intersection. That is why the blueprint must draw not only water lines, but also world changes, town limits, and the promise that no one receives the burden alone.

“Next?”

No typhoon was visible yet.

But the town was no longer a town that did nothing just because something was invisible.

Part 7 Episode 14 ends here.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading Part 7 Episode 14.

This episode showed a town moving a temporary rain garden toward a full design while thinking not only about water flow at an intersection, but also about the larger background of global warming, El Niño, and a summer with many typhoons.

In 2026, the Japan Meteorological Agency regarded El Niño conditions as having continued from spring and likely to continue toward winter.

However, individual typhoons and heavy rains cannot be explained by El Niño alone.

Sea-surface temperature, the Pacific High, the westerlies, monsoon winds, and multiple other conditions relate to their paths and intensity.

It was reported that ten typhoons formed in August 2026, and ten in August alone was the highest number in sixty years. This active formation helps explain why multiple typhoons appeared to exist over the ocean at short intervals.

Japan was not affected to the same degree everywhere, but Typhoon Dolphin in early August brought evacuations, transport cancellations, and warnings for strong winds, high waves, and heavy rain mainly around Okinawa.

Typhoon Saudel in late August also heavily affected Okinawa and Amami, with reports of more than 30,000 households without power in the Amami region of Kagoshima Prefecture, more than 230 millimeters of rain in 24 hours, and warnings for landslide disasters.

Therefore, “many did not make landfall on Honshu” and “there was no impact on Japan or the areas around Kyushu” are not the same.

Especially in the southwestern islands, Amami, and the southwestern side including Kagoshima Prefecture’s island areas, strong winds, heavy rain, high waves, power outages, and transportation disruptions affected daily life.

The main points of this episode are four.

### 1. Global warming and El Niño are not magical one-to-one explanations for the town’s rain

El Niño and ocean warmth are part of the background that can influence typhoon formation areas and paths.

But it is not correct to simplify individual heavy rain as “because of warming” or “because of El Niño.”

What matters is connecting this background to the posture of not assuming only the same rain as in the past.

### 2. A rain garden is not an all-powerful disaster-prevention facility; it is an auxiliary wheel that creates time and escape routes

Rain gardens and planting strips do not receive all rainwater.

Their role is to slow the speed at which water concentrates at the intersection, move water away from crosswalks and residential walkways, and create time for people to evacuate or decide.

On days when rain may exceed their capacity, such as typhoons, evacuation, traffic decisions, information sharing, cleanup, and restoration must be layered together.

### 3. Do not push large climate problems onto one piece of land or one person’s goodwill

A design that says, “More heavy rain may come, so please receive all of it in this vacant lot,” is unfair.

Roads, gutters, planting strips, roofs, schools, shopping streets, administration, and residents’ communication networks must be combined, and burdens must be divided.

The vacant-lot owner’s opposing opinion is not a voice that stops the auxiliary wheel. It becomes a condition for making the town’s design realistic.

### 4. Think about mitigation and adaptation together, not separately

Reducing greenhouse gases and restraining the progress of warming is mitigation.

Reducing the impacts of heat, heavy rain, typhoons, and other changes already happening or likely to happen is adaptation.

The town’s mist, rain garden, school-route checks, evacuation, and communication systems are part of adaptation, but they cannot stop the rise of global temperature by themselves.

The distant line.

Reduce emissions and restrain warming.

The near line.

Prepare for the impacts of heat, heavy rain, and typhoons.

One alone is not enough.
