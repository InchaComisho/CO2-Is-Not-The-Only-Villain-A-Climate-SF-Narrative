# Part 7 Episode 15 — Where do we place auxiliary wheels in a town with no vacant lots?

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 14, the new town wrote not only the flow of water into the rain garden blueprint, but also the people who manage it, the people who clean it up, the costs, the contact points, and the limits of the equipment.

Far out in the Pacific, warm seas and the influence of El Niño overlapped, and a summer continued in which typhoons formed at short intervals.

Not all of Japan suffered the same kind of major damage every time.

But in Okinawa, Amami, and the southwestern side of Kyushu, there were effects such as strong winds, heavy rain, high waves, power outages, and transport disruption.

A town rain garden cannot stop global warming, distant ocean heat, or typhoons themselves.

Even so, it can become part of a town system that slightly slows the speed at which water gathers all at once, creates time for people to evacuate, and does not force the cleanup after rain onto one person.

In Episode 15, the protagonist and the AI take the white file and the blueprint to yet another town.

However, that town has no vacant lots.

The station front is filled with buildings.

The residential areas are narrow.

The parking lots are indispensable for deliveries and shopping.

There are few planting strips along the roads.

“If there is no place to make a rain garden, can we not place any auxiliary wheels?”

The town asks that question.

The protagonist and the AI begin walking again, carrying not an answer, but another question.

---

“There are no vacant lots.”

That was the first thing I thought after getting off at the station.

Buildings to the right.

Buildings to the left.

Shops in front.

Parking lots behind.

A small vacant lot.

An unused planting strip.

A wide grassy area.

Somewhere that looked able to receive rainwater.

There was nothing.

“This is difficult.”

I said.

“The previous town’s rain garden cannot be placed here.”

“Then did we bring the rain garden blueprint for nothing?”

The AI opened the white file.

Think separately about places that receive water and places that let water escape.

Check overflow routes in advance.

Do not concentrate the burden of water, mud, trash, and management on one person.

Support equipment limits with evacuation and communication.

“The method of digging a hollow cannot be used. But the questions can be used.”

“Questions again?”

“Auxiliary wheels are about sequence more than shape.”

“Sequence more than shape?”

“Before asking what to place, look at what is causing trouble.”

Feeling the heat at the station front, I nodded.

“Part 7 Episode 15. Searching for auxiliary wheels in a town with no vacant lots begins.”

## Section 1 — This town has few places to escape

The station-front square was concrete.

A bus stop.

A taxi stand.

A small supermarket.

Restaurants.

A drugstore.

Apartment buildings.

A bank.

A police box.

Summer heat bounced back from the ground.

“It’s hot.”

“It is.”

The person from the shopkeepers’ association, who had been waiting at the station front, answered.

“There is very little shade too.”

“What about mist?”

When I asked, the shopkeepers’ association member looked troubled.

“We would like to place it. But wind swirls here. The shops are close. We cannot easily connect water. And no one has decided who would inspect it.”

“It sounds like the previous town.”

“Only the similar parts.”

The AI displayed a map.

The previous town’s station front.

This town’s station front.

In the previous town, there was a little extra space beside the bus stop.

There was room to avoid the bakery entrance and adjust the nozzles.

There was also space for the tank.

This town had none of that extra space.

“What if we placed the same mist?”

“It would narrow passage. It would also be more likely to affect storefronts. The paths for water supply and inspection would be hard to secure.”

“Then no mist?”

“Please wait.”

The shopkeepers’ association member said.

“I do not want to decide that we can place nothing.”

I smiled a little.

“That is a good line.”

“Summer is too hot. Just leaving the station and waiting for the bus is exhausting. There are children, elderly people, and people coming back from shopping.”

“The trouble exists. But there is no place to put the previous town’s equipment.”

“Then either make the equipment smaller.”

“Or think about things other than equipment.”

A person who looked like a schoolteacher said from behind us.

“So there is a teacher here today too?”

“The school in this town uses the station front as part of its school route.”

“I see.”

I looked at the hot bench.

“Auxiliary wheels for heat are not only mist.”

“First, look at the lack of places to escape.”

“Places to escape?”

“Water needed an escape route. People need places to escape too.”

“That connects to Episode 11.”

“It is not the same town. But the questions connect.”

## Section 2 — There was an invisible vacant lot on the roof

“This way.”

We were guided behind the small supermarket at the station front.

Between buildings.

A narrow passage.

A delivery entrance.

And above it.

A flat roof.

“The rooftop?”

I looked up.

“Yes. The rooftop.”

The supermarket manager said.

“There are no vacant lots. But there is a roof.”

The AI brightened slightly.

“An invisible vacant lot.”

“It is different from the previous town’s vacant lot.”

“Rain falls on the roof.”

The manager said.

“Right now, it just goes down through the gutters. On days of strong rain, the gutter behind the store immediately looks strained.”

“Receive the roof water a little while it is still up there?”

“That is what we are thinking.”

On the rooftop were old outdoor air-conditioning units and an inspection walkway.

Everything else was a flat gray surface.

“We cannot make a large rain garden here.”

The landscaping worker said.

“If we put in deep soil, we would also have to think about the load on the building.”

“But can we do something?”

“There is a possibility.”

The AI drew several lines.

A tank that temporarily stores roof rainwater.

A part of the rooftop with shallow planting.

A structure that lets rain flow slowly.

A way to shift the timing at which water flows downward.

“It is not a rain garden.”

I said.

“It is not a ground-level rain garden.”

“But its role is similar: not making water rush downward all at once.”

“An auxiliary wheel placed on the roof.”

The manager said.

“However, the store cannot manage it alone.”

“That is where the previous town’s records can be used.”

I opened the white file.

Do not separate the place that receives water from the people who manage it.

Write down trash, mud, inspection, costs, and contact points from the beginning.

Do not create the equipment first and search for the people who clean it up later.

“It is the same even on a rooftop.”

The manager nodded.

“If we store rainwater, we have to decide in advance who checks it, who inspects it, and where the water goes if it overflows.”

“Even without a vacant lot, burdens can still become uneven.”

“Even when the place changes, the weak points of auxiliary wheels are similar.”

I looked down from the edge of the roof.

The ground was narrow.

But the sky was wide.

“A town with no vacant lots also has to look upward.”

“A town’s margins are not always only on the ground.”

## Section 3 — Roof water runs before ground water

“But what changes with only rooftop water?”

The shopkeepers’ association member asked.

“When strong rain falls, the water that lands on the roof gathers into the gutters all at once.”

I answered.

“If that water overlaps with road and parking-lot water at the same time, the gutter and low places below become strained.”

“So we make it wait a little on the roof?”

“Yes.”

The AI displayed two lines on the screen.

Left side.

Rain falls.

Roof water immediately flows into the gutters.

It gathers into the storm drain at the same time as road water.

Water increases in the low place.

Right side.

Rain falls.

Part of the roof water is temporarily received by a tank or rooftop mechanism.

After the peak of the rain passes a little, it flows slowly.

The time when water concentrates in the drain below shifts.

“It does not erase the amount of water.”

The delivery worker said.

“It does not.”

“But it reduces the amount that arrives at the same time.”

“Yes.”

“An auxiliary wheel is not magic that erases water. It is a structure that keeps water from rushing.”

“It is the same as the rain garden in the previous town.”

“But done on the roof instead of the ground.”

The manager looked at the edge of the roof.

“But what if the rooftop drain clogs?”

That one sentence changed the air slightly.

“That is important.”

The landscaping worker said.

“On a rooftop, the rooftop drain clogs instead of the gutter grate.”

“There are fewer leaves, but dust and trash build up.”

“What if inspection is forgotten?”

“Water may go somewhere unexpected.”

“Again, it is the same.”

I gave a wry smile.

“Whether on the ground or on a roof, water routes change on their own if we do not watch them.”

“That is why we write ‘after-rain inspection’ for the rooftop too.”

The manager said:

“Our employees cannot handle it alone.”

“Let’s make it a form that is not left to the store alone from the beginning.”

“Really?”

“We were troubled in the previous town because we delayed that.”

I wrote in the white file:

—If roof water is made to wait, rooftop drain inspection must be designed together with it.
—Do not make the roof auxiliary wheel the store’s responsibility alone.

“Even in a town with no vacant lots, the red notes increase.”

“Red notes do not choose places.”

## Section 4 — Auxiliary wheels for heat were scattered around the station front

After coming down from the roof, we walked the station front again.

There was no margin for mist.

There was little space to plant large trees.

There was no space to add wide benches.

“What do we do about heat?”

The shopkeepers’ association member asked.

“Receiving rain on the roof will not cool the people waiting for the bus.”

“That is true.”

I looked around the station front.

The supermarket entrance.

The eaves of the drugstore.

The small patch of shade beside the police box.

The bus-stop roof.

An unused space by the wall on the first floor of an apartment building.

“If we cannot make one large refuge from heat—”

The AI placed small circles on the map.

“Then increase small places to escape.”

“Small places to escape?”

“For example, place a spot near a store entrance where people can sit briefly.”

“But it could get in the store’s way.”

“Then make the form slightly different for each store.”

At the supermarket entrance, a sign for shaded rest and water guidance.

In front of the drugstore, a small folding stool.

At the bus stop, a simple seat that does not hold heat easily.

Near the police box, an information sign for people troubled by heat.

For the shopkeepers’ association, a mark for cooperating stores that can briefly let people at risk of heatstroke rest.

“Does that become an auxiliary wheel?”

The manager asked.

“I think it does.”

I answered.

“Even without one large device, if hot places are connected to places where people can stop, ask for help, and drink a little water, then people can choose their next action.”

“Even if they cannot completely escape the heat, it creates time to rest before collapsing.”

The schoolteacher said:

“It could also be used on the school route.”

“Not only the station front?”

“If the locations of cooperating stores and public facilities between the station and the school are clear, children and guardians can feel safer.”

“The whole town becomes a little shaded.”

“Not only physical shade?”

“Shade where people know help exists.”

The AI displayed:

Auxiliary wheels connect not only equipment, but also the ways places are used.

“Shade where people know help exists.”

“Even without vacant lots, places where people can stop can be made.”

“That is another kind of town margin.”

## Afterword

Thank you for reading Part 7 Episode 15.

This episode took place in a town filled with buildings and roads, without vacant lots or wide planting strips.

In the previous town, we used a vacant lot near a low intersection and considered how a rain garden and planting strip could slow the flow of water.

However, not every town has vacant lots.

In a town with little land, the previous town’s equipment cannot simply be copied.

Even so, the way of thinking behind auxiliary wheels can be handed over: do not make water rush all at once, create time to rest in heat, and do not concentrate burdens on one person or one place.

The main points of this episode are five.

### 1. Even without vacant lots, places that let rainwater wait can be found

Building rooftops, gutters, rainwater tanks, and shallow rooftop planting can become candidates for temporarily receiving rainwater or shifting the timing at which it flows all at once into drains or gutters.

They cannot erase the total amount of water.

Even so, they may reduce the concentration of water during the rain peak and lessen the burden on low places and drainage equipment.

### 2. Rooftops also have water routes and management exits

If a rooftop drain clogs, rainwater may flow somewhere unexpected.

When equipment that stores rainwater is installed, the town must decide in advance who inspects it, what is checked before and after rain, where the emergency contact point is, and how maintenance costs will be handled.

As with a ground-level rain garden, the method of “place the water first and think about management later” does not continue.

If roof water is made to wait, rooftop drain inspection must be designed together with it.

That viewpoint is necessary.

### 3. Heat measures do not have to rely only on one large device

Even in a town with no margin for mist, small places to escape can be increased by connecting brief rest at cooperating stores, water guidance, bus-stop seating, shade under eaves, and information signs for risky heat.

This does not erase the heat.

Even so, it creates time and options for people to rest, ask for help, or reconsider movement before their condition worsens.

### 4. The narrower the land, the more necessary it becomes to divide roles

Do not leave rooftop water to the store alone.

Do not leave resting places to one store alone.

Do not leave school-route checks to the school alone.

Do not leave after-rain danger information to the disaster-prevention officer alone.

By dividing small margins and small roles throughout the town, auxiliary wheels become easier to keep turning without concentrating the burden on one person.

A town with no vacant lots does not have no margins.

It can create small margins between roles.

### 5. Auxiliary wheels should be judged not by the size of the equipment, but by the room they create for people to choose the next action

If there is a vacant lot, a town can choose the form of a rain garden.

If there is no vacant lot, it can choose a form that connects roofs, gutters, eaves, guidance, cooperating stores, and inspection systems.

Part 7 Episode 15 shows that even when town conditions differ, the purpose of auxiliary wheels remains the same: to create time for people to move a little more safely in the face of sudden water and heat, and to divide burdens.

In a town with no vacant lots, do not place auxiliary wheels in one place.

Connect small margins in roofs, eaves, storefronts, guidance, inspection, and rest, and create time for people to move between heat and rain.

Auxiliary wheels are not only placed on wide land.

They connect small margins in a town and create time for people to choose their next action.

These two lines are the center of Part 7 Episode 15.

A tank that lets rainwater wait.

Inspection of drains.
