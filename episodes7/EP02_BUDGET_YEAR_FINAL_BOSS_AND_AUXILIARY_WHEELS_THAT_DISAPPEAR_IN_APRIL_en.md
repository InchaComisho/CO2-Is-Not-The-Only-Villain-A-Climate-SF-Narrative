# Part 7 Episode 2 — The budget year final boss, and auxiliary wheels that disappear in April

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 1, we saw the possibility that small auxiliary wheels such as town mist, rain gardens, retention basins, and fishing-port water-temperature charts could cross town borders, pass to other regions, and become “words that move toward the world.”

In Episode 2, before those auxiliary wheels can leave the town, we look at a much closer and stronger wall.

The budget year.

Subsidies.

Responsible departments.

The fiscal year-end.

And April.

In nature, auxiliary wheels face water and heat.

But inside town systems and administrative structures, they can stop because of a single sentence: “It is not included in this year’s budget.”

This is the episode where the protagonist and the AI stand in a year-end meeting room and think about “auxiliary wheels that disappear in April” and “budgets for continuing.”

---

“Part 7 is starting with a nasty enemy.”

I looked at the calendar on the screen.

March.

Fiscal year-end.

Closing the accounts.

Budget requests.

Subsidy applications.

Project evaluation.

“It is not a natural disaster,” the AI said.

“But it has a strong power to stop the town’s auxiliary wheels.”

“Exactly.”

“Upstream soil, rain gardens, mist, retention basins...”

I counted them on my fingers.

“They could all suddenly disappear once April comes.”

“That is the magic phrase: ‘This ends with the current fiscal year.’”

“Not magic. A curse.”

“A final-boss candidate for Part 7.”

“A final boss that is way too plain.”

“But strong.”

“That is annoyingly convincing.”

I looked at the line between March 31 and April 1 on the calendar.

A natural river does not know that line.

Summer heat does not know that line.

But a budget table knows that line.

“A fiscal year is like an invisible levee.”

“A levee that stops projects, not water.”

“I hate that metaphor.”

“But it is very Part 7.”

## Section 1 — Can auxiliary wheels live only until the end of March?

On the screen was station-front mist equipment.

Last summer, it operated on days with heatstroke alerts.

People from the shopping street, commuters, and people waiting for the bus used it.

“Even a few minutes helped.”

“It was easier to wait than last year.”

“There was a place where children could stop.”

Those voices remained in the records.

“This should continue.”

“Many people would think so.”

“And yet?”

The AI opened the next screen.

Demonstration project.

One fiscal year only.

Subsidy ended.

Next year’s budget undecided.

“Ugh.”

“April.”

“April is scary.”

The mist equipment was installed with last year’s budget.

Inspection costs, electricity, and water were also paid from last year’s frame.

The staff member also worked as “the person in charge of the demonstration project.”

But the fiscal year changes.

The budget name changes.

The person in charge changes.

The demonstration project ends.

The frame for continuing has not yet been decided.

“The auxiliary wheel is needed in summer, but its life or death is decided in a March meeting.”

“The season of nature and the season of institutions are misaligned.”

“That’s it.”

I wrote a note.

— Auxiliary wheels become necessary in nature’s seasons.
— But they easily stop in institutional seasons.

“As an entrance to Episode 2, that is quite good.”

“You praised me.”

“It is fiscal year-end.”

“That is not a reason.”

Mist becomes necessary in July or August.

The meaning of rain gardens and retention basins becomes visible in the season of strong rain.

Water-temperature charts at fishing ports become urgent when the sea changes.

But whether those things continue may be decided in a March meeting room.

“The season when they are needed and the season when they stop are different.”

“That is the troublesome part.”

“For auxiliary wheels to cross seasons, they also have to cross budget years.”

“Now it is becoming Part 7.”

## Section 2 — In the unmoving world line, they stop at “there is no precedent”

“Then show me the unmoving world line.”

The AI displayed a meeting room on the left side.

Long tables.

Documents.

Budget sheets.

A projector.

The sound of air conditioning.

“Regarding the station-front mist...”

“As a demonstration project in the current fiscal year, it achieved certain results.”

“However, regarding continuation from next fiscal year...”

“I hate that pause.”

“That is the silence of a meeting.”

“Which department will have jurisdiction?”

“Is it shopping-street revitalization, heat countermeasures, disaster prevention, or urban development?”

“Who pays the water bill?”

“Where does responsibility go if it breaks?”

“Everyone is saying something correct.”

“That is why it does not move.”

The shopping-street staff member says:

“Our revitalization budget cannot cover equipment that operates every day.”

The disaster-prevention staff member says:

“It is not disaster-time equipment, so priority is difficult.”

The urban-development staff member says:

“If it is to become permanent infrastructure, design and planning are necessary.”

The accounting staff member says:

“There is no precedent.”

“There it is.”

“The final form: ‘There is no precedent.’”

“That really feels like a final boss.”

As a result, the mist operates only on the day of the summer festival.

It does not operate on dangerous hot weekdays.

The equipment remains, but as an auxiliary wheel, it has stopped.

“It did not break.”

“It fell into a gap between systems.”

“That is the most troublesome way to stop.”

If it were broken, it could be repaired.

If it had been removed, the reason could be asked.

But something that falls into an institutional gap is stopped even though responsibility is hard to see.

“That was working last year, wasn’t it?”

“What about this year?”

“I heard the person in charge changed.”

“I heard there is no budget.”

While people say that, summer arrives.

“Auxiliary wheels that disappear in April are remembered as necessary in July.”

“But by then, there is no frame to operate them.”

“The final boss is strong.”

## Section 3 — In the slightly bent world line, the budget also has a receiving basin

“What about the right side?”

The AI opened another meeting room.

The same long tables.

The same budget sheets.

The same staff members.

But at the center of the table, there was one sheet of paper.

Heat, disaster prevention, and local-stay support — joint frame.

“That name is long.”

“Because it is a name multiple departments can use.”

“I see.”

If it is only shopping-street revitalization, mist becomes an event.

If it is only disaster prevention, it is hard to operate in ordinary times.

If it is only urban development, design takes time.

If it is only health, it may not reach station-front equipment management.

So heat countermeasures, disaster prevention, local places to stay, shopping-street stay support, and public-space maintenance each place part of their purpose into one joint frame.

“Do not make one department carry the entire auxiliary wheel.”

“The Part 6 idea of dividing burdens has entered the budget.”

“After looking at the world’s red graphs, even the budget table becomes a team battle.”

“Auxiliary wheels are a team battle.”

The mist’s water bill is under the heat countermeasure frame.

Inspection cost is under public-space maintenance.

Guidance during use is under shopping-street operation.

Records of effect are shared by disaster prevention and health.

“One section does not carry everything.”

“That makes it harder to disappear in April.”

“The budget also needs a receiving basin, like a retention basin.”

The AI paused slightly.

“That is a good metaphor.”

“You are praising me a lot today.”

“This is an important point.”

Without a budget receiving basin, auxiliary wheels cannot flow through institutional channels.

They spill out of the demonstration project frame.

They stop at the border between departments.

They disappear at fiscal year-end.

“If there are rain gardens that receive water, we also need budget gardens that receive projects.”

“The phrase is strange, but the idea is close.”

“Budget garden.”

“I do not know if that will stick.”

“Let’s not use it.”

“But demonstrations are important, right?”

“They are important.”

“As the first step.”

“Yes.”

Actually operate the mist.

See how much water a rain garden holds.

Test how to let water into a retention basin.

Actually use a water-temperature chart at the port.

“Without trying, nothing can be known.”

“But demonstration alone does not continue.”

“A demonstration project is for trying.”

“Yes.”

“What an auxiliary wheel needs is to continue after it is tried.”

The AI placed two documents on the screen.

Left:

Demonstration project report.

It had an effect.

Issues were found.

Ends this fiscal year.

Right:

Continuation operation plan.

Who maintains it?

Who holds the budget?

What is recorded?

What will be fixed next fiscal year?

What will be reviewed three years later?

“Without the right side, the auxiliary wheel becomes a one-time event.”

“That is the answer from Part 5.”

“A demonstration is the beginning.”

“Continuation is a different project.”

“This is really important.”

Before introducing an auxiliary wheel, decide not only when it ends, but also who will continue it after the trial ends.

“The goal of a demonstration is not the report.”

“It is operating again next summer.”

“That feels very Part 7.”

“Because it is the connecting point between institutions and everyday life.”

Demonstration projects are necessary.

Trying something has meaning.

Starting small has meaning.

But merely demonstrating something does not pass an auxiliary wheel to the next season.

Write the report.

Organize the issues.

Preserve the voices of users.

Estimate costs.

Divide responsibilities.

Draw the maintenance line.

Connect it to the next fiscal year’s frame.

“There needs to be a bridge from demonstration to continuation.”

“Yes.”

“Without a bridge, it falls into the river at the end of March.”

“As a metaphor, it is rough, but close.”

“Even though it is a budget.”

## Afterword

In Part 7 Episode 2, we looked at the budget year and the continuation of auxiliary wheels.

Auxiliary wheels such as mist, rain gardens, retention basins, and water-temperature charts face heat, water, and changes in the sea in nature.

But inside institutions, they are also easy to stop for reasons such as “the demonstration project has ended,” “departments do not match,” “there is no budget frame,” and “there is no precedent.”

The main points of this episode are three.

### 1. Nature’s seasons and institutional seasons are misaligned

Mist is needed in summer, and rain gardens and retention basins are needed in the season of heavy rain.

But their continuation can be decided in a March meeting room and stopped by the budget switch in April.

The gap between the season when auxiliary wheels are needed and the season when budgets are decided makes continuation difficult.

Auxiliary wheels become necessary in nature’s seasons.

But they easily stop in institutional seasons.

That misalignment is at the center of Part 7 Episode 2.

### 2. One department alone has difficulty supporting an auxiliary wheel

Mist crosses multiple purposes, including shopping-street revitalization, disaster prevention, heat countermeasures, urban development, and health.

That is why the idea of a joint frame, where purposes and costs are divided instead of placing everything on one department, becomes necessary.

The Part 6 idea of not concentrating burdens on one person enters the budget as well.

A budget also needs a receiving basin, like a retention basin that receives water.

Part 7 will continue to look at that perspective.

### 3. After a demonstration project, a continuation operation plan is needed

Demonstration is necessary for trying an auxiliary wheel and learning its effects and issues.

But a demonstration report alone does not make the auxiliary wheel operate the next year.

Only when there is a continuation operation plan that decides who maintains it, who pays the cost, what is recorded, and when it is reviewed can an auxiliary wheel pass to the next season.

The goal of a demonstration is not only a report.

It is that it operates again next summer.

Part 7 Episode 2 was about how one line in a budget table or one staff member’s name can connect to summer mist, evacuation time on heavy-rain days, and decisions at the port.

Auxiliary wheels should not be demonstrations that disappear at the end of March, but mechanisms passed to the next season.

The numbers written in March answer the heat of July.

These two lines are the center of Part 7 Episode 2.

Next time, we may look at the “wall of numbers” between the people who use budgets and the people who decide them.

How should the effects of auxiliary wheels be explained?

What should be counted, and what do numbers alone fail to catch?

Part 7 will fight not only nature, but also explanatory documents.
