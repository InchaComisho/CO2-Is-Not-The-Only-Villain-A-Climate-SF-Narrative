# Part 7 Episode 3 — How do we write “helped” that cannot become a number into a budget document?

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 2, we saw that auxiliary wheels tend to stop not inside nature, but at the walls of budget years and responsible departments.

Mist, rain gardens, retention basins, and water-temperature charts do not continue just because they were tested once.

To hand them to the next season, maintenance costs, roles, shared budget frames, and continuing-operation plans become necessary.

In Episode 3, we deal with the wall that comes even before that.

The wall of numbers.

How many people were helped by the station-front mist?

How much water did the rain garden temporarily hold?

How much damage did the retention basin prevent?

How much earlier did the fishing port’s water-temperature chart help people decide?

The effects of auxiliary wheels do not always become neat numbers.

This is the episode where the protagonist and the AI think about how to place the feeling of “that helped” inside a budget document.

“See?”

## Section 1 — In the world line that does not move, it stops as “effect unknown”

On the left side of the screen, an end-of-year evaluation meeting appeared.

A projector.

A white table.

The person in charge.

The budget officer.

The minutes.

“Please explain the project effect of the station-front mist.”

“Yes. It was operated on extremely hot days and received a certain amount of use.”

“We did not capture the exact number of people.”

“Ugh.”

“The air in the meeting cools down.”

“Compared with the case where there was no mist, how much did heatstroke risk decrease?”

“We were not able to measure that far.”

“What about economic impact?”

“We do not have a detailed comparison of time spent in the shopping street.”

“The numbers they’re asking for are too strong.”

“Auxiliary-wheel effects are hard to test with a control group.”

“You can’t make a simultaneous world line where the mist didn’t exist.”

“No.”

The same thing happens in a rain-garden meeting.

“How much flooding did it prevent during last year’s heavy rain?”

“We cannot state that exactly as the rain garden’s effect alone.”

The same thing happens in a retention-basin meeting.

“Compared with not sending water into it, how much damage cost was reduced?”

“Because multiple conditions were involved, it cannot be calculated simply.”

The same thing happens with the port’s water-temperature chart.

“If there had been no chart, how much more loss would have occurred?”

“Fishing grounds, tides, wind, and experience also matter, so we cannot isolate the chart alone.”

“They’re all doing real work.”

“And it ends with ‘effect unknown.’”

“That’s too harsh for auxiliary wheels.”

“If they are seen only through numbers, they stop easily.”

Auxiliary wheels are not devices that create miracles on their own.

Mist alone does not erase extreme heat.

A rain garden alone does not stop heavy rain.

A retention basin alone does not make floods zero.

A water-temperature chart alone does not guarantee a good catch.

That is why explanation becomes difficult if someone tries to carve out only one effect.

“They all work a little inside multiple overlapping conditions.”

“Yes.”

“But in meetings, people ask, ‘Please show the effect of this project alone.’”

“They do.”

“The auxiliary wheel is being sent into the meeting room solo.”

“And then told it lacks firepower.”

“What a terrible game balance.”

## Afterword

In Part 7 Episode 3, we looked at how to leave “helped” that cannot become numbers inside budget documents and continuing-operation materials.

The effects of auxiliary wheels do not always become simple numbers.

A person who could rest a little under the mist.

A period of time when water stopped once in a rain garden.

The possibility that a retention basin helped evacuation begin earlier.

A port whose decision moved a little earlier because of a water-temperature chart.

Because perfect control experiments are not possible, these differences cannot always be written simply as “this many people were saved” or “this much money was gained.”

The points in this episode are mainly three.

### 1. Auxiliary wheels easily stop as “effect unknown”

Mist, rain gardens, retention basins, and water-temperature charts work under overlapping conditions, so it is difficult to isolate the effect of the auxiliary wheel alone.

In meetings that demand numbers only, they may be treated as “the exact number of people is unknown,” “comparison is impossible,” or “the effect cannot be proven,” making continuation difficult.

Auxiliary wheels are not devices that create miracles alone.

That is why they can look weak when judged only by solo firepower.

### 2. Effects should be preserved by “counting, observing, and listening”

Count.

Operating days, operating hours, water level, rainfall, evacuation start time, and so on.

Observe.

Photos, maps, water in the rain garden, the state of the retention basin, the reach of the mist, the state of the sea at the port, and so on.

Listen.

“I could rest.”

“I could move earlier.”

“The water did not come all at once.”

“We could read the sea a little.”

These voices from life and work.

By layering numbers, the site, and voices, the small differences made by auxiliary wheels can be preserved without erasing them.

Do not exaggerate the effect.

But do not erase it.

That stance matters.

### 3. A viewpoint for “damage that did not happen” is needed

Auxiliary wheels do not make damage zero.

As heat, rain, and sea changes become harsher, they may carry the possibility that things “did not become worse than they otherwise would have” or “did not become even later.”

Therefore, meeting rooms need not only damage costs and incident counts, but also the comparison question: “what if the auxiliary wheel had not existed?”

A perfect answer cannot be produced.

Even so, a comparison viewpoint can be placed.

To avoid being labeled “effect unknown,” do not leave “impossible to compare” untouched.

This too is part of the work of handing auxiliary wheels to the next season.

Part 7 Episode 3 became an episode about carrying the feeling of “that helped” together with numbers, photos, time, and voices into the next budget and the next season, without leaving it as emotion alone.

Leaving behind the “helped” that cannot become numbers is also part of the work of handing auxiliary wheels to the next season.

Do not exaggerate the effect.

But do not erase the small difference that helped.

These two lines are the center of Part 7 Episode 3.

Next time, we will likely look at who those numbers are explained to.

Budget officers, residents, the shopping street, the council, another town, the port.

Depending on the listener, the same auxiliary wheel needs different words of explanation.

Part 7 moves toward “auxiliary wheels for explanation,” so that auxiliary wheels can be passed outside the town.
