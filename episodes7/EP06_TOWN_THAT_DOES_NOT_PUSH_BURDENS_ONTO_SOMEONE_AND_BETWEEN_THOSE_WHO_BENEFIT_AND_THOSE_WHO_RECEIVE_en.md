# Part 7 Episode 6 — A town that does not push burdens onto someone, and the space between “those who benefit” and “those who receive it”

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 5, we did not turn people who oppose auxiliary wheels into villains. Instead, we looked at the anxieties behind their opposition.

For mist, the anxiety is cost and the effect on products.

For rain gardens, it is maintenance and responsibility.

For retention basins, it is fear of water and unevenly placed burdens.

For water-temperature charts, it is the fear that experience and work will be treated carelessly.

In Episode 6, we deal with the center of those anxieties: burden.

What should a town do when the people helped by an auxiliary wheel and the people who receive the burden near it are not the same?

This is the episode where the protagonist and the AI revisit the shopping street, rain gardens, retention basins, and fishing port, and build a bridge between “those who benefit” and “those who receive it.”

## Afterword

In Part 7 Episode 6, we dealt with the problem that the people who are helped by an auxiliary wheel and the people who receive burdens near it may be different.

Mist, rain gardens, retention basins, and water-temperature charts may bring benefits to the region as a whole.

But if the cost, maintenance, anxiety, responsibility, or relearning burden concentrates on specific people, the auxiliary wheel becomes hard to continue.

The main points in this episode are three.

1. When benefits are wide but burdens concentrate nearby, opposition is more likely to appear
   With station-front mist, the whole town may use it while only the shopping street carries costs and inspections.

With rain gardens, the watershed may benefit while only the person with the garden carries maintenance and responsibility.

With retention basins, downstream areas may be helped while nearby residents receive anxiety about water and the burden of recovery.

With port water-temperature charts, the whole port may make decisions more easily while specific people alone carry the burden of reading and updating them.

Even if an auxiliary wheel is “good,” opposition naturally appears when the burden is biased toward the people nearby.

2. Auxiliary wheels also need “auxiliary wheels” around them
   Mist needs shared costs and shared maintenance.

Rain gardens need maintenance support, contact points, and clear responsibility lines.

Retention basins need ordinary-time use, recovery support, and dialogue with nearby residents.

Water-temperature charts need systems that place experience and data side by side and share updating work.

By placing not only auxiliary wheels, but also auxiliary wheels that support them, the concentration of burdens can be reduced.

People who support auxiliary wheels also need auxiliary wheels.

3. Fairness is not making everything identical; it is making burdens visible
   It is difficult for everyone to benefit equally and carry the same burden.

That is why it is necessary, from the beginning, to make visible who is helped, who pays, who maintains, and who carries anxiety.

Once the burdens are visible, the possibility appears to share costs, labor, information, compensation, and recovery.

Part 7 Episode 6 portrayed auxiliary wheels not only as structures that increase the number of people helped, but also as structures that prevent burdens from concentrating on one person.

Do not reproduce, inside regional auxiliary wheels, the unfairness shown by the world’s red graphs in miniature.

For that reason too, auxiliary wheels need a map of burdens.

Auxiliary wheels are not enough if they only increase the number of people helped.

They must become structures that do not gather the burden onto one person.

Do not lay one person’s life under an auxiliary wheel.

These three points are the center of Part 7 Episode 6.

Next time, we will likely look at how the people who pay for auxiliary wheels, the people who receive burdens, and the people who use them come to agreement.

Not only meetings, but walks, site visits, trial runs, and small promises.

Auxiliary wheels are sometimes agreed upon not only on paper, but in the field.
