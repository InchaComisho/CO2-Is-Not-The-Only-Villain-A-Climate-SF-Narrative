# Part 7 Episode 5 — What are people who oppose auxiliary wheels afraid of?

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 4, we saw that when explaining auxiliary wheels, “correct materials” are not enough. We also need words that connect to the other person’s next action.

Residents, shopping streets, ports, budget officers, and other towns.

By changing the entry point for each audience, auxiliary wheels can gradually pass outside the town.

However, explaining something does not mean everyone will agree.

For mist, some people say, “Maintenance will cost money.”

For rain gardens, some worry, “Won’t mosquitoes increase?”

For retention basins, some fear, “Are you going to put water near my home?”

For water-temperature charts, some push back, “Are you denying the intuition we have built over the years?”

In Part 7 Episode 5, we look at the fear and circumstances behind that opposition, so that people who oppose auxiliary wheels are not turned into “villains.”

To see what lay behind them.

## Section 1 — Opposition does not always mean hating the auxiliary wheel itself

“Let’s start with mist.”

A shopping arcade appeared on the screen.

Thin mist pipes.

Water supply.

Electricity.

Inspection records.

A summer alert day.

And the words of a shop owner.

“I don’t mind turning on the mist, but who pays the water bill?”

“If it breaks, who repairs it?”

“If the area in front of the shop gets damp, will the merchandise be okay?”

“That’s less opposition and more a realistic question.”

“Yes.”

The shop owner is not denying the heat.

He is not denying a place where people can rest.

He is simply carrying his shop’s sales, products, tasks, labor, and monthly payments.

“If we just say, ‘It’s a good thing, so let’s do it,’ we can’t see the burden on that person.”

“If the cost of the auxiliary wheel falls on a specific person, opposition is natural.”

“Behind the opposition, there may be, ‘Am I the only one who has to carry this?’”

“In some cases, yes.”

Mist may cool the station front a little.

It may help people waiting for the bus.

It may create time for people to stop in the shopping street.

But if the water bill, inspections, and breakdown response all slide toward one shop owner, the story changes.

“Auxiliary wheels were supposed to be a system that does not push everything onto one person, right?”

“We saw that in Part 6.”

“But if the cost of mist gets pushed onto only some shops, the auxiliary wheel stops being an auxiliary wheel.”

“Exactly.”

“Maybe the people opposing it are not opposing heat countermeasures. Maybe they are opposing where the burden is being placed.”

“That is an important line for Episode 5.”

## Section 2 — A rain garden begins with the anxiety of the person lending the garden

“Next, the rain garden.”

The AI showed a small garden in a residential area.

A shallow depression.

Grass.

A rain gutter.

A place where water gathers.

Beside it were residents’ speech bubbles.

“Won’t mosquitoes increase?”

“Won’t a child fall in?”

“If the water overflows, will our house be responsible?”

“Who will maintain the grass?”

“I understand these too.”

“A rain garden is a place that receives water once.”

“But from the garden owner’s point of view, it’s a place that holds water on their own property.”

“Yes.”

From the whole watershed, it is a small auxiliary wheel.

But for the person lending the garden, it is a place they see every day.

If the grass grows, they notice it.

If water gathers, they worry.

If insects appear, it becomes a problem for their home.

If neighbors say something, they are the one who receives it.

“Even if someone says, ‘Please do this for the watershed,’ the person with the garden has a life.”

“If we want to spread auxiliary wheels, we must not turn that anxiety into ‘personal selfishness.’”

“Support for maintenance. Clear responsibility lines. Checks for mosquitoes and safety. A contact point for problems.”

“Without those, a rain garden becomes an imposition of goodwill.”

“An imposition of goodwill.”

“One form in which auxiliary wheels stop continuing.”

If we hurry only to create rain gardens, the daily life of the person living there gets left behind.

On a watershed map, it may be a small green dot.

But that dot is someone’s garden.

Someone is looking at the grass.

Someone is checking it after rain.

Someone is feeling anxious.

“A dot on the map has a life attached to it.”

“A very Part 7 summary.”

## Section 3 — Opposition to a retention basin is the fear of “the side that receives the water”

“Retention basins are even heavier.”

A grassy area along the river appeared on the screen.

On ordinary days, people walk there.

Dogs pass through.

Children run.

A levee can be seen in the distance.

And on heavy-rain days, it is also a place where water enters.

“Why put water near us?”

“Does having a retention basin mean only our town has to endure it?”

“What happens to land value?”

“If it floods, who cleans up?”

For a moment, I had no words.

“What is the reason for the opposition?”

“Fear.”

“Yes.”

From the whole watershed, the retention basin is an auxiliary wheel that receives water.

But for people living nearby, it is also a system that brings water close to them.

“Even if someone says, ‘It’s for the watershed,’ the people in that place carry that anxiety every year.”

“It is a problem where the burden and the benefit separate into different places.”

“The downstream town may be helped. But the people near the retention basin alone carry the anxiety.”

“That is why explanation alone is not enough.”

“Compensation. Rules for use. Recovery. Ordinary-time uses. Who cleans up after water enters.”

“We must not leave only anxiety and burden with the people who receive the burden.”

“This continues the Part 6 idea of dividing time and burden.”

A retention basin may be necessary for the watershed.

But necessity does not erase the anxiety of nearby people.

A place that receives water has the fear of receiving water.

After the water recedes, cleanup remains.

The way the land is seen changes.

The way it is treated inside the town also changes.

“Goodwill won’t be enough here.”

“No.”

“If we don’t look at roles, burdens, and compensation, it becomes an imposition, not an auxiliary wheel.”

“Exactly.”

But if it enters with a face that says, “Old ways are outdated” or “data is more correct than experience,” pushback is natural.

## Afterword

In Part 7 Episode 5, we looked at the theme: what are people who oppose auxiliary wheels afraid of?

Mist, rain gardens, retention basins, and water-temperature charts can look necessary as countermeasures against climate heat and water.

But for people who actually live and work in those places, there are real issues: costs, maintenance, responsibility, anxiety about water, and pride in their work.

The main points of this episode are these three.

1. Opposition is not always a rejection of the auxiliary wheel itself

   A shop owner worries about water bills and effects on products.

   A person with a rain garden worries about maintenance, safety, and responsibility.

   People near a retention basin fear incoming water and uneven burdens.

   Fishers worry that introducing data will treat their experience carelessly.

None of these are simply “it would be better to do nothing.”

In some cases, people are not afraid of the auxiliary wheel itself, but of how cost, responsibility, unfairness, and dignity are handled.

2. Before persuasion, ask, “What are you afraid of?”

   Showing materials and proving correctness is not enough to remove anxiety inside someone’s life or work.

   What do they not want to lose?

   How far can they accept it?

   Who is the burden concentrating on?

   Where might we be treating their experience or life carelessly?

We need to ask these questions and then change the shape of the auxiliary wheel, as well as the placement of cost, maintenance, and responsibility.

3. Opposition can be a voice that shows the weak places of the auxiliary wheel

   Who pays for mist?

   Who maintains the rain garden?

   How do we avoid concentrating anxiety and burden on people near the retention basin?

   How do we place water-temperature charts alongside fishers’ experience?

An auxiliary wheel that cannot answer these questions is unlikely to continue.

Opposition is not always an enemy that destroys auxiliary wheels.

It can also be an entrance for rethinking where the burden is placed.

Part 7 Episode 5 was an episode about receiving people who oppose not as “enemies of auxiliary wheels,” but as voices saying, “Do not leave our lives behind.”

Opposition to auxiliary wheels is not a voice that hates the future.

It is a voice saying, do not leave our lives behind.

Opposition is not always the enemy of auxiliary wheels.

It can also be an entrance for rethinking where the burden is placed.

These two lines are the center of Part 7 Episode 5.

Next time, after listening to the reasons for opposition, we will likely look at “then how do we divide the burden?”

The cost of mist.

The maintenance of rain gardens.

The anxiety around retention basins.

The decisions at the port.

Who carries the burden, who receives the benefit, and how do we compensate?

Part 7 moves toward consensus and burden sharing for continuing auxiliary wheels.
