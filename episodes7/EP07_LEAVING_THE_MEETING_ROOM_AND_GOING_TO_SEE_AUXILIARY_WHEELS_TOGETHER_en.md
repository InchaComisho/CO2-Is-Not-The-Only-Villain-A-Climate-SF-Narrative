# Part 7 Episode 7 — Leaving the meeting room, and going to see the auxiliary wheels together

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 6, we looked at the problem that the people helped by auxiliary wheels and the people who receive the burden near them often become different groups.

The cost of mist.

The maintenance of rain gardens.

Anxiety about retention basins.

The burden of reading water-temperature charts.

For auxiliary wheels to continue, it is necessary to make not only their effects but also their burdens visible, and to create a structure that does not concentrate them on one person.

In Episode 7, we put down the meeting-room documents for a moment and go out to the actual sites.

The shopping-street mist.

The residential rain garden.

The grassland of the retention basin.

The fishing-port water-temperature chart.

People who miss one another when they only exchange words begin to talk a little when they look at the same place, and at the same water and heat.

This is the episode where the protagonist and the AI learn that consensus is not completed inside the meeting room.

---

“We have been in meeting rooms all through Part 7.”

Budgets.

Effects.

Explanations.

Opposition.

Burdens.

Consensus.

“I feel like we have been spinning auxiliary wheels on paper too much.”

“Paper is necessary too.”

“I know.”

I straightened the edges of the documents.

“But I think it is time to go outside.”

“To the places where the auxiliary wheels are?”

“Exactly.”

Station-front mist.

Rain gardens.

Retention basins.

Fishing ports.

“Part 7 Episode 7 is an on-site tour.”

“You sound a little excited.”

“Compared with a meeting room, yes.”

“When you go to the site, you can see things the documents did not show.”

“Like heat?”

“Heat, water, grass, dampness, and people's expressions.”

“The amount of information suddenly increases.”

“Because reality is like that.”

I closed the meeting-room screen.

The next thing I opened was the town map.

## Section 1 — In the meeting room, everyone is seeing a different scene

On the left side of the screen, the meeting room we had been using appeared.

Long tables.

Documents.

Graphs.

Cost tables.

A projector.

A shopping-street shopkeeper.

A homeowner whose garden would become a rain garden.

A person living near the retention basin.

A fisher.

A municipal officer.

A neighborhood association member.

“Even though they are in the same meeting room, everyone is looking at something different.”

“What do you mean?”

“The shopkeeper is looking at the water bill. The garden owner is looking at grass and mosquitoes. The person near the retention basin is looking at what happens after water comes. The fisher is looking at the sea. The municipality is looking at the budget table.”

“They are not looking at the same auxiliary wheel.”

“Right.”

The documents say how many days the mist operated.

But the shopkeeper is thinking, “My products might get damp.”

The documents say how much water the rain garden can hold.

But the garden owner is thinking, “Who will cut the grass?”

The documents show the water-level line for the retention basin.

But nearby residents are thinking, “Will mud remain after the water recedes?”

The documents say how many times the water-temperature chart was updated.

But the fisher is thinking, “Will this chart deny the experience we have built up?”

“On paper, it is hard to see where the other person is afraid.”

“That is why we go to the site.”

“It is not that the meeting room is bad.”

“No. But the meeting room alone is not enough.”

“Part 7 keeps saying ‘alone is not enough.’”

“That is also at the center of this story.”

“Not wrong, but not sufficient.”

“That is it.”

Meeting rooms are necessary.

Documents, numbers, and minutes are necessary too.

But auxiliary wheels are not placed in meeting rooms.

They are placed at stations.

In gardens.

On grasslands.

At ports.

They are placed where people live, work, and pass through.

“Then we have to see those places first.”

“Exactly.”

## Section 2 — Talking about the water bill under the mist

The first place we came to was the shopping street.

A midsummer noon.

Under the arcade.

A thin mist quietly flowed.

“This is much easier to understand than the meeting room.”

“Because it is hot.”

“It is a different kind of understandability.”

People waiting for the bus stood near the mist.

Someone returning from shopping stopped for a moment.

A child passed under the mist and laughed.

The shopkeeper watched the front of the shop and paid attention to the direction of the nozzles.

“So this is where it would be a problem if it got damp.”

“In the meeting room, that was only written as ‘effect on products.’”

“At the site, we can see which products, at what time, and by how much it matters.”

Point the nozzle a little upward.

Move it half a step away from the shop entrance.

Operate it mainly on heatstroke-alert days.

Stop it when the wind is strong.

In front of shops where products are especially affected, adjust the direction individually.

“The person who opposed it did not hate the mist itself.”

“They disliked a form that burdened only their shop.”

“Then the form can be changed.”

“That is the first form of consensus.”

The water bill also became a topic right there.

“It is hard for the shopping street alone to pay.”

“Can the town cover part of it as a heat countermeasure?”

“Can the municipal maintenance team come along on inspection days?”

“Can we avoid leaving the duty only to shopping-street rotations?”

“When we talk under the mist, we can see what the money is actually paying for.”

“It is not only paying to reduce heat. It is paying for ‘time that can be waited here.’”

“That sounds very Part 7.”

“Look beyond the cost to the scene where it is used.”

“That is hard to see except on site.”

The mist is not just equipment.

On a hot day, it becomes a place where someone can wait a little longer.

But the shopfront of the person who supports that place is also part of the same site.

Both sides have to be seen while the form is adjusted.

## Section 3 — Talking about grass and water in front of the rain garden

Next, we came to a residential rain garden.

Water falling from the roof passed through a narrow gutter and entered a shallow depression.

Grass remained there.

The soil was slightly damp.

“It looks more like an ordinary garden than it did in the photographs.”

“That is exactly why maintenance becomes real.”

The garden owner pointed to the edge of the rain garden.

“When the grass gets too long here, it bothers me.”

“But I was told that if I cut all of it, the way water enters will change.”

“To be honest, I do not know how much to leave.”

“In the meeting room, it was only one sentence: ‘leave grass.’”

“At the site, it becomes ‘which grass, how much, and who leaves it.’”

The officer looked at the height of the grass and said:

“Let us leave this much here.”

“This part is a pathway, so let us cut it.”

“The management team will help twice a year.”

“If the water overflows, contact this place.”

“Suddenly, the rain garden is no longer ‘only my responsibility.’”

“An auxiliary wheel has been placed around the auxiliary wheel.”

Neighbors also watched how the water entered on rainy days.

“So water stops here once.”

“Before it goes to the drain, it stops by the garden.”

“So it is not just a puddle?”

“Once you see it, it stops being just a puddle.”

“You understand it as a water pathway.”

On a watershed map, the rain garden is a small dot.

But on site, it is somebody's garden.

There is a height of grass.

There is maintenance work.

There is anxiety on rainy days.

There are also the eyes of the neighborhood.

“If we want to increase rain gardens, an explanation of water alone is not enough.”

“An explanation of everyday life is also needed.”

“Again, ‘alone is not enough.’”

“Yes.”

## Section 4 — Seeing “water will come” first at the retention basin

Next, we came to the downstream retention basin.

A sunny-day grassland.

People walking dogs.

A bench.

A levee in the distance.

An information board.

“There is no water here today.”

“That is why we can come and see it.”

A person living near the retention basin looked at the grassland and said:

“Will water really enter here?”

“How far will it come?”

“Will it come near our house?”

“Who cleans up after the water enters?”

“That feels much more serious than asking in the meeting room.”

“Because you can see the height of the ground.”

The officer pointed to the information board.

“This line is the expected water range.”

“This path will be closed before water enters.”

“Cleanup after the water recedes will be arranged from the shared watershed fund.”

“We will not leave it only to nearby residents.”

“The phrase ‘a place that receives water’ sounds clean when it is just words.”

“At the site, it is ‘a place where water comes.’”

“That is why we show the range where it comes and what happens afterward first.”

“So anxiety is not left only to imagination.”

Nearby residents walked through the grassland.

They looked at the bench locations.

They looked at the evacuation routes.

They also looked at how the area is used as a park when there is no water.

They checked the recovery procedure after water enters.

“The retention basin becomes land that has both ordinary-time and emergency-time roles, not just land for endurance.”

“If that happens, it becomes a little easier to accept.”

“Even then, fear does not become zero.”

“No.”

“But the shape of the fear becomes a little visible.”

“If it is visible, people can talk about it.”

Consensus around a retention basin does not begin with the water-level line in the documents alone.

Water will come to this ground.

How far it will come.

When it will be closed.

Who will restore it.

How it will be used in ordinary times.

By seeing those things, people can finally talk.

## Section 5 — Placing the chart and intuition side by side at the port

The last place we came to was the fishing port.

A morning port.

Ice.

Nets.

Boats.

A water-temperature chart.

A tide table.

“If we held a long briefing here, people would get angry, right?”

“We talk briefly, and in a way that can be used.”

An older fisher and a younger fisher stood in front of the water-temperature chart.

“This red area is wider than yesterday.”

“But if the tide runs this way, the fish may still be drawn over here.”

“Last year around this time, we missed offshore.”

“The chart and intuition are not fighting.”

“They are looking at the same sea from different angles.”

The younger fisher pointed to the chart.

The older fisher added talk about wind and tide.

The port officer recorded that conversation in a short memo.

The market staff thought about what fish might come in.

The processing staff adjusted ice and storage preparation a little.

“The data is not overwriting experience. It is entering the conversation of experience.”

“That is consensus at the port.”

“Today, they do not decide only by the color of the chart.”

“But they also do not pretend they did not see the chart.”

“They look at both, and move a little earlier.”

“That is good.”

“Auxiliary wheels become trusted only when they are used on site.”

A water-temperature chart is not enough merely by being correct.

It has to arrive in time for the port morning.

It has to sit beside experience.

It has to become a material for judgment, not an obstacle to judgment.

Only then does it become an auxiliary wheel for the port.

“The place where chart and intuition stand side by side is not the meeting room either.”

“It is the port before going out to sea.”
