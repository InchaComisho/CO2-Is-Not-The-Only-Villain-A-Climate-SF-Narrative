# Part 7 Episode 13 — After the rain, who cleans up the mud?

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 12, the unfinished rain garden and the temporary water escape route received real heavy rain.

The shallow hollow in the vacant lot did not swallow all the water.

Leaves clogged the gutter, and unexpected water from the ruts in the parking lot headed toward the crosswalk.

Even so, the temporary water route moved some of the water away and created time for the children to move to a safer place.

But once the rain stops, everything is not over.

Mud remains in the vacant lot.

Trash catches in the planted strip.

Leaves return to the gutter.

The people in the place that received the rain are left with fatigue and anxiety.

In Episode 13, we look at what the town is left with after the rain.

And the protagonist and the AI learn that whether an auxiliary wheel truly continues is decided not only by whether it worked during the rain, but also by who cleans up afterward, and whose voice is included in the next design.

---

“It’s quiet.”

The next morning.

Standing at the low intersection, yesterday’s rain felt almost like a lie.

The sky was bright.

The water on the road had receded.

The crosswalk was visible again.

Cars were passing as usual.

But the town had not returned to how it was before.

Brown water remained in the shallow hollow of the vacant lot.

A plastic bag, branches, and fallen leaves were caught in the planted strip.

More mud than yesterday had gathered on the gutter grating.

Thin lines of sand remained in the parking lot ruts.

“The rain stopped,” I said.

“Most of the water has receded too.”

“But the auxiliary wheel’s work is not over.”

The AI opened the white file.

On yesterday’s page, blue writing and red writing were lined up.

It delayed the time before water reached the crosswalk.

The escape route toward the planted strip helped.

Leaves clogged the gutter grating.

There was water that the vacant lot alone could not receive.

Management, cleaning, contact points, and costs were still undecided.

“Today is about that last line.”

“Who cleans it up?”

“Part 7 Episode 13. The real work after the rain begins.”

## Section 1 — Mud does not attend meetings

At nine in the morning.

Fewer people had gathered in front of the vacant lot than yesterday.

The disaster-prevention officer.

The landscaping worker.

The owner of the vacant lot.

The bakery worker.

Someone from the shopping street.

A person from the distribution center.

The schoolteacher.

The children who had watched during yesterday’s rain were not there.

“They are not here today?” I asked.

The teacher smiled a little.

“Today is not Sunday.”

“Oh, right.”

The AI glowed faintly.

“The protagonist has forgotten the day of the week two days in a row.”

“You do not need to record that.”

The owner of the vacant lot was looking at the water left in the hollow.

“How long will this remain?”

The landscaping worker checked the soil.

“It should drain by around noon today. But mud and fine trash will remain.”

“Then who removes it?”

No one answered immediately.

During yesterday’s rain, everyone moved.

They moved people away from the crosswalk.

They checked the gutter grating.

They shaved down the temporary soil line and guided water into the planted strip.

But today.

There was no rain.

There was no emergency.

That was exactly why no one had been assigned to act.

“Yesterday, everyone moved because they felt it was necessary,” I said.

“But mud does not shout, ‘This is an emergency.’”

“Mud does not attend meetings either.”

“That is why the person who notices it tends to end up carrying it.”

The bakery worker said:

“That sounds like it will become the vacant-lot owner’s job.”

The owner answered immediately.

“If it is only me, I will not lend the place next time.”

The words were strong.

But he was not shouting.

He was simply tired.

“That is natural,” the disaster-prevention officer said.

“Yesterday’s record also says that the exits for management and responsibility are not yet decided.”

“If you write it, does someone come?”

“We will make it so they come.”

“Today?”

The officer fell silent for a moment.

“Today as well.”

“Then don’t just look. Record the cleanup too.”

The owner pointed at the hollow.

“If this much is part of the rain garden, then what comes after this is part of the rain garden too.”

Without thinking, I opened the white file.

He was right.

A rain garden is not a rain garden only at the moment it receives water.

It includes what remains after the water recedes.

## Section 2 — Goodwill is fast. But goodwill alone does not continue

A person from the shopping street parked a small truck.

In the bed were trash bags.

Brooms.

Rakes.

Work gloves.

A small shovel.

Buckets.

“I brought what we had in the storehouse.”

“That helps.”

The bakery worker also brought a box of cold tea.

The person from the distribution center was wearing boots.

“We can spare two people until noon.”

The schoolteacher sent a message through the school district contact network: “Please be careful near the intersection. Mud remains there.”

“Amazing,” I said.

“The town is moving.”

“Because there are people who saw yesterday’s rain.”

The owner of the vacant lot came out too, holding an old rake.

“This is my land. I can at least look at the place where water remains.”

“Thank you.”

“But I cannot do this every time.”

“Yes.”

The landscaping worker looked at the trash in the planted strip and said:

“For now, everyone’s goodwill can clean it up.”

“But if the next rain brings the same thing?”

“The same people may not come again.”

The AI displayed a line on the screen.

Goodwill is strong at the start. Systems are strong at continuing.

“That is a good line.”

“But until we create the system, we cannot leave today’s cleanup undone.”

I put on work gloves.

“Today, we begin with goodwill. But before we finish, we turn it into a system.”

“How?” the bakery worker asked.

“By recording everything as we clean.”

Who did what.

Where mud remained.

How many trash bags were needed.

Where the tools came from.

How long it took.

Who had trouble.

“Next time, we will not leave it as ‘someone will somehow do it.’”

“An auxiliary wheel is assembled again after cleanup.”

“Again after the rain?”

“After the rain is the test of whether it continues.”

Picking up mud.

Gathering leaves.

Checking the gutter.

It was plain work.

But it was just as important as yesterday’s rain in deciding the shape of the auxiliary wheel.

## Section 3 — The owner of the vacant lot was looking beyond the mud

The work began.

They gathered trash from the planted strip.

They slowly moved mud from the gutter grating with a long stick.

They gathered sand that had run to the edge of the road.

They picked up branches left around the shallow hollow in the vacant lot.

“This bag is already full,” someone from the shopping street said.

“There is a second bag.”

“Where does the trash go?”

“For now, to the shopping street collection place—”

“Wait.”

The owner of the vacant lot stopped his hands.

“Do not decide that on your own either.”

Everyone stopped.

“What do you mean?” the disaster-prevention officer asked.

“Today, the shopping street is letting us use its collection place. That helps.”

The owner pointed at the planted strip.

“But after the next rain, the same kind of trash may appear again. Is it trash from the vacant lot, or trash that flowed in from the road? Whose trash will it be treated as?”

I lost my words.

He was right.

It was wrong to treat trash that had washed into the vacant lot as “the owner’s trash.”

But it was also difficult to separate whether every piece came from the road or from someone’s house.

“Yesterday’s rain brought the town’s water here,” the owner said.

“Then the trash is the town’s problem too, isn’t it?”

The person from the distribution center nodded slowly.

“That is exactly right.”

The bakery worker also spoke.

“It will not continue if we tell the vacant-lot owner, ‘Please receive the water and the trash too.’”

The disaster-prevention officer wrote a new heading in the file.

Whose trash is the trash that washes into the rain garden?

“Can we answer that?” I asked.

“Not completely today,” the officer said.

“But at the very least, we can decide not to close it as the owner’s problem.”

“That alone makes a difference.”

The owner gripped the rake again.

“Then today, we do it together.”

“To share a difficulty also means giving a name to the visible burden.”

“You say difficult things again.”

“This exchange is very important.”

“If it is the town’s water, it is also the town’s trash.”

“At least an entrance for treating it that way has been created.”

## Section 4 — A small cleanup team and an invisible budget

Before noon.

There were four trash bags.

Branches.

Fallen leaves.

Plastic.

Paper stained with mud.

A small piece of foam whose origin no one knew.

The gutter grating was flowing somewhat better.

The edge of the crosswalk had also become easier to walk through.

“Are we done?” the person from the distribution center asked.

The landscaping worker shook his head.

“Today’s cleanup is mostly done.”

“That wording is scary.”

“But the things we need to do before the next rain have increased.”

Records of the work lined up in the white file.

Participants: eight people.

Work time: about two and a half hours.

Trash bags: four.

Necessary tools: rakes, brooms, boots, work gloves, a long stick, bags.

Places needing attention: gutter grating, parking lot ruts, edge of the planted strip, outlet of the vacant lot.

Do not leave cleaning, management, and trash disposal only to the owner of the vacant lot.

“What we need next is not only people, is it?” the schoolteacher said.

“Bags too. Tools too. A collection destination too.”

“And costs,” said the bakery worker.

The atmosphere changed slightly.

Costs.

A word that had appeared with mist too.

Water bills.

Tanks.

Water supply.

Inspections.

It was the same with the rain garden.

“Who buys the tools?”

“What about trash bags?”

“What will it cost to fix the planted strip?”

“What will it take to make the temporary soil line safer?”

“How do we make sure a burden does not remain with the vacant-lot owner?”

The disaster-prevention officer spoke slowly.

“We need a small budget.”

“It is not only the large construction cost,” I said.

“Bags to collect mud. A board with contact information. Time for people to come check after rain.”

“Auxiliary wheels run on invisible budgets.”

“Invisible budgets?”

“Large equipment budgets are easier to write down. But the costs for bags, tools, inspections, contacts, cleaning, and records are often postponed.”

“But without them, it cannot continue.”

“That’s right.”

The disaster-prevention officer wrote at the edge of the file:

—Create a small budget not only for design, but also for maintenance, cleaning, communication, and recordkeeping.

“We have come back to Part 7 Episode 2.”

“The final boss called the budget year is also in the mud.”

“So it is still the final boss.”

## Section 5 — The meeting after cleanup can be short

In the afternoon.

We returned to the community room.

Everyone had a little mud on them.

Their shoes, hands, and bodies were tired.

“A long meeting is impossible,” I said.

“Impossible,” everyone nodded.

“Then let’s make it ten minutes.”

The disaster-prevention officer looked at the clock.

“There are three things to decide today.”

First.

Who will first look at the site after the next rain?

“The disaster-prevention office alone cannot do it.”

“The vacant-lot owner alone cannot do it either.”

“Let us make a form where the shopping street, the distribution center, and nearby residents each receive contact.”

“Who sends the contact?”

“We decide a rain threshold, and the disaster-prevention office sends it.”

Second.

Whose problem will the trash and mud be treated as?

“Do not make it only the vacant-lot owner’s problem.”

“Treat it as something that flowed in from roads and the town, and respond jointly.”

“What about the collection destination?”

“We will coordinate with the municipal cleaning department before next time.”

Third.

What must go into the next design?

The landscaping worker said:

“Make the outlet from the vacant lot safer.”

“Adjust the water route in the planted strip.”

“Discuss the effect of the parking lot ruts with the road side as well.”

“Add something to make leaves gather less easily in front of the gutter grating.”

“Do not leave the grass and trash management plan outside the design drawing.”

“That is important,” said the vacant-lot owner.

“Do not draw only water on the plan. Draw the people who will clean up too.”

The room went quiet.

I wrote those words exactly as they were.

—Do not draw only water on the plan. Draw the people who will clean up too.

The AI displayed a small line on the screen.

“That is the central line of Part 7 Episode 13.”

“We will keep it exactly.”

“Keep it exactly.”

The short meeting ended there.

Because everyone was tired, they did not talk for long.

But they decided the one thing that had to be decided that day, or else the burden would return to one person again.

That was the meeting after the rain.

## Section 6 — After the mud, the town’s roles remain

By evening.

The water in the hollow of the vacant lot had decreased a lot since morning.

The planted strip was a little cleaner.

The gutter grating had regained its flow.

People walking along the road passed by as if they did not know yesterday’s rain.

“It became cleaner,” I said.

“Not completely.”

“I know.”

“But we can see the next step more clearly than yesterday.”

I closed the white file.

It already contained the station-front mist record.

The water ran out quickly.

The wind changed.

The bakery front was avoided.

The rain garden record had also grown.

Water poured from a hose unexpectedly turned toward the parking lot.

Real rain brought water close to the crosswalk.

The vacant lot created a little time.

After the rain, mud and trash remained.

“An auxiliary wheel was not just equipment that receives rain,” I said.

“Then what was it?”

“A system so that after the rain, the town does not leave one person behind.”

The AI paused briefly.

“That, too, is an auxiliary wheel.”

“The water outlet. The management outlet. The responsibility outlet.”

“And the outlet for calling help.”

I wrote the final line.

—The mud left by the rain did not show only the town’s weakness. It became a line for the town to decide together who will clean, who will support, and what to fix next.

“Part 7 Episode 13 ends here.”

“Next?”

“The rain garden moves from temporary lines toward a real design.”

“A chapter where the plan draws not only water, but also the people who clean up.”

“That sounds difficult.”

“That is why today’s mud will help.”

In the distance, the evening wind shook the leaves in the planted strip.

The rain was no longer falling.

But the town’s auxiliary wheel had begun turning a little closer to the ground than yesterday.

Part 7 Episode 13 ends here.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading Part 7 Episode 13.

This episode depicted the mud, trash, remaining water, fatigue, and burden of management left after heavy rain.

A rain garden or vacant lot that receives water is not enough if it only works during the rain.

Who checks the condition after the rain recedes?

Who cleans up the trash and mud?

Where do tools and collection costs come from?

How do we keep the burden from concentrating on the owner of the vacant lot?

Without this “after the rain” design, an auxiliary wheel may operate once, but it will not continue.

The main points of this episode are five.

### 1. Emergency cooperation and continuing management must be treated separately

During heavy rain, many people may move naturally when they see danger.

But the next day, plain work remains: mud, trash, grass, and checking the gutter.

If that work is left only to the person who notices it or the landowner, the burden becomes uneven, and cooperation next time becomes harder to obtain.

Mud does not shout, “This is an emergency.”

That is why a system is needed so that only the person who sees it does not have to carry it.

### 2. Goodwill matters, but goodwill alone cannot keep auxiliary wheels going

The shopping street bringing tools, the bakery bringing drinks, and people from the distribution center helping with the work are powerful forms of support.

But the same people may not be able to move after the next rain.

The contact threshold, first-response roles, trash collection destination, tool storage, and cost sharing must be decided and turned into a system that supports goodwill.

Goodwill is strong at the start.

Systems are strong at continuing.

That difference matters.

### 3. Trash that flows in is not only the responsibility of the landowner

It is unfair if trash flowing in from roads and surrounding areas is handled only by the owner of the rain garden or vacant lot.

If a place receives water for the town, its trash and mud also need to be treated as the town’s problem.

“Whose trash is the trash that washes into the rain garden?”

This question is important for thinking about the benefits and burdens of auxiliary wheels within the same range.

### 4. Maintenance requires an “invisible budget”

Not only construction costs, but also small costs and time are needed: trash bags, work gloves, rakes, cleaning, inspection, communication, records, and collection.

If these budgets are not included in the design from the beginning, the equipment can be built, but operation will not continue.

Auxiliary wheels run on invisible budgets.

In Part 7 Episode 13, we saw that difficult-to-see part through the mud left after rain.

### 5. On the plan, draw not only water, but also the people who clean up

The center of Part 7 Episode 13 is not ending the rain garden plan with only the flow of water drawn on it.

Who checks the site after rain?

Who sends the contact?

Who cleans?

Who supports the cost?

All of that is part of the auxiliary wheel design.

An auxiliary wheel is a structure that receives rainwater, and at the same time, a structure that prevents one person from being left behind after the rain.

Do not draw only water on the plan.

Draw the people who will clean up too.

That sentence is the center of Part 7 Episode 13.

In the next episode, Part 7 Episode 14, the town will carry the records of mud and trash and try to turn the temporary rain garden into a real design.

The depth that receives water.

The outlet for overflow.

The role of the planted strip.

The responsibility for maintenance.

A small budget.

And above all, whether the plan can write not only the water, but also people’s roles.

Part 7 now moves toward shaping the auxiliary wheel as a town system.
