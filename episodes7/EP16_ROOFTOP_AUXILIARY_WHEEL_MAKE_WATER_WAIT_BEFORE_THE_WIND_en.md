# Part 7 Episode 16 — The rooftop auxiliary wheel: make water wait before the wind arrives

## Foreword

In Part 7 Episode 15, we went to a town with no vacant lots.

The station front was filled with buildings, the roads were narrow, there were few wide planting strips, and there was no spare space to dig a rain garden.

But the protagonist and the AI did not decide, “If there is no vacant lot, we cannot place auxiliary wheels.”

Roofs.

Gutters.

Eaves.

Partner shops.

Bus stops.

Places where people can rest briefly.

People who inspect before and after rain.

By connecting the small margins scattered through the town, they searched for different auxiliary wheels to prepare for heat and rain.

In Episode 16, the first of those begins: a rainwater trial using the roof of a supermarket.

Let the water falling on the roof wait for a little while instead of immediately sending it downward.

One tank is small.

It is not large enough to stop flooding in the town.

Even so, it might slightly delay rainwater from concentrating in the gutters at the same time as water from the roads.

However, roofs are closer to the wind than the ground is.

Strong wind is dangerous.

A clogged drain is dangerous.

Storing too much water is dangerous.

And what happens on the roof is hard to see from below.

The protagonist and the AI try to turn the first auxiliary wheel on top of this “invisible vacant lot.”

---

“It’s high.”

I said that the moment I stepped out onto the supermarket roof.

The wind was strong.

On the ground, it had only felt a little hot.

But on the roof, the wind wrapped around the building corners and suddenly pushed at my back.

“The rooftop auxiliary wheel has a big attitude from day one.”

“The wind did not attend the meetings on the ground.”

“That again?”

New equipment had been placed on the roof.

A small rainwater tank.

A pipe branched from the gutter.

An overflow outlet to let water escape when too much collected.

A transparent tube that showed the water level.

An inspection cover near the drain.

And a caution area marked with yellow lines.

“It is smaller than I expected,” the schoolteacher said.

“Can this really change anything?”

The store manager lightly tapped the tank.

“It will not change the water of the whole town,” I said.

“But it may prevent part of the roof water from flowing downward immediately during the strongest part of the rain.”

The AI displayed two lines.

A roof with nothing.

Rain falls.

Roof water gathers into the gutter.

It enters the roadside drains at the same time as water from the road.

The burden on low places increases.

A roof with an auxiliary wheel.

Rain falls.

Part of the roof water enters the tank.

After the rain peak passes a little, it flows out slowly.

The amount of water gathering into the drain at the same time is reduced slightly.

“It doesn’t erase the water,” said the distribution-center worker.

“It doesn’t.”

“It shifts the timing.”

“Exactly.”

“An auxiliary wheel gives water a place to wait for a little while.”

The manager looked toward the edge of the roof.

“But what if it stores too much?”

“That is the most important thing to check today.”

I opened the white file.

A new heading had been written on the cover.

Rooftop / invisible vacant lot — trial record.

“Part 7 Episode 16. Let’s make water wait on the roof.”

## Section 1 — Invisible vacant lots have invisible responsibility

A responsibility chart had been posted beside the tank.

Checking the drain before rain.

The supermarket facilities staff.

Checking the water level.

Shared between the management company and the store.

Contact during strong rain.

The disaster-prevention officer.

Decision to stop in abnormal conditions.

The management company.

Inspection records after rain.

Checked by the store, the management company, and disaster prevention.

“This is more detailed from the start than the previous town,” I said.

The manager gave a wry smile.

“With a vacant lot, someone can go look and understand. But the roof cannot be seen from below.”

“So it is easy to forget.”

“When it rains, the store is busy with customers. No one may go up to the roof.”

The distribution-center worker said:

“It’s like a truck bed. If you cannot see the cargo, you forget what you loaded.”

“That example is easy to understand.”

The AI marked red points on the rooftop drawing.

The drain.

The overflow outlet.

The water-level tube.

The no-entry line.

The inspection cover.

“Invisible equipment needs a promise that someone will go look.”

“A promise to go look.”

“If you think equipment will work on its own just because it has been placed there, the auxiliary wheel stops.”

I looked at the responsibility chart.

“In this town, we do not leave it to one person.”

“Not only the manager.”

“Not only the management company.”

“Not only disaster prevention.”

The manager said:

“But if everyone is supposed to look, doesn’t that mean no one looks?”

For a moment, the air stopped.

“…That can happen,” I answered.

“Then not ‘everyone looks,’ but ‘the first person who checks, the next person who informs, and the last person who records.’ Let’s separate those.”

The AI drew three arrows.

The person who checks.

The person who informs.

The person who fixes.

“Do not pile the roles too heavily,” the schoolteacher said.

“Right.”

“But do not create gaps between the roles.”

“Auxiliary wheels fall more easily into holes of responsibility than holes in the water path.”

“Lots of memorable lines today.”

“Because rooftops are close to the sky.”

“That sounds like a reason and not quite like one.”

“But it is easy to remember.”

The rooftop auxiliary wheel cannot be seen from below.

That is exactly why someone has to go look.

Someone has to inform.

Someone has to record.

Not one person alone, but not everyone vaguely responsible either.

Invisible vacant lots need invisible responsibility to be made visible.

## Section 2 — Before rain falls, the drain is already nearly clogged

“All right, we will inspect it.”

The supermarket facilities staff opened the drain cover.

Inside was a small amount of mud.

Sand.

Tiny leaves.

A paper scrap whose origin no one knew.

“It hasn’t even rained yet, right?” the schoolteacher said.

“That is why we look before it rains,” the facilities staff replied.

“It is dangerous to come up to the roof after rain becomes strong.”

The landscaping worker looked around the drain.

“Here, the wind wraps around the roof corner. Leaves may collect easily.”

“It is like the previous town’s roadside gutter,” I said.

“On the ground, fallen leaves clogged the grate.”

“On the roof, the wind carries trash.”

“Trash sits down in front of the water entrance.”

The manager said:

“So do we clean before every rain?”

“It does not have to be a major cleaning every time,” the facilities staff answered.

“But before strong rain is expected, we check it. After rain, we also check whether anything clogged.”

“Who does that?”

The manager looked at me.

I pointed to the responsibility chart.

“At first, facilities staff check. If they notice anything abnormal, they tell the management company. If the situation is dangerous, they also contact disaster prevention.”

“And the cleaning cost?”

“We record that too.”

The first red lines were written in the white file.

—Sand, leaves, and paper scraps also collect around rooftop drains.
—A structure for inspection before rain is necessary.
—As with ground-level gutters, do not postpone the cost of drain management.

“We already have red lines, before the water has even been stored,” said someone from the shopping association in a tone that sounded like something the bakery worker would have said.

“It is better to notice now than after digging and failing.”

“Auxiliary wheels can add to their manual even before they start moving.”

“In invisible places, red lines have to come first.”

“Yes.”

## Section 3 — The first rain is so quiet that it makes you uneasy

Afternoon.

The sky darkened a little.

“Is it coming?” I asked.

“A small rain cloud is approaching.”

“So not a typhoon.”

“Today is a trial. We do not force the roof to do too much.”

The first rain was quiet.

Drop.

Drop.

Drop.

Water dots multiplied across the flat roof.

The thin lines of water heading toward the gutter slowly grew thicker.

From the branched pipe, water began entering the tank.

“It’s going in.”

The manager looked at the transparent water-level tube.

The water level rose slowly.

One mark.

Two marks.

Three marks.

“On the ground, we could see water entering the rain garden,” I said.

“On the roof, it is the same.”

“Even when the place changes, water goes to the lower point.”

“But today, it is actually waiting.”

“Because there is a tank,” the schoolteacher said.

“For now.”

The landscaping worker looked at the sky.

“The wind is rising.”

“Wind again.”

Something went clink at the edge of the roof.

A light paper scrap rolled across the floor.

“Is the drain all right?”

“We checked it earlier, so it should be all right for now.”

“Should be is a dangerous phrase.”

“But today’s purpose is to confirm that ‘should be.’”

The water level reached four marks.

The tank quietly received water.

“Is it working?” the manager asked.

I did not answer.

In an earlier kind of story, this might be where applause happens.

But in Part 7, the unexpected arrives after the applause.

The AI screen turned yellow for just a moment.

“Wind direction changing.”

## Section 4 — On the roof, the wind changes the plan

The rain grew a little stronger.

Wind slipped past the side of the tank.

Tiny leaves came flying in from the edge of the roof.

“The drain!”

The facilities staff hurried forward.

“Wait, inside the caution line is dangerous!”

The disaster-prevention officer shouted.

Near the drain, the wet roof surface was slippery.

The wind was also growing stronger.

“We should not clean it now,” I said.

“But what if it clogs?”

The AI displayed the water level.

The tank was at five marks.

There was still room before the overflow outlet.

“This is not an emergency clog. Prioritize human safety.”

“On the ground, we moved gutter leaves with a long stick,” the distribution-center worker said.

“This is a rooftop. We cannot approach recklessly.”

“So there is nothing we can do from below?” the facilities staff said, frustrated.

“There is.”

I looked at the rooftop drawing.

“What about the overflow outlet?”

“If the tank fills, it sends water back into the original drainpipe through that route.”

“Then we do not need to stop the water.”

“But if leaves get into the tank?”

“We check after the rain weakens.”

“To protect an auxiliary wheel, people do not have to become dangerous auxiliary wheels themselves.”

“I will remember that.”

The water level reached six marks.

Rain struck the roof.

The wind grew slightly stronger.

But the tank did not overflow.

Water entered through the branched pipe, and at a certain level, it began slowly escaping through the overflow route.

“The water is escaping.”

“As planned,” the facilities staff said.

“We do not store everything. We return it before it becomes dangerous.”

“It is the same as a rain garden,” I said.

“Make an exit for the water.”

“Rooftops also need an exit before overflow becomes dangerous.”

Looking at the wet roof, the schoolteacher said:

“Auxiliary wheels do not overwork themselves, do they?”

“Because if they overwork, they break.”

“People and equipment are the same.”

At that line, the place grew a little quiet.

Only the sound of rain remained on the roof.

## Section 5 — On the ground, invisible water had created time

When the rain weakened, we went back down to the ground.

Behind the supermarket.

Water was flowing through the gutter.

The road was wet.

But the place where the manager had said rain usually gathered all at once looked a little calmer than usual.

“Is it really different?” someone from the shopping association asked.

The AI displayed simple records.

The time when part of the roof rainwater entered the tank.

The time when it slowly flowed through the overflow outlet.

Changes in the water entering the gutter.

The timing overlap with road water.

“With today’s small rain, we cannot determine the effect conclusively.”

“Honest as always.”

“But we confirmed that roof water did not all flow downward at once.”

“Is that enough?”

“For today’s trial, yes,” I said.

“What happens in heavy rain? Whether the tank capacity is enough. What happens if the drain clogs. There are still many things we do not know.”

The manager nodded.

“But the roof water is no longer water that has no choice but to go straight down.”

“That’s right.”

“It became water that can wait a little.”

“To give water time is also to give time to the ground.”

“Another good line.”

“Today, the good lines are all coming from the roof,” the schoolteacher said with a laugh.

It was a small rain.

It had not greatly changed the town’s water flow.

Even so, roof water no longer simply fell immediately.

It waited a little.

It was delayed a little.

Its timing shifted a little.

That small shift might create a margin for the gutter and sidewalk on the ground.

## Section 6 — Red lines on the roof protect people on the ground

Evening.

A new page had been added to the white file in the community room.

Rooftop / invisible vacant lot — first trial.

Blue lines.

Even with small rain, part of the roof water could be temporarily received by the tank.

The overflow outlet worked as an exit that prevented the tank from holding too much water at once.

The visible water-level tube helped on-site judgment.

It helped to divide roles among facilities staff, the store, the management company, and disaster prevention.

On the ground, time was created in which roof water did not immediately flow downward.

Red lines.

Even before rain, sand, leaves, and paper scraps had collected around the drain.

When wind is strong, approaching the drain area on the roof is dangerous.

Rather than forcing inspection or cleaning during rain, a sequence for checking before and after rain is necessary.

Do not treat the result of a light rain as proof that the system will work in heavy rain or a typhoon.

Before the next rain, recheck the tank, overflow outlet, drain, and caution line.

Looking at the red lines, the manager asked:

“Is this a failure record?”

“Some of it is a list of issues,” I answered.

“But it is also a record that stops the dangerous idea that placing equipment on the roof means everything is safe.”

The disaster-prevention officer said:

“We can see what has to be done before the next typhoon.”

“Look before rain. Do not take risks during rain. Record after rain.”

“And do not leave it all to the store.”

The manager smiled a little.

“In that case, maybe there is meaning in lending the rooftop vacant lot.”

“Lending?”

“Well, it is our store’s roof.”

“Auxiliary wheels do not only borrow places to stand.”

“They also borrow trust,” the AI said.

“Can we return it?”

“By cleaning up and keeping records.”

I wrote the final line.

—The rooftop auxiliary wheel is not only for storing water. It turns so people do not take risks in strong wind, so someone goes to look before and after rain, and so a little time is returned to people on the ground.

“Part 7 Episode 16 ends here.”

“Next?”

“We connect the rooftop auxiliary wheel to the station-front heat auxiliary wheels.”

“Not only water. Heat too.”

“We increase small escape places throughout the town.”

“And the next enemy?”

“Heat remains even after rain stops.”

The evening station front was humid, even after the rain.

On the roof, a little water still remained.

But that water was no longer only water rushing down to the ground.

I added one last line.

—To protect an auxiliary wheel, people must not become dangerous auxiliary wheels themselves.

Part 7 Episode 16 ends here.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading Part 7 Episode 16.

In this episode, a town with no vacant lots used a supermarket roof as an “invisible vacant lot” and ran a trial of an auxiliary wheel that temporarily stored part of the rainwater falling on the roof.

A rooftop rainwater tank is not equipment that stops heavy rain across the whole town.

However, by making water falling on the roof wait a little instead of sending it downward during the rain peak, it may reduce the amount of water that concentrates in drains at the same time as water from roads and parking lots.

The main points of this episode are five.

### 1. A rooftop can become an “invisible margin” in a town with few vacant lots

Even when there is no space on the ground for a rain garden, roofs, gutters, rainwater tanks, and shallow rooftop planting can be combined to slightly delay the flow of rainwater.

Water cannot be erased.

Even so, the timing when water concentrates in sewers and gutters can be shifted.

A rooftop is also an “invisible vacant lot” left in a town with no ground-level margin.

### 2. A tank always needs an exit for water

If water is only stored in a tank, exceeding capacity becomes dangerous.

In this episode, the overflow outlet was an exit for returning water slowly to the original drainage route before the tank became too full.

As with a ground-level rain garden, rooftop equipment needs both “a place to receive water” and “a route for overflow to escape.”

Do not store everything; let water escape before it becomes dangerous.

That too is part of auxiliary-wheel design.

### 3. Because rooftops are hard to see, roles and safety boundaries are important

Even before rain, sand, leaves, and paper scraps had collected around the drain.

However, going onto a roof to clean during strong wind or heavy rain is dangerous.

The person who checks before rain.

The person who reports abnormalities.

The person who contacts the management company.

The person who records after rain.

Those roles have to be separated so the system does not become “someone will probably look.”

Invisible equipment needs a promise that someone will go look.

### 4. People must not take on danger in order to protect an auxiliary wheel

Even if a drain clog is worrying, no one should recklessly approach a caution area during strong wind.

Auxiliary wheels are structures that support safety.

If people must perform dangerous work to protect the equipment, the design and operation sequence need to be revised.

To protect an auxiliary wheel, people must not become dangerous auxiliary wheels themselves.

This is one of the important lines of this episode.

### 5. Success in light rain is not a guarantee for heavy rain or typhoons

The first small rain confirmed that roof water entered the tank and could escape through the overflow outlet.

But that does not prove that the system will work sufficiently in heavy rain or a typhoon.

Capacity.

Drain clogging.

Wind.

Inspection timing.

Surrounding water flow.

Those need to be recorded through future rains so the auxiliary wheel can become a little stronger.

Part 7 Episode 16 portrayed the rooftop auxiliary wheel not as a device that merely stores water, but as a structure for understanding wind and safety limits, assigning human roles, and returning a little time to people on the ground.

The rooftop auxiliary wheel is not only for storing water.

It turns so people do not take risks in strong wind, so someone goes to look before and after rain, and so a little time is returned to people on the ground.

This is the center of Part 7 Episode 16.

In Part 7 Episode 17, we return to the humid station front after rain.

Can the town connect the rooftop water auxiliary wheel with bus stops, eaves, partner shops, and water stations, creating small places to escape the heat throughout the town?

After the water auxiliary wheel comes the heat auxiliary wheel.

The town begins to connect the invisible roof above with the ground beneath people’s feet.
