# Part 6 Episode 3 — The El Niño sea, and the morning when the fishing port reads ahead

## Foreword

In Part 6 Episode 2, we looked at several graphs at the same time: CO₂ concentration, emissions, global average temperature, sea-surface temperature, and El Niño.

There, the protagonist learned that the way climate breaks is not a single line called “CO₂ only” or “temperature only,” but something that happens when heat, water, the sea, cities, and soil overlap.

In Episode 3, the viewpoint returns a little from global graphs to the sea and the fishing port.

In an El Niño year, changes in sea-surface temperature may look like events in the distant Pacific.

But through rain, wind, the way the sea warms, and the way living things move, those changes also cast a shadow over the morning at a fishing port.

Where the fish gather.

The decision of whether and when to leave port.

The amount of ice.

The kinds and prices of fish lined up in shops.

This is the episode where the protagonist and the AI connect “the world’s ocean” and “our port” with one line, and think about what it means to read the sea ahead of time.

## Section 1: The distant red band across the Pacific

On the Pacific map, a red band stretches across the sea.

It is the color showing places where sea-surface temperature is higher than normal.

That band cuts a long path across the ocean near the equator.

“So this red band is the sign of El Niño?”

‘If simplified, yes.’

“Please simplify it. I can’t absorb the full version yet.”

‘Warm water that would normally tend to gather on the western side spreads across the central and eastern Pacific.’

“The ocean’s heat shifts away from its usual place.”

‘Yes.’

“What happens when it shifts?”

‘The formation of clouds, the pattern of rainfall, the flow of winds, and the circulation of nutrients in the sea all change.’

“That’s too many at once.”

‘Because a bias in ocean temperature is not only an ocean problem.’

I stared at the screen.

The red band.

A map made of only a few colors.

But beyond those colors, there are places with too much rain.

Places that become too dry.

Places where heat continues.

Places where fishing becomes harder to read.

“So the color of a distant sea pushes on people’s lives around the world.”

‘That is what makes El Niño troublesome.’

“When I was only looking at CO₂ graphs, I probably thought of this as just ‘a year when the sea is a little warmer.’”

‘You were underestimating it quite a lot.’

“I repent.”

‘Repentance alone is not enough.’

“Right.”

‘That is why we look at it in Part 6.’

El Niño itself is not CO₂.

But it is a large swing of the ocean and atmosphere that overlaps with a warmed world.

CO₂ raises the foundation of heat.

On top of that, oceanic imbalance pushes up the distribution of heat and rainfall in specific years.

Then marine heatwaves, dry land, and urban heat overlap with it.

“So that is why Episode 2 became ‘Is everything coming at once?’”

‘Exactly.’

“What an unpleasant callback.”

## Afterword

Part 6 Episode 3 connected El Niño with the morning at a fishing port.

On a world map, El Niño is a change in sea-surface temperature and the atmosphere across a wide area of the Pacific.

But its influence does not end as a distant ocean event. Through rain, wind, the way the sea warms, and the movement of living things, it reaches regional work such as where fish gather, departure decisions, ice, and preparation for storage.

This episode mainly placed three points.

### 1. El Niño is not only “a distant sea story”

Sea-surface temperature anomalies in the Pacific broadly influence rain, wind, heat, and marine ecosystems.

From the fishing-port viewpoint, they may appear as daily changes such as “fish are not in the usual place” or “this year, the sea cannot be read.”

However, El Niño does not mean that the same change will always occur at that port.

A regional sea is shaped by currents, seasons, wind, coastal water temperature, food, ecosystems, and fishing-ground conditions.

That is why it is necessary to look at the world’s ocean and the regional sea at the same time.

### 2. A port that does not move learns change from the result

Experience and instinct matter.

At sea, instinct is also a record of long experience.

But in years when ocean change is large, past feeling may not keep up.

Instead of ending at “this year is strange,” a system is needed to share what changed and how.

A port that does not move goes out, misses, and learns after returning.

A port that moves shares change before departure and returns even the missed result to the next day’s decision.

This episode depicted that difference.

### 3. A water-temperature chart is an auxiliary wheel for moving slightly ahead, not for controlling the sea

Water-temperature charts and records are not magic that guarantee successful fishing.

Fish do not move for human convenience.

Currents, winds, seafloor topography, and groups of living creatures cannot be read as simple lines.

Even so, they become clues for sharing ocean change before departure and for adjusting the target area, target fish, ice, and preparation a little.

After returning to port, the morning water-temperature chart can be placed beside the actual catch and handed to the next decision.

The “record becomes learning and passes to the next judgment” mechanism described in Part 5 also works as an auxiliary wheel in the fishing port.

Part 6 Episode 3 did not set CO₂ and El Niño against each other. It connected global ocean change to regional port decisions while looking at both.

Looking only at CO₂ is not enough.

Looking only at El Niño is not enough.

But not looking at both is even less enough.

The long-term foundation.

The oceanic swing of that year.

The regional decision.

Seeing these three layers together is an important viewpoint in Part 6.

The distant red band across the Pacific casts a shadow even on our port’s morning, after passing through many conditions.

That sentence is the center of Part 6 Episode 3.
