# Part 6 Episode 5 — The day the sea’s heat becomes rain, and the river jams all at once

In Part 6 Episode 4, we saw how the heat held by the sea overlaps with the city’s summer through wind, humidity, and nighttime temperature.

## Afterword

Part 6 Episode 5 depicted the flow of water in which “the sea’s heat becomes rain and heads through the watershed toward the river.”

However, this does not mean that sea heat directly becomes rain in a simple way.

Sea-surface temperature, atmospheric conditions, wind, fronts, low-pressure systems, terrain, and other factors can overlap and come to a region as strong rain.

After that rain falls from the sky, it flows through upstream slopes, midstream residential areas, and downstream rivers and retention basins.

The points depicted in this episode are mainly these three.

### 1. Rain comes from the sky, but the ground decides how it flows

A region cannot completely stop rain created by conditions in the sea and sky.

A town cannot choose the amount of rain itself.

But where fallen rain is received, and how quickly it is sent to the river, can be changed a little by the state of soil, gardens, roads, drains, and retention basins.

We cannot choose the rain that comes from the sky.

But we can slightly change the speed at which it flows across the ground.

This viewpoint becomes the starting point of Episode 5.

### 2. Watershed auxiliary wheels delay “water coming all at once” little by little

Sponge-like soil upstream slows the speed at which water runs down slopes a little.

Rain gardens midstream receive part of the water from roofs and roads once.

Retention basins downstream become places that can receive part of the river water.

None of them erase heavy rain.

None of them reduce flooding to zero.

Even so, by not sending everything to the river at the same timing, they become auxiliary wheels that slightly bend water peaks and concentrated burdens.

### 3. It is not “emissions reduction or watershed measures”; both are necessary

CO₂ emissions reduction is necessary to reduce the amount of heat the whole Earth stores.

Ocean and atmospheric observation is necessary to understand changes coming from far away.

At the same time, for heavy rain and flood risks that are already arriving, local auxiliary wheels such as soil, rain gardens, and retention basins are also necessary.

Rain gardens cannot save the world.

Sponge-like soil does not erase heavy rain.

Retention basins do not make floods zero.

However, they can slightly change the local burden of water that has already arrived.

If we mistake the scale, the value also becomes invisible.

In Part 6 Episode 5, we looked at the global-scale problem of heat and the regional-scale problem of water inside the same circulation.

Part 6 is the part that looks at the world’s red graphs while also placing small, concrete things that can be done in regions.

Next time, we will layer upstream, midstream, and downstream auxiliary wheels as “one watershed team,” and look at what small devices can change when they are connected.

Stopping the sea’s heat alone is not enough.

It is also necessary not to send the rain that has fallen to the places that break fastest.

That sentence is the center of Part 6 Episode 5.
