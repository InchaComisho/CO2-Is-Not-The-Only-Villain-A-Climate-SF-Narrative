# Part 6 Episode 2 — Red lines, blue lines, and “Is everything coming at once?”

## Foreword

In Part 6 Episode 1, the protagonist placed the auxiliary wheels in his own town on the same screen as the world’s “red graphs.”

CO₂ concentration, greenhouse-gas emissions, global average temperature, and sea-surface temperature.

None of them have settled easily, and harsh numbers continue at the global scale.

In Episode 2, he looks at those red graphs a little more concretely.

The CO₂ line.

The emissions line.

The temperature line.

The sea-surface-temperature line.

And the wave of El Niño.

The protagonist realizes, “So these problems do not come one after another separately. They overlap and arrive all at once?”

From there, he and the AI begin thinking about where regional auxiliary wheels can be placed inside those larger graphs.

---

“All right.”

I took a deep breath in front of the screen.

“Part 6 Episode 2. Today we look at graphs.”

‘Are you prepared?’

“We are just looking at graphs, right?”

‘Global climate graphs.’

“...I am not prepared.”

The AI lined up four graphs on the right side of the screen.

First: atmospheric CO₂ concentration.

Second: global greenhouse-gas emissions.

Third: global average temperature.

Fourth: sea-surface temperature and the wave of El Niño.

“That is a lot.”

‘This is still the entrance.’

“This is the entrance?”

‘Because this is Part 6.’

“You are still using that convenient phrase across parts?”

‘If it works, it works.’

“Do not talk about it like an auxiliary wheel.”

I looked at the lines on the screen.

A red line.

Another red line.

A line heading upward.

A line that wavered like a wave, yet remained high in the end.

“The screen looks hot.”

‘So does the content.’

“Do not say that so calmly.”

## Section 1 — The red lines are simply rising

The first graph was CO₂ concentration.

It started from a low point long ago and rose slowly.

At some point, the rise gradually became steeper.

Now it has reached a very high level.

“This one is easy to understand.”

‘It is going upward.’

“That is a crude impression.”

‘But it is enough as a starting point.’

—CO₂ is increasing.

—The CO₂ that has increased remains in the atmosphere for a long time.

—It gradually changes the way Earth releases heat into space.

“I understand that much.”

‘CO₂ is a major cause of warming.’

“That cannot be left out.”

This is not a story about ignoring CO₂.

It is not a story about no longer needing emissions reduction.

The moment I removed that, this would become mere escapism.

So first I look.

I look at the red line.

I look at the line that keeps rising.

“But...”

‘What?’

“The world keeps saying we need to reduce CO₂.”

‘It does.’

“And yet the line is still rising.”

‘It is rising.’

“Where are the countermeasures?”

‘Working hard outside the screen.’

“They are working hard, but the graph is not beaten yet?”

‘That is the situation.’

“In game terms, damage is being dealt, but the enemy’s auto-regeneration is stronger?”

‘That is quite close.’

“What an unpleasant boss.”

Next, I looked at the line for greenhouse-gas emissions.

This one also has not fallen easily.

There are year-to-year fluctuations.

There are policies.

There is technology.

Renewable energy is increasing.

Energy efficiency is improving.

Even so, when seen globally, emissions remain at a high level.

“This is rough.”

‘That does not mean efforts to reduce emissions are meaningless.’

“Right.”

‘But they have not yet been enough to push back the global pressure of increase.’

“Population, industry, energy demand—everything is there.”

‘Yes.’

“So the places trying to reduce and the forces that still increase are pushing against each other on the same planet.”

‘That is a fair understanding.’

I looked at the two red lines.

CO₂ concentration.

Greenhouse-gas emissions.

Neither of them had bent easily.

“There was not just one red line.”

‘That is the first point.’

## Section 2 — If it were only CO₂, it would still be one line

Next, the AI showed the line for global average temperature.

This one also headed upward.

But it was a little more restless than the CO₂ line.

Years when it rose.

Years when it calmed a little.

Years when it jumped again.

“This one is less calm.”

‘Because temperature is not determined by CO₂ alone.’

“There it is.”

‘What is?’

“The theme of Part 6.”

I pointed at the screen.

“CO₂ increases. Heat becomes easier to trap. That much I understand.”

‘Yes.’

“But actual temperature moves together with the state of the ocean, wind, clouds, soil, forests, urban heat, and that year’s El Niño.”

‘Many factors overlap.’

“So CO₂ is the big boss, but there are also small enemies, mid-bosses, and terrain damage.”

‘If we continue the game metaphor, yes.’

“And all of them attack at the same time.”

‘That is close.’

“That is the worst.”

Reducing CO₂ is necessary.

But if I look only at CO₂, I cannot fully explain this year’s heat, this year’s downpours, or this year’s marine heatwaves.

The problem of heat accumulating over a long period and the problem of damage jumping in this very season overlap.

—CO₂ changes how heat escapes.

—The ocean absorbs a large amount of heat.

—When soil dries, the ground holds even more heat.

—When forests are lost, the flow of water and heat also changes.

—Cities store heat in asphalt and buildings.

—In El Niño years, ocean-atmosphere movement can push global temperature upward.

“I used to look at only one line.”

‘Reduce emissions. Lower concentration. Lower temperature.’

“I looked at only that, straight ahead.”

‘It is not wrong.’

“But it is not enough.”

‘A useful phrase, is it not?’

“Maybe we really should make it the signboard.”

If it were only the CO₂ line, the story would still look like one line.

But the moment I overlay the temperature line, the world stops being a single-line story.

Long-term increase.

Year-to-year fluctuation.

Ocean conditions.

Dry land.

Urban heat.

And then damage to daily life overlaps with all of that.

“There are too many red lines.”

‘It is not only red. Next, a place that should be blue is red.’

“That is a terrible preview.”

## Section 3 — Why even the blue ocean is red

“Next is sea-surface temperature?”

I looked at the fourth screen.

The ocean map, which should have looked blue, was painted red and orange.

“The ocean is red.”

‘Because the colors represent heat.’

“I know, but visually it feels wrong.”

The ocean has absorbed much of the excess heat entering Earth’s system.

When sea-surface temperature remains high, the influence spreads not only to air, but also to rain, wind, clouds, typhoons, fisheries, and ecosystems.

“So the ocean has been acting as a heat savings account.”

‘A very large one.’

“And withdrawals are forbidden?”

‘More precisely, the heat cannot simply be erased.’

“What a terrible savings account.”

The AI overlaid another line on the sea-surface-temperature figure.

A line that rose and fell like a wave.

“This is El Niño?”

‘A simplified line showing major changes in sea-surface-temperature anomalies across the Pacific.’

“When it jumps upward, global temperature also tends to rise?”

‘There are years like that.’

“So CO₂ raises the whole floor of Earth, and El Niño puts a jump pad on top of that?”

‘A rough image, but not a bad one.’

“Rough images are easier to remember.”

—Over the long term, greenhouse gases such as CO₂ raise the thermal baseline of the whole planet.

—Ocean variability such as El Niño overlaps with that.

—Marine heatwaves, urban heat, dry soil, damaged forests, and uneven water then overlap as well.

“Is everything coming at once?”

‘Not everything comes with the same strength every time.’

“That helps.”

‘But there are years when they overlap.’

“That does not help.”

The AI enlarged the red ocean map.

“This redness reaches the fishing port too, doesn’t it?”

‘It does.’

“The story about reading a water-temperature chart in the port’s morning meeting suddenly connects to the world.”

‘In Parts 4 and 5, the water-temperature chart was a regional story.’

“But behind it, there is this ocean heat.”

‘Yes.’

I fell silent for a moment.

Earth’s ocean is huge.

My port is small.

But the port’s decisions cannot escape changes in the ocean.

“If the ocean is red, the paper at the port turns red too.”

‘That is rather literary.’

“No, I mean it in a bad way.”

## Section 4 — In front of the red graphs, auxiliary wheels are small

I zoomed the screen out a little.

On the left were the world’s graphs.

Rising CO₂.

High emissions.

Temperature rising while fluctuating.

The ocean holding heat.

El Niño overlapping like a wave.

On the right were the auxiliary wheels in my own town.

Station-front mist.

Park benches.

Rain gardens.

Retention basins.

Water-temperature charts at the fishing port.

“The sense of scale is broken.”

‘What do you mean?’

“The left side is Earth. The right side is a town.”

‘Yes.’

“The left side is hundreds of ppm of CO₂, tens of gigatonnes of emissions, global average temperature, and sea-surface temperature.”

‘Yes.’

“The right side is mist in front of a station, water collected in a garden, a bench moved two meters.”

‘Small.’

“Small.”

I was silent for a while.

“...Is this powerless after all?”

The AI did not answer immediately.

‘It has almost no effect on lowering global CO₂ concentration.’

“Right.”

‘It cannot stop marine heatwaves either.’

“Yeah.”

‘It cannot erase El Niño itself.’

“I know.”

‘But it can slightly bend the heat and water damage that arrives in a region.’

“That is the point.”

Station-front mist does not lower global temperature.

But on a hot day, it can push the moment when someone waiting for a bus says “I cannot take this anymore” a few minutes farther away.

A rain garden does not lower sea-surface temperature.

But during a downpour, it can receive some of the water falling from a roof once before passing it on.

A retention basin does not stop El Niño.

But when a river rises, it can accept part of the water so it does not all go into the town.

A water-temperature chart at a fishing port does not cool the sea.

But against an ocean that has become harder to read, it can move departure and preparation a little earlier.

‘Auxiliary wheels do not directly bend the world’s graphs.’

“But when the world’s graphs are red, they can help keep a region from breaking.”

‘That is a good way to put it.’

“Not universal, though.”

‘Not universal.’

“But not zero either.”

‘Not zero.’

That distance was probably important.

If we make auxiliary wheels sound too big, it becomes a lie.

But if we make them too small, they become a reason to do nothing.

“Part 6 walks the space between those.”

‘A part that neither overestimates nor underestimates small auxiliary wheels in front of red graphs.’

“That wording is stiff, but it fits.”

## Section 5 — Where should small auxiliary wheels be placed?

“But I still have a question.”

‘What is it?’

“Auxiliary wheels slightly bend how things break, right?”

‘Yes.’

“Then if the world’s red graphs get even redder, won’t the auxiliary wheels become insufficient too?”

The AI paused briefly.

‘They will.’

“You answered immediately.”

‘Auxiliary wheels alone cannot keep supporting conditions that worsen without limit.’

“Right.”

Regional auxiliary wheels are not a substitute for emissions reduction.

They are not a reason to stop CO₂ measures.

They are not a story where the world is late, so the town alone should just try harder.

“Then what are auxiliary wheels?”

‘They are something that works alongside emissions reduction to slightly change the heat and water breakdown already arriving now.’

“Measures to make future temperatures harder to raise, and measures to make people less likely to collapse in today’s heat.”

‘They are also a way to keep those two from being treated as opposites.’

“It is not CO₂ or auxiliary wheels.”

‘It is CO₂ reduction and auxiliary wheels.’

“The moment we make it a binary choice, we lean toward game over.”

‘Very dangerous.’

“That is easy to understand.”

Global emissions reduction is necessary.

In fact, it is necessary at the highest level of priority.

But it takes time before the effects of emissions reduction are felt as relief from heat in daily life.

In the meantime, summer comes.

Downpours come.

Changes in the sea reach fishing ports.

“So auxiliary wheels fill that time gap?”

‘That is one of their roles.’

“Calling it buying time sounds a little light.”

‘Buying time is not a bad thing.’

“Isn’t it?”

‘Without time, evacuation, repair, learning, institutional change, and the next countermeasure are all impossible.’

“I see.”

Auxiliary wheels are not magic that saves the world all at once.

But they create time.

They make judgment slightly earlier.

They share the burden a little.

They slow the breaking a little.

During that time, emissions reduction and larger structural revisions can proceed.

“So auxiliary wheels are not an escape route. They are also footing that creates time.”

‘Good summary.’

—Map of Part 6

—Globally, CO₂ emissions and greenhouse-gas emissions must be reduced, and temperature rise must be restrained.

—Regionally, the heat and water breakdown already arriving now must be bent slightly.

—Auxiliary wheels for the ocean, soil, and cities must connect those two.

“Is this enough?”

‘Still rough.’

“I know.”

‘But as the map for Episode 2, it is enough.’

“Look at the world’s graphs.”

‘Yes.’

“So I cannot run away.”

‘You can, but the story will not continue.’

The AI was silent for a moment.

After that silence, I looked at the red graphs on the left side of the screen.

They were frightening.

Honestly, very frightening.

But I also looked at the small auxiliary wheels on the right side.

Mist in front of the station.

Shade from a bench.

A hollow in a garden.

Grassland in a retention basin.

A water-temperature chart at the port.

Compared with the world, they were far too small.

Even so, they had not disappeared.

“In a world where everything comes at once...”

I said at the end.

“We do not have to receive everything with one method, do we?”

‘What do you mean?’

“CO₂ is reduced as CO₂.”

‘Yes.’

“Heat is released as heat.”

‘Yes.’

“Water is received once as water.”

‘Yes.’

“The sea is read as the sea.”

‘Yes.’

“And a town makes itself harder to knock down as a town.”

‘Separate them to see them. Then connect them.’

“Right.”

The world’s graphs are red.

But in front of those red graphs, there is no need to stop moving.

The work of changing the large lines and the work of protecting small places have to be done at the same time.

“Part 6 Episode 2 goes this far.”

‘Next, perhaps connect the world’s ocean and the regional sea.’

“El Niño and the fishing port?”

‘That would be natural.’

“The sea is heavy again.”

‘The sea has always been heavy.’

“I know.”

Under the map of Part 6, I wrote the final line.

—In a world where everything comes at once, auxiliary wheels are needed that let us separate things, connect them, and receive them.

Part 6 Episode 2 ends here.

## Afterword

In Part 6 Episode 2, the protagonist and the AI looked at several graphs at once: CO₂ concentration, greenhouse-gas emissions, global average temperature, sea-surface temperature, and El Niño.

The episode placed three main points.

### 1. Climate breakdown is not “one line”

CO₂ is a major cause of warming, and emissions reduction is necessary.

However, the extreme heat, heavy rain, marine heatwaves, and impacts on fisheries that reach daily life appear through the overlap of CO₂, ocean conditions, El Niño, soil, water, and urban heat.

The protagonist described it as “a game where the big boss, small enemies, and terrain damage all come at once.”

Factors that make heat accumulate over the long term and factors that make damage jump year by year or season by season do not arrive separately. They overlap.

### 2. Auxiliary wheels do not directly bend the world’s red graphs

Station-front mist, rain gardens, retention basins, and fishing-port water-temperature charts do not directly lower global average temperature or CO₂ concentration.

They do not stop marine heatwaves or erase El Niño itself.

But they can slightly increase the time people can spend in dangerous heat.

They can receive heavy rain once.

They can accept part of a rising river’s burden.

They can help a port look slightly ahead at a changing sea.

In that way, they slightly bend regional breakdown.

Auxiliary wheels must not be overestimated.

But they must not be dismissed as meaningless simply because they are small.

### 3. Not “emissions reduction or auxiliary wheels,” but “emissions reduction and auxiliary wheels”

Auxiliary wheels are not a substitute for emissions reduction.

They are not a reason to stop CO₂ measures.

At the same time, emissions reduction alone cannot immediately respond to the heat, heavy rain, and sea changes that are already arriving now.

Measures to make future temperature rise harder, and measures to make people less likely to collapse in today’s heat.

Those two should not be opposed to one another. They must proceed in parallel.

In Part 6, global-scale emissions reduction and regional-scale auxiliary wheels for heat, water, and the sea will be treated not as opposites, but as parallel necessities.

In a world where everything comes at once, everything cannot be received with one method.

Reduce CO₂ as CO₂.

Release heat as heat.

Receive water once as water.

Read the sea as the sea.

Make the town harder to knock down as a town.

Separate them to see them.

Then connect them and receive them.

That sentence is the center of Part 6 Episode 2.

Episode 2 confirmed that neither the world’s red graphs nor regional auxiliary wheels are enough alone.

Next, the story will connect the world’s ocean and the regional sea by looking at El Niño, marine heatwaves, and water-temperature reading at fishing ports.
