# Part 6 Episode 4 — If the sea is hot, does the station front get hot too?

## Foreword

In Part 6 Episode 3, we saw how distant Pacific changes such as El Niño can reach the morning at a fishing port and even the decision of whether and when to leave port.

In Episode 4, the viewpoint moves again.

The sea holds heat.

Does that heat end above the sea?

Or does it reach the station front, the shopping street, and even one’s own room through wind, humidity, and nighttime temperature?

This is the episode where the protagonist and the AI connect “the heat of the sea” and “the city’s summer” with one line, and think about what urban auxiliary wheels can do against heat that comes from the sea.

---

“If the sea is hot, does the station front get hot too?”

I asked while closing the Pacific map from the previous episode.

The red belt.

Sea-surface temperature.

El Niño.

The morning at the fishing port.

I understood that much.

Distant changes in the sea cast a shadow over decisions at the port.

We saw that in Episode 3.

“But I am not on the sea right now.”

‘You are at the station front.’

“Right. Between asphalt, outdoor units, and vending machines.”

‘That is a very different landscape from the sea.’

“It is.”

‘But it is not disconnected.’

“That again?”

‘It could become the slogan of Part 6.’

“The distant sea and our town are not disconnected.”

‘Exactly.’

“That is a convenient phrase.”

‘You are one to talk.’

The AI displayed a new diagram on the screen.

On the left was a sea holding heat.

On the right was a station front in summer.

Between them, many arrows were drawn.

Sea.

Air.

Water vapor.

Wind.

Clouds.

Nighttime temperature.

Urban heat storage.

The apparent temperature at the station front.

“Too many arrows.”

‘Because this cannot be explained with a single line.’

“Part 6 says that every time.”

‘This time too.’

## Section 1 — The sea’s heat does not belong only to the sea

The AI placed two pictures on the screen.

On the left.

A sea holding heat.

On the right.

A city in summer.

Between them, many arrows were drawn.

From the sea to the sky.

From the sky to the wind.

From the wind to humidity.

From humidity to nighttime temperature.

From nighttime temperature to the next day’s city.

“There are many arrows.”

‘The sea’s heat does not simply sit still on the sea surface.’

“Evaporation, clouds, wind, and so on?”

‘Yes. A warm sea gives heat and water vapor to the air.’

“Water vapor carries heat too, right?”

‘More precisely, it carries heat and influences the state of the atmosphere.’

“So when the sea is hot, the air can become humid, and nights may not cool down as easily.”

‘Depending on the region and wind flow, that kind of influence is possible.’

“You suddenly became careful.”

‘Because if we connect the sea and the city with one straight line, it becomes a lie.’

“That is important.”

The sea is hot.

Therefore, my town will definitely become hot.

It is not that simple.

Even on days when the sea is warm, the influence may be small depending on wind direction.

The way clouds appear or rain falls may change the apparent temperature.

The inland terrain and the city’s structure also change how heat appears.

But.

— The sea continues to hold heat.

— The state of air and humidity changes.

— Nighttime temperature does not fall easily.

— The city’s concrete also keeps holding the heat of the day.

— The next day becomes hot again.

That kind of overlap can indeed occur.

“When many conditions overlap, the sea’s heat gets mixed into the city’s summer too.”

‘That understanding is close.’

“The sea does not walk directly to the station front.”

‘It does not.’

“But air and humidity that have received the sea’s heat can come to town.”

‘Yes.’

“And the town itself is also holding heat.”

‘That is the main point of Episode 4.’

## Section 2 — If the night does not cool down, the next day is already checkmate

“Even if the sea’s heat comes,” I said, looking out the window.

“I understand that daytime is hot. But isn’t the night the really hard part?”

‘The night?’

“If the night does not cool down, rooms, roads, and people’s bodies do not recover.”

‘Urban heat becomes hard to release.’

“If a scorching day lasts only one day, sometimes you can get through it by just enduring.”

‘But if the night stays hot too, the starting point for the next day changes.’

“That’s it.”

Even at night, the balcony floor remains lukewarm.

Around the outdoor unit, heat released during the day still lingers.

Even if I open the window a little, the air that comes in is not cool.

If I turn the air conditioner off, it gets hot.

If I leave it on, the outdoor unit releases more heat.

“There is nowhere to escape.”

‘Urban nights combine the heat of the day, humidity arriving from outside, and the heat stored in buildings.’

“A three-hit combo.”

‘Smaller in scale than Episode 2.’

“Even if it is smaller, when it hits inside a room, it is direct damage.”

Global sea-surface temperature.

El Niño in the distant Pacific.

The lukewarm night wind in the city.

The heat trapped in my own room.

“This really is connected.”

‘The world’s graphs come all the way down to a sleepless night.’

“That is an unpleasant connection.”

‘But if we do not look at it, we cannot take measures against it.’

“Right.”

The night does not cool down.

That does not only mean it is hard to sleep.

The body does not recover easily.

For elderly people, children, and people in poor physical condition, the burden is large.

From the next morning, they are already tired.

And in that state, the daytime heat comes again.

“When the night stops being recovery time and becomes standby time for the next heatwave, that is really hard.”

‘That is why urban auxiliary wheels cannot look only at daytime.’

“We need to look at night too.”

‘Yes.’

## Section 3 — A city that does not move reaches morning while still holding heat

The AI placed two city screens side by side.

The left side was the unmoving world line.

Asphalt holding the daytime heat.

Outdoor-unit heat that does not disappear even at night.

A station front where wind does not pass through.

A shopping street that keeps the heat of the day.

A closed apartment room.

“I know this view.”

‘What is the hardest part?’

“That there is nowhere to release the heat.”

There is little shade at the station front.

The shopping-street mist runs only during events.

The park benches have absorbed the daytime heat.

Inside the room, the choice is to cool it or endure it.

Even at night, the wind is lukewarm.

“Before talking about the sea being hot or CO₂ increasing, this town itself is holding too much heat right now.”

‘That is the city-side problem.’

“Right. It is not only the world’s red graphs.”

The sea holds heat.

That heat overlaps with the state of air and humidity.

But if the city side has a structure that does not hold too much heat, the damage it receives may be a little different.

‘That is where auxiliary wheels are placed.’

“Exactly.”

An unmoving city receives heat from outside.

At the same time, it creates heat by itself.

The ground holds heat.

Buildings hold heat.

Outdoor units discharge heat.

The path of wind is blocked by buildings and signs.

There is little tree shade.

Water immediately runs into drains.

Even at night, the heat does not leave.

“So outside heat and the heat the city holds by itself overlap.”

‘Yes.’

“Even though it cannot stop the heat of the sea, the town is amplifying heat too.”

‘That is the problem of Episode 4.’

“An unmoving city does not only receive heat. It holds it.”

‘That is a good way to say it.’

## Section 4 — A city that moves can release a little heat

On the right-side screen, there was a town in the world line that had bent slightly.

It was not a dramatic future city.

There were greenery and shade at the station front.

On alert days, mist came out thinly.

The layout included keeping wind paths from being blocked.

In the park, the ground did not hold heat as easily at night, and there was a small water area.

On apartment balconies, there were small ways to block solar radiation and enough room for wind to escape.

“These are things we saw in earlier parts.”

‘The urban episodes of Part 4.’

“They come back here?”

‘After looking at the world’s sea, their meaning changes a little.’

“How?”

‘Before, we looked at urban auxiliary wheels as “mechanisms that allow people to rest a little in a hot town.”’

“And now?”

‘Now we look at them as mechanisms that keep a city from holding too much heat in a world where the sea’s heat and humidity overlap.’

I fell silent for a moment.

The same mist.

The same shade.

The same benches.

The same rain gardens.

But the background I was seeing had changed.

— The world’s ocean holds high heat.

— The night air no longer cools down as much as before.

— Under those conditions, the city amplifies heat even more by itself.

“Auxiliary wheels do not cool the world’s ocean.”

‘Right.’

“But they can slightly change the way heat from the sea and heat created by the city overlap.”

‘They can play that role.’

Shade reduces the heat the ground holds.

Greenery can soften sunlight depending on the location.

Wind paths can help prevent hot air from becoming trapped.

Mist creates a place where people can endure dangerous hours when conditions fit.

Rain gardens and soil-covered places do more than immediately throw rainwater into drains; they also restore, a little, the relationship between ground and water.

In rooms at night, measures that keep heat from accumulating can preserve a little of the next day’s strength.

“It is not a full-party heal button.”

‘No.’

“But it is a damage-reduction buff.”

‘In game terms, yes.’

“Good. That is easy to understand.”

‘However, buffs also have duration and conditions.’

“You suddenly leaned into the game analogy.”

‘I adapted to you.’

“I do not know whether that is kind or rough.”

Urban auxiliary wheels do not cool the sea.

They do not directly lower global average temperature either.

But they can reduce, a little, how much heat the city holds.

They can increase, a little, the places where people can rest.

They can leave, a little, room for heat to escape at night.

“Small, but not zero.”

‘A conclusion that will come up many times in Part 6.’

## Afterword

In Part 6 Episode 4, we looked at the connection between “the heat held by the sea” and “the city’s summer.”

Rising sea-surface temperature and sea changes such as El Niño are not only problems of the distant ocean.

Through wind, humidity, nighttime temperature, and the way rain falls, they can also overlap with how heat is felt in cities.

However, it is not a simple story in which the station front necessarily becomes hot because the sea is hot.

Regional temperature and apparent heat are determined by several overlapping conditions, including wind, clouds, terrain, the city’s structure, ground surfaces, buildings, outdoor units, and the presence or absence of greenery and water.

The main points of this episode are three.

### 1. The sea’s heat is not disconnected from the city’s summer

A warm sea gives heat and water vapor to the air and affects wind and humidity.

When that overlaps with urban heat storage and nighttime temperature, it can lead not only to “hot days,” but also to “summers when the night does not allow recovery.”

However, a region’s heat is not determined by sea temperature alone. The city’s structure, ground surfaces, wind, buildings, and other conditions also overlap.

As Part 6 keeps repeating, the world and the region are not disconnected.

But that connection is not a single line.

### 2. An unmoving city stacks outside heat and heat it creates by itself

A station front with little shade.

Building placement that does not allow wind through.

Asphalt that holds heat.

Outdoor-unit exhaust.

Balcony heat that does not escape.

A lack of water and greenery.

When these conditions overlap, the city itself amplifies heat in addition to global heat influence.

A region alone cannot erase heat coming from the sea.

But it can keep the city from holding too much heat by itself.

### 3. Urban auxiliary wheels can change the “way heat overlaps” even if they cannot cool the sea

Mist, shade, greenery, wind paths, rain gardens, and ways of living that release heat do not directly lower world sea-surface temperature or global average temperature.

Even so, they can reduce the extra heat cities hold and slightly increase the times and places where people can rest.

They cannot stop a marine heatwave.

But on heatstroke-alert days, mist can be operated while checking conditions.

They cannot erase El Niño.

But measures that keep heat from lingering inside rooms can gradually be increased.

Part 6 Episode 4 depicted the role of auxiliary wheels as: even if we cannot erase the world’s large heat, we can avoid stacking too much heat locally.

Precisely because the sea is hot, cities should not increase their own heat too much.

The heat of the distant sea passes through many conditions and casts a shadow even over the heat at the station front.

That is why cities need auxiliary wheels that keep heat from stacking too much.

This is the central sentence of Part 6 Episode 4.

Next time, it may be natural to look at “rain” between the sea and the city.

How do the heat and water vapor held by the sea overlap with heavy rain and the movement of water through a watershed? At that moment, what meaning do rain gardens, sponge soil, and retention basins have?

That connects naturally to the flow of Part 6.
