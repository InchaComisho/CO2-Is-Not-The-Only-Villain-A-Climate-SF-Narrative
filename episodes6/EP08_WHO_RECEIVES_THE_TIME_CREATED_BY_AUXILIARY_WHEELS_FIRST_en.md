# Part 6 Episode 8 — Who receives the time created by auxiliary wheels first?

## Foreword

In Part 6 Episode 7, we saw how the “little time” created by upstream soil, midstream rain gardens, and downstream retention basins might be used by the town’s people for evacuation and calling out to one another.

In Episode 8, we think about who that time reaches.

Even if evacuation information arrives, not everyone can move immediately.

People without cars.

People who have difficulty walking.

People with small children.

People who have difficulty receiving information in Japanese only.

People who live with pets.

People who are at work and cannot see the town’s notifications.

This is the episode where the protagonist and the AI face the question: “Is the time created by auxiliary wheels really time for everyone?”

---

“I said something pretty good last time.”

I looked back at the final lines from the previous episode.

— Auxiliary wheels are not a reason to avoid evacuation.  
— They are a reason to begin evacuation a few minutes earlier.

“Even I think that was pretty good.”

‘Self-praise.’

“But today, I will break it.”

‘Break it?’

“Question it again.”

I opened a new note.

— Who receives those few minutes first?

The AI did not answer immediately.

“That is an unpleasant question, isn’t it?”

‘It is a necessary question.’

“Part 6 Episode 8 is about whose time the auxiliary wheels create.”

‘More precisely, it is about who we arrange that time to reach.’

“It does not reach people naturally.”

‘Right.’

I displayed a heavy-rain notification on the screen.

The same town.

The same rain.

The same river.

The same evacuation information.

But the time needed before people begin to move differs from person to person.

## Section 1 — Even with the same notification, people cannot move in the same way

A heavy-rain notification appeared on the screen.

Heavy rain.

Rising river level.

Evacuation information.

The community center has opened.

“If this arrives, everyone evacuates.”

‘Do they really?’

“...Some people do not.”

‘Why?’

“Because it is troublesome?”

‘Not only that.’

The AI divided the screen into four.

Top left.

An elderly person living alone near the river.

Top right.

A parent with two small children.

Bottom left.

Someone at work on a night shift.

Bottom right.

Someone without a car, living with a dog.

“The same notification has arrived.”

‘But the preparation time they need is not the same.’

The elderly person looks for medicine, a cane, and necessary documents.

The parent checks children’s clothes, drinks, a baby carrier, and emergency food.

The night-shift worker cannot immediately see the rain in town.

The person without a car thinks about the route to the shelter and whether the dog can come along.

A person with difficulty walking imagines walking on wet roads and hesitates to begin moving at all.

A person who has difficulty receiving information in Japanese only needs time to confirm what the notification means.

“One sentence saying ‘evacuate immediately’ cannot move everyone in the same way.”

‘Right.’

“The notification may arrive equally.”

‘But the difficulty of evacuation is not equal.’

“That is heavy.”

‘Because it is reality.’

Notifications arrive all at once.

But what happens after that is not all at once.

People who can look at a smartphone.

People who cannot.

People who are at home.

People who are at work.

People who have cars.

People who can only walk.

People who can go out alone.

People who need someone’s help.

“It looks like the same starting line, but it is actually different.”

‘Yes.’

“If the few minutes created by auxiliary wheels are left alone, people who can move quickly will use them first.”

‘That is the problem of Episode 8.’

## Section 2 — In the unmoving world line, only those who can move quickly move quickly

“Then, the unmoving world line.”

The same town appeared on the left side of the screen.

The rain is strong.

The river level is rising.

A notification has been issued.

But the town’s message is only one sentence.

— Evacuation information has been issued.  
— Those in dangerous areas should evacuate.

“It is not wrong.”

‘But it is not enough.’

People who can move quickly can carry their bags and leave by themselves.

People with cars can put their families inside and move.

People who know the shelter location do not get lost.

People who are looking at their smartphones can read the notification.

“People who can move quickly can move.”

‘Then what about people who have difficulty moving?’

The elderly person thinks, “I may cause trouble for others.”

The parent wonders, “Maybe taking children out into this rain is more dangerous.”

The person without a car stops while imagining walking through wet streets.

The foreign-language speaker spends time looking up the details of the notification.

The person living with a pet wonders whether the pet can be taken to the shelter.

The person at work misses the timing to contact family.

“Only people who can move quickly can use the auxiliary-wheel time.”

‘In the unmoving world line, time gaps become evacuation gaps as they are.’

“The rain falls on everyone in the same way.”

‘It does. But not everyone can escape in the same way.’

“That is hard.”

‘That is why we have to look at it.’

In the unmoving world line, the few minutes created by auxiliary wheels do not automatically reach everyone.

Rather, people who are already able to move easily are more likely to use them first.

People who can decide for themselves.

People who have transportation.

People whose family is together.

People who can read the information.

People who feel relatively little anxiety about the shelter.

“There is a difference in how the time created by auxiliary wheels reaches people too.”

‘Yes.’

## Section 3 — In the slightly bent world line, time is handed over first

“Then the right side.”

The AI opened the slightly bent world line.

The same rain.

The same river.

The same evacuation information.

What was different was that the town had thought a little, during normal times, about who needs time.

Elderly households near the river have a contact person.

Before evacuation information is issued, someone calls and says, “The rain is getting stronger, so please gather your medicine and necessary items.”

Households with small children have been told about a nearby temporary meeting place.

A simple map shows routes where strollers can pass easily and shelter entrances with fewer steps.

For people without cars, the town already knows who can provide neighborhood cars or welfare vehicles.

However, who can drive, which roads tend to become dangerous, and what to do if vehicles cannot be used have also been checked during normal times.

People living with pets have minimal guidance: “At this shelter, you can consult about this area.”

It is not completely reassuring.

Even so, it can reduce some hesitation time.

People who need information in languages other than Japanese receive short multilingual evacuation guidance.

Even if it is not a perfect translation, it makes “where,” “when,” and “what to bring” a little easier to understand.

People who are away from home for work have shared the priority order for contacting family.

Who will watch the children?

Who will call the grandparents?

Who will first check whether the shelter has opened?

“That is impressive.”

‘No flashy equipment has increased.’

“But the town knows who will be in trouble first.”

‘Yes.’

The time created by the watershed team is handed first to people who need preparation first.

“The auxiliary-wheel time is not monopolized by people who can run fast.”

‘That is the difference in the slightly bent world line.’

“Not first-come, first-served.”

‘Right.’

“It is not only the water peak that is shifted. The way time reaches people is shifted too.”

‘It is arranged to reach those who need it first.’

## Section 4 — Fairness does not mean moving everyone at the same time

“This part is a little difficult.”

I said while looking at the screen.

‘What is?’

“I thought fairness meant sending the same notification to everyone.”

‘That is one kind of fairness.’

“But if it is only the same notification, people who can move first are helped first.”

‘That can happen.’

“Then is it fairer to provide different support to different people?”

‘To hand the necessary time and support first to those who need them.’

“That is closer to fairness.”

‘Closer.’

I thought for a moment.

One minute of margin for someone who can walk.

Ten minutes of margin for someone who has difficulty walking.

A notification for someone who can leave alone.

A phone call and a ride for someone who has difficulty leaving alone.

Guidance about an acceptance area for someone anxious about the shelter.

Short multilingual guidance for someone who has difficulty with the language.

“Not making it the same, but making it in time.”

‘That is a good way to say it.’

“Is today a day when you praise me too?”

‘Because this is important.’

Auxiliary wheels delay water a little.

The town’s contact network hands that time to people.

And when it reaches people who need support first, the auxiliary wheel becomes, for the first time, a town’s auxiliary wheel.

“A town’s auxiliary wheel.”

‘Soil and water auxiliary wheels are still only half of it.’

“When they reach people, they become town auxiliary wheels.”

‘Yes.’

## Section 5 — Even so, some people will still be missed

“But this is not universal either.”

I said it first.

‘That is right.’

There are days when the contact person is ill.

There are times when the phone does not connect.

There are times when there are not enough cars.

The shelter may become crowded.

There may be confusion on-site over accepting pets.

Even translated guidance may not convey everything.

The support list may be outdated.

Family situations may have changed.

“Even with a system, people will still be missed.”

‘They will.’

“Then why do it?”

‘Not to promise zero missed people.’

“That is the same answer again.”

‘Because it is an important answer.’

A single phone call to someone who was hesitating alone until yesterday.

One available car for someone who has no car.

One additional guidance sheet for someone who will struggle at the shelter.

A short evacuation notice for someone who has difficulty with Japanese.

Information about an entrance with fewer steps for a household with small children.

Just that can slightly increase the number of people who make it in time.

“In front of the world’s red graphs, this looks small.”

‘It is small.’

“But on an evening when the river is rising, it is huge.”

‘Yes.’

“If we call small things meaningless just because they are small, we get checkmated at the front door.”

‘That is a very Part 6 way to put it.’

“Are you praising me?”

‘A little.’

On the right.

On top of that, the town moves a little ahead of time.

Early contact and help with movement reach those who need more time.

“Shall we end with this?”

## Afterword

Part 6 Episode 8 depicted the theme: “Who receives the time created by auxiliary wheels first?”

Even if watershed auxiliary wheels shift the peak of heavy rain a little, there are people who can use that time and people who cannot.

The difficulty of evacuation changes depending on age, health, family structure, language, work, transportation, whether someone has pets, and other conditions.

The points depicted in this episode are mainly these three.

### 1. Even with the same evacuation information, the conditions for movement are not the same

Elderly people, households with small children, people without cars, people at work, and people who have difficulty receiving information may need more preparation time before they can begin evacuation.

This episode depicted how issuing a notification all at once can easily create a situation where only people who can move quickly move first.

Notifications may arrive equally.

But the difficulty of evacuation is not equal.

### 2. Auxiliary-wheel time is handed first to those who need it

Contact roles, movement support, evacuation routes, shelter entrances, multilingual guidance, and consultation points for pets can be prepared during normal times.

This creates the possibility of handing the “small margin” created by the watershed team earlier to people who especially need preparation.

Do not let the time created by auxiliary wheels be monopolized by people who can already move quickly.

To do that, the town needs to know, even a little, who needs time during normal times.

### 3. Fairness does not only mean moving everyone at the same time

It is important to deliver the same notification to everyone.

However, to truly make it in time, the necessary time and support must be considered for each person.

For someone who can walk, one minute of margin may be enough.

For someone who has difficulty walking, ten minutes of margin may be needed.

For someone who can leave alone, a notification may be enough.

For someone who has difficulty leaving alone, a phone call or a ride may be needed.

In Part 6 Episode 8, we placed the viewpoint of “making it in time” rather than merely “making it the same” as a role of auxiliary wheels.

Even so, some people will still be missed.

Calls may not connect.

There may not be enough cars.

Shelters may become confused.

That is why this should be considered not as a perfect system, but as an auxiliary wheel for increasing even slightly the number of people who make it in time.

Part 6 Episode 8 showed how the world’s heat and water problem ultimately descends to someone’s front door, a single phone call, one car, and one sheet of shelter guidance.

The time created by auxiliary wheels is handed first to those who need time first.

Delivering the same notification is not enough.

Deliver the time needed to make it in time first to those who need it.

This sentence is the center of Part 6 Episode 8.

Next time, we will likely look at who keeps supporting that time.

Contact persons, neighborhood associations, community centers, shopping streets, family members, and neighbors: the episode will likely examine how to continue the human-side auxiliary wheels.
