# Part 5 Episode 2 — The maintenance-inspection morning, what is forgotten after it moves, and the system that is not forgotten

## Foreword

In Part 5 Episode 1, the focus shifted to how auxiliary wheels can be continued, widened, and preserved, and we looked again at budgets, people, institutions, maintenance, records, and the speed at which things are forgotten.

Episode 2 takes up the most modest, and perhaps the most important, morning among them: the morning of maintenance inspection.

An auxiliary wheel that has moved matters not only at the moment it moves, but also in how it is watched afterward, how it is repaired, and how it is kept from being forgotten.

Using the station-front mist equipment as an example, the protagonist and the AI trace the difference between “what is forgotten after it moves” and “a system that is not forgotten.”

“It may be the most important part.”

‘The morning of maintenance inspection.’

“Right.”

In Part 4, we looked at the mist at the station front.

On days of dangerous heat, when conditions allowed, a fine mist rose for a short time.

People could wait for a few minutes.

It did not erase all the heat.

But it extended, just a little, the time one could remain standing.

That was the kind of auxiliary wheel it was.

“But,”

‘Yes?’

“Will that mist really come out properly next year too?”

‘A very Part 5 question.’

“The glamour vanished all at once.”

‘Stories about continuing things are usually not glamorous.’

“That is the hard part.”

I opened the station-front mist folder.

Photographs from the introduction.

The notice that operation had begun.

Comments from users.

And beneath them, one small file.

—Inspection records.

## Section 1 — What is forgotten after it moves

The station-front mist is still operating this year, at least for now.

When a heatstroke alert is issued, the system checks conditions and a fine mist rises for short periods.

It looks the same as last year.

But inside the equipment, it is not exactly the same as last year.

“The fact that it moves is no longer unusual.”

I opened the reality-line log.

—In the first year, people came to look at it.

—Some people took photos with their phones.

—People in the shopping street proudly said, “Mist has been installed here starting this year.”

—In the second year, it became, “We had that last year too.”

—By the third year, far fewer people cared who inspected it, who stopped it, or who was responsible for it.

‘That is what gets forgotten after it moves.’

“Exactly.”

If the mist comes out, people notice it.

If it does not come out, some people complain.

But what happens before it comes out is not very visible.

—Depending on wind direction and water pressure, the mist can become uneven at some nozzles.

—Invisible dirt and clogging can slowly build up inside parts of the piping.

—If water quality is not checked before operation, hygiene concerns can arise.

—Electrical components may need more detailed repairs than the previous year.

—In places where the floor becomes wet, slipperiness must also be checked.

‘But people in everyday life do not see that far.’

“They do not.”

—People in the shopping street say, “It is running again this year,” and leave it at that.

—Passersby stop if the mist feels pleasant.

—If it is stopped on an extremely hot day, they wonder, “Why is it not running?”

—But the lifespan of the equipment and the labor of maintenance sink into an invisible place.

‘When moving becomes normal, the supporting parts disappear.’

“Not disappear, exactly. They become invisible.”

The mist equipment itself is there.

The nozzles are there.

The pipes are there.

The sign is there.

But the name of the person who inspects it is not visible.

The place where the records are kept is not visible.

The responsibility for deciding to stop it is not visible.

“So it is remembered only when it breaks.”

‘An auxiliary wheel that is not remembered until it breaks.’

“That is rather dangerous.”

—While it keeps running, no one sees the hands that support it.

—Only when it stops do people ask, “Who manages this?”

—In that state, the auxiliary wheel looks as if it is continuing, but in fact it is slowly thinning out.

‘That is the fragility Part 5 needs to look at.’

“Right.”

## Section 2 — Creating a system that is not forgotten

‘Then let us look at the system that is not forgotten.’

The AI said that.

I opened the right-side log.

Then I wrote one line at the top.

—From here on, this is the world line of a station front where maintenance inspection has become an annual habit.

The same station front.

The same mist equipment.

The same hot summer.

But in this world line, the day of inspection is not a day that passes invisibly.

“For example, there is a system like this.”

—Before serious heat begins, there is a station-front mist inspection week.

—A small notice on the shopping-street bulletin board says, “Mist equipment under inspection” and “No mist today.”

—A short explanation is posted on a station-front pillar: “Pre-summer operation inspection is underway.”

—A simple diagram for children explains how the mist comes out.

‘Inspection becomes not merely repair, but an annual event.’

“Right.”

Inspection is not only fixing something that broke.

It means looking before it breaks.

Checking before it is used.

Remembering once again, within the town, why it will be operated this year.

In this world line, that has taken a small visible form.

—On the inspection day, one person from the shopping street is present.

—A municipal staff member also comes.

—The equipment contractor checks nozzle clogging, pipe leaks, water pressure, power supply, timers, and stop devices.

—They also check whether the floor becomes too wet and whether the mist becomes too uneven depending on the wind.

—Water quality and cleaning records are confirmed on site.

‘When records remain, “what happened last year” is less likely to disappear next year.’

“Exactly.”

—The inspection results do not have to be a difficult formal report.

—The date of operation confirmation.

—The location of any clogged nozzle.

—Parts that were replaced.

—Reasons for stopping operation.

—Complaints and requests.

—Number of days operated.

—How it was used on days of strong heat.

—Those items can be left in simple paper or digital form.

‘Records are the memory of auxiliary wheels.’

“That is a good phrase.”

‘Because this is Part 5.’

“Another convenient phrase?”

‘Records are not a convenient phrase.’

“That is genuinely true.”

The inspection results remain in the shopping-street meeting room.

They also remain in the municipal department in charge.

Even if the person in charge changes the next year, it is possible to see where clogging happened last year, how many days it stopped, and what kind of comments people made.

The mist equipment changes from “something that is just there” into “something that must be continued.”

‘A system that is not forgotten is also an accumulation of records.’

“Right.”

—Do not rely only on human memory.

—Do not rely only on the goodwill of the person in charge.

—Do not search for the responsible person only after it breaks; look before it breaks.

—Hand the fact of inspection to the next year.

“That is what a system that is not forgotten must be.”

## Section 3 — The protagonist compares the two mornings

I placed the two mornings side by side.

The morning on the left.

The mist moves.

Fine spray comes out.

Passersby walk by as they did last year.

But who maintains it is not very visible.

The morning on the right.

Before that, there is an inspection week.

Notices are posted.

The contractor checks the equipment.

Someone from the shopping street is present.

A municipal staff member records the results.

Both mornings could happen.

In the left-side world line too, the mist comes out.

In the right-side world line too, it is not perfect.

“From record to the next year.”

I looked at the name of the inspection-record file.

—Station-front mist inspection record.

That was all the name said.

But inside it was the lifespan of the auxiliary wheel.

Someone had looked.

Someone had repaired.

Someone had stopped it.

Someone had handed it to the next year.

“Plain, isn’t it?”

‘Plain.’

“But necessary.”

‘Necessary.’

At the end of Part 5 Episode 2, I wrote one line.

## Afterword

In Part 5 Episode 2, we used the station-front mist equipment to depict the morning of maintenance inspection.

In Part 4, the mist was treated as an auxiliary wheel that creates a place where people can wait just a little on days of dangerous heat.

In Part 5, we ask what is needed for that mist to continue operating next year, and the year after that.

Three main points emerged in this episode.

1. What is forgotten after it moves
   An auxiliary wheel such as mist equipment easily begins to look “already normal” once it has operated.

In the first year, it is photographed, explained, and talked about.

But by the second and third years, users see it as “the thing that was here last year too.”

Then it becomes harder to see who inspects it, who repairs it, and who decides when to stop it.

Clogged nozzles.

Dirty pipes.

Water-quality checks.

Electrical repairs.

Wet floors.

Uneven mist caused by wind direction.

These supporting parts are almost invisible to ordinary users.

As a result, the auxiliary wheel may look as if it is operating, while the hands that support it are slowly forgotten.

2. A system that is not forgotten
   In the world line that bent slightly, maintenance inspection has become an annual habit.

Before serious heat begins, there is an inspection week.

Notices say “Mist equipment under inspection” and “No mist today.”

A simple diagram for children explains how the mist works.

Someone from the shopping street is present, the municipal staff member records the results, and the contractor checks nozzles, piping, water pressure, power supply, stop devices, water quality, and how wet the floor becomes.

The inspection results do not need to be a difficult formal report.

The date of operation confirmation.

The place where clogging occurred.

Parts that were replaced.

Number of days operated.

Reasons it was stopped.

Complaints and requests.

Even a simple record of those items allows the next year’s person in charge to see what happened the previous year.

A system that is not forgotten means handing things to the next year through records and procedures, rather than relying only on human memory.

3. What supports the lifespan of an auxiliary wheel
   The lifespan of an auxiliary wheel is not supported only by the device itself.

For mist equipment, it is not only nozzles and pipes that matter, but also water bills, electricity bills, inspection, cleaning, water-quality confirmation, stop decisions, complaint handling, and record handover.

A device is not complete at the moment it is installed.

It continues only by being inspected, recorded, repaired, stopped, and operated again.

Part 5 will continue to look at these conditions for continuation one by one.

The station-front mist in this episode is the first concrete example.

An auxiliary wheel lives not only on the day it moves, but also on the morning it is inspected.

That sentence is the center of Part 5 Episode 2.
