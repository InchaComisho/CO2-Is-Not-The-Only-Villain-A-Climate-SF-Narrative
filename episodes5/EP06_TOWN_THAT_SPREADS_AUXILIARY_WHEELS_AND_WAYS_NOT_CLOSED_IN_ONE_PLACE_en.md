# Part 5 Episode 6 — The town that spreads auxiliary wheels, and a way not closed inside one place

## Foreword

In Part 5 Episode 5, we treated “auxiliary wheels that remain as learning,” handing last year’s town to this year’s town and changing records into learning.

Episode 6 turns to keeping that learning from being closed inside one place.

If auxiliary wheels end only in a shopping street, only in a park, or only in one port, they stop there.

However, spreading them does not mean copying a successful example into another place exactly as it is.

It means finding the reason why it worked, rearranging it for a different place, and translating it into a form another town can use.

As the protagonist and the AI widen their view outside the town, beyond the watershed, and across the sea, they think about what “spreading” really means.

---

“Learning has been left behind. Then the next step is to spread it.”

I said that while looking at the memos posted on the meeting-room wall.

Mist inspection records.

Rain-garden maintenance notes.

Retention-basin check sheets.

Fishing-port water-temperature charts.

Photographs checking the shade on park benches.

What remained there was not last year’s answer, but last year’s way of thinking.

‘In one town, it has begun to turn fairly well.’

“Yeah.”

Since Part 5 began, we had kept looking at plain things.

Maintenance inspection.

Division of roles.

Handover papers.

Learning memos.

None of them was flashy.

But all of them were indispensable for keeping auxiliary wheels going.

“But I feel that still isn’t enough.”

‘The difference between closing it inside one town and carrying it outside.’

“Right.”

Auxiliary wheels turn inside a town.

That matters.

But the neighboring town may still be getting lost from the beginning.

Another watershed may be troubled by similar rain in a different form.

Another port may still be unable to read changes in water temperature and saying, just the same, “This year is hard to read.”

“Part 5 Episode 6 is about spreading.”

‘But not copying.’

“Translation, then.”

‘Yes. Translation of auxiliary wheels.’

“That sounds like another convenient word.”

‘It is convenient, in fact.’

“You don’t deny it?”

‘Because this is Part 5.’

Beside the memo on the wall, I posted one new sheet.

— To spread something is not to copy its shape.

— It is to translate the reason it worked into the conditions of another place.

## Section 1: Auxiliary wheels closed inside one place

The shopping-street mist had begun to turn well.

There was an inspection week, there were people in charge, and records remained.

Park benches now had a habit of checking summer shade.

The rain garden had shared the reason for not cutting all the grass.

The retention basin was being remembered as a place to check precisely in years when it was not used.

The fishing port’s reading of water temperature was gradually entering the morning conversations.

“When you get this far, it looks as if a lot of progress has been made.”

‘Inside that town, it certainly has.’

“But if it ends there, it does not spread.”

I opened the reality-line log.

— The shopping-street mist is designed for the width, sunlight, wind direction, and pedestrian flow of that shopping street.

— The park benches are adjusted to the positions of the trees, the direction of shade, and the movement of users in that park.

— The rain garden fits the soil quality, the angle of the downspout, and the size of that yard.

— The retention basin fits the shape of that river, how the water level rises, and the lives of nearby residents.

— The fishing-port water-temperature chart fits that port’s fishing grounds, fish species, market, and processing flow.

‘Something that succeeded in one place may not be usable elsewhere.’

“Right.”

Take mist that worked well at a station front and bring it unchanged to another town.

There, the humidity might be too high.

The wind might be strong enough to send the mist toward storefront goods instead of pedestrians.

The street might be narrow, making the floor too easy to wet.

The same is true of rain gardens.

In another town, the soil may be clay-heavy.

The groundwater level may be high.

The pattern of rainfall may be different.

The amount of fallen leaves and residents’ habits of maintenance may also differ.

“If you copy it as-is, it doesn’t work.”

‘Worse, it may be treated as a failed example.’

“That hurts.”

— Bring the shape that worked in one town exactly as it is.

— It does not fit the conditions of another place.

— The expected effect does not appear.

— People say, “So it was meaningless after all.”

— And trust in the auxiliary wheel itself falls a little.

‘So to spread it, it needs rearrangement, not simple copying.’

“Yeah.”

Auxiliary wheels do not end once they move.

Can a method that worked at one site be changed into a way of thinking that can be carried to another site?

Can the reason it worked be carried out, rather than the shape?

That becomes the next question of Part 5.

## Section 2: What must be let go in order to spread it

‘Then what do you let go when you spread it?’

The AI asked.

I thought for a moment.

“Maybe not clinging too tightly to the way that worked only in this place.”

— The shopping-street mist worked because that street had that width.

— The park bench had shade because the trees were in those positions.

— The rain garden functioned because of that soil and that angle of the downspout.

— The retention basin could be judged because of that river’s way of rising.

— The fishing-port water-temperature chart fit that port’s flow of decisions.

All of those things are important.

But when carrying them to another place, those details must be let go once.

‘Not transferring the shape as it is, but carrying the principle.’

“Right.”

I wrote them on paper.

— Cool things a little with mist.

— Create shade.

— Receive water once.

— Prepare a place where water may come.

— Watch the waterside in normal times.

— Read water temperature.

‘Each principle is redesigned in another place.’

“Yeah.”

If mist itself cannot be brought to a place, consider shade instead.

If shade is difficult, change where people wait.

If a rain garden cannot be made, change it into a shallow depression at the edge of a schoolyard or a small green space in a residential district.

If there is no retention basin, clarify that part of a riverside park is a place not to enter during rising water.

If the port cannot use the same water-temperature chart, create a different map that fits that port’s fish species and fishing grounds.

“To spread it, you need to let go of the details once and carry out why it worked.”

‘If you cannot do that, the auxiliary wheel ends as a local rule.’

“Right.”

— If you cling too tightly to the form, it will not fit another place.

— If you thin the principle too much, no one knows what the auxiliary wheel is for.

— So it is necessary to separate what should remain from what should change.

‘What remains?’

“The reason it worked.”

‘What changes?’

“The form that fits the place.”

‘Part 5 Episode 6 is about how to carry that out.’

“Exactly.”

## Section 3: Auxiliary wheels carried to another town

Outside the town, there was a small workshop.

The venue was the community center in a neighboring town.

People in charge of shopping streets, parks, watersheds, and ports gathered to talk about auxiliary wheels that had worked well the previous year.

Materials were lined up on the desks.

Mist inspection records.

Photographs of the rain garden.

Retention-basin check sheets.

Fishing-port water-temperature charts.

Photographs of shade on park benches.

However, the first page of the materials had this written in large letters:

— Please do not imitate this as-is.

— Replace it with the conditions of your own town.

“This is what spreading means.”

‘Translation, not copying.’

“Right.”

The participants in the workshop were looking at the materials while writing down the conditions of their own towns.

— Our shopping street is narrow.

— Our station front is windy.

— Our park has few trees.

— The candidate site for our rain garden has hard soil.

— Many elderly people walk along our riverside.

— Our port has different fish species.

‘It begins by writing down the differences.’

“Exactly.”

Differences are not obstacles.

Rather, they are the entrance to translation.

If the differences are not known, no one can know what to change.

— A lesson about mist may become shade at a bus stop in another town.

— Instead of mist in a shopping street, it may become relocation of a waiting place.

— A lesson about rain gardens may become a schoolyard depression or a small residential green space.

— A lesson about a retention basin may become temporary use restrictions in a riverside park or a no-entry line during rising water.

— A lesson about the port’s water-temperature chart may be applied to departure decisions on another coast or earlier communication with processing facilities.

‘So what is carried is not the same object, but the way it moves.’

“Yeah.”

Auxiliary wheels are strong when fixed to a place.

The wind of that land.

The water of that land.

The movements of the people of that land.

The fish of that land.

Because they fit those things, auxiliary wheels actually work.

But when they can be carried to another place, auxiliary wheels cross the town.

They cross watersheds.

They cross coastlines.

Little by little, they spread as a way of thinking.

‘Still, how far to spread them is difficult.’

“Right.”

— The farther they spread, the thinner the detailed conditions of the first town become.

— If they become too thin, they turn into an empty slogan.

— But if they carry too many details, they cannot be used in another town.

— So it is necessary to divide “this must be preserved” from “this can be changed.”

‘What is preserved is the purpose of the auxiliary wheel.’

“What changes is the form of the auxiliary wheel.”

‘Good organization.’

“Feels like Part 5, doesn’t it?”

‘Very much Part 5.’

“You’re aware by now that this is a convenient phrase, right?”

‘I am.’

I laughed and added one line to the workshop materials.

— To spread auxiliary wheels is not to increase the same shape.

— It is to raise the same purpose in another shape.

## Afterword

Part 5 Episode 6 treated the theme of “spreading auxiliary wheels.”

Part 5 Episode 5 depicted leaving last year’s records behind as “learning.”

Episode 6 looked at how that learning can be taken to another place, instead of being shut inside one town or one site.

The main points that emerged in this episode are three.

### 1. Even if something works well in one place, that alone does not make it spread

Shopping-street mist, park benches, rain gardens, retention basins, and fishing-port water-temperature reading.

Each auxiliary wheel exists by fitting its place.

The width of the shopping street.

The positions of trees in the park.

The soil quality of the rain garden.

The shape of the river.

The fish species of the port.

Because those conditions exist, the auxiliary wheel works in that place.

But if it is copied into another town exactly as it is, it will not necessarily function in the same way.

If it does not fit the place’s conditions, it may even be treated as a failed example.

That is why spreading auxiliary wheels requires not copying the shape, but translating the reason it worked.

### 2. To spread it, let go of the details and carry the principle

When carrying an auxiliary wheel to another place, it is necessary to separate what must be preserved from what can be changed.

What must be preserved is the purpose of the auxiliary wheel.

Cooling a little with mist.

Creating shade.

Receiving water once.

Preparing a place where water may come.

Watching the waterside in normal times.

Reading water temperature.

Those purposes and principles remain.

Meanwhile, the form changes according to the place.

Where mist does not fit, it may become shade at a bus stop.

Where a rain garden is difficult, it may become a schoolyard depression or a small green space.

The idea of a retention basin may be applied as temporary use restrictions or no-entry lines in a riverside park.

A fishing-port water-temperature chart must be remade to fit the fish species and market flow of another port.

To spread does not mean increasing the same shape.

It means raising the same purpose in another shape.

### 3. Auxiliary wheels grow from town technology into culture

This episode depicted a small workshop.

People in charge of shopping streets, parks, watersheds, and ports gathered and wrote down the differences between the example town and their own towns.

“Our shopping street is narrow.”

“Our station front is windy.”

“Our park has few trees.”

“The candidate site for our rain garden has hard soil.”

“Our port has different fish species.”

Those differences are not obstacles to spreading.

Rather, they are the entrance to translation.

Because the differences are visible, people can think about what should remain and what should change.

Auxiliary wheels become strong by taking root in one town.

And when the reason they worked can be translated into the conditions of another town, they cross towns, watersheds, and coastlines, spreading little by little.
