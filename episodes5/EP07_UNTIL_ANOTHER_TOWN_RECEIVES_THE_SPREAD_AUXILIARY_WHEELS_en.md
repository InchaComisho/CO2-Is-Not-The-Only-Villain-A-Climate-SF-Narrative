# Part 5 Episode 7 — Until another town receives the spread auxiliary wheels

## Foreword

In Part 5 Episode 6, we thought about auxiliary wheels that are not closed inside one place, considering how shopping streets, parks, rain gardens, retention basins, and fishing-port water-temperature readings could be carried into forms that other towns can use.

Episode 7 continues from there by depicting the town on the receiving side.

Auxiliary wheels do not end with the town that created them.

How another town sees them, and how it changes them into something of its own, completely changes the way they spread.

This episode has the protagonist and the AI look, inside a small briefing session, at the difference between the town that brings a method and the town that receives it.

---

“Spreading something isn’t only about the side that brings it, is it?”

I said that while gathering the documents on the desk.

Shopping-street mist.

A rain-garden hollow.

A retention-basin gate.

A fishing-port water-temperature chart.

Photographs of shade falling on park benches.

In Episode 6, I wrote that carrying those things into another town requires not copying their form, but translating the reason why they worked.

But if there is no one to receive that translation, nothing begins to spread.

‘It spreads because there is a receiving side.’

“Right.”

‘Even if the town that brings it is enthusiastic, it stops if the receiving town cannot make it its own.’

“That’s the point.”

The town that explains.

The town that introduces.

The town that hands over documents.

That is still only half of it.

The receiving town opens those documents.

It compares them with its own road widths, soil, river, and port.

It finds what fits and what does not.

Only then do the auxiliary wheels begin to move in another town.

“Part 5 Episode 7 is about the town that receives them.”

‘The second half of spreading.’

“The second half is plain, but important.”

‘Because this is Part 5.’

“That phrase has become a fixed pattern.”

‘A settled auxiliary wheel.’

“Don’t use it there.”

I opened the documents for a small briefing session.

## Section 1 — Not the town that operates it, but the town that receives it

People have come to see the mist at the station front.

People from another town’s shopping street.

A park-management officer.

A watershed officer.

People connected to a port.

People from a neighborhood association.

They have come to see a method that worked last year.

“But they aren’t taking it home exactly as it is.”

I opened the briefing-session log.

— Documents are handed out in the venue.

— Records from the shopping-street mist equipment.

— Rain-garden maintenance notes.

— Retention-basin operation check sheets.

— Fishing-port water-temperature charts.

— Photographs of shade on park benches.

However, those documents are not finished products.

They are not manuals saying, “If you do exactly this, you can do it too.”

What is lined up there is only a set of examples.

— In this town, it was like this.

— With this street width, we did this.

— In this soil, water collected this way.

— In this river, we used this water level as a guide.

— In this port, we looked at this fish species and this water temperature.

‘The receiving side interprets those things according to its own town.’

“Right.”

The receiving town is not a spectator.

Nor is it someone who simply applauds and goes home.

If it only says, “That is a good initiative,” and puts the documents on a shelf, nothing has spread.

— In one town, mist may be replaced not at the station front, but by shade at a bus stop.

— In another town, a rain garden may be placed not in the corner of a park, but along the edge of a schoolyard.

— In another town, the idea of a retention basin may become a temporary refuge space along the river, or a line marking where people must not enter during high water.

— In another port, a water-temperature chart may become a basic document for port meetings.

‘In other words, the receiving town is not a spectator. It is an editor.’

“Yeah.”

The town that brings it offers a draft.

The receiving town rewrites it in its own language.

Without that editing, the auxiliary wheels do not become the town’s own.

“Spreading doesn’t mean just handing something over. It includes the point where the receiving side can edit it.”

‘Exactly.’

## Section 2 — To receive it, the town must know its differences

‘Then what does the receiving town look at first?’

The AI asked.

“I think it looks at how it is different.”

I looked at the blank spaces in the briefing materials.

There were fields where participants could write about their own towns.

— How wide is our street?

— What direction does the summer wind come from?

— At what time of day is shade lacking?

— What kind of soil do we have?

— Does rain fall hard over a short time, or gently for a long time?

— How quickly does the river water level rise?

— What fish species does the port watch?

— Who makes the morning decisions?

“It starts by writing down the differences.”

‘Because a complete copy is impossible.’

“Right.”

— The street width is different.

— The soil is different.

— The way rain falls is different.

— The shape of the river is different.

— The age structure of residents is different.

— The number of people who can manage it is different.

— The way fish approach the port is also different.

If those differences are ignored and an “advanced case” is brought in exactly as it is, it will not work.

Mist may make the ground too wet.

A rain garden may fail to drain.

A retention-basin water-level guide may not fit that river.

A water-temperature chart may be hard to use for that port’s fish species.

‘That is why the receiving side first looks at the differences.’

“Differences are not reasons for failure. They are the entrance to editing.”

‘Good arrangement.’

At the briefing, the side that brought the method explains why it worked.

But the receiving side first searches for what is different in its own town.

Unless it finds that, the method cannot become its own auxiliary wheel.

‘What the receiving town needs is understanding and editing.’

“Yeah.”

— Sometimes it removes the mist and keeps only the shade.

— Sometimes it does not use shade, but moves the waiting place itself.

— It makes the rain-garden hollow smaller.

— It changes a rain garden into an infiltration strip along the edge of a schoolyard.

— It replaces retention-basin operation with temporary use restrictions in a riverside open space.

— It changes a water-temperature chart into a single sheet used at a port meeting.

‘The principle is the same, but the form changes.’

“That’s right.”

— Support people against heat a little.

— Receive water once.

— Decide where water may come.

— Watch the water in ordinary times.

— Read water temperature.

What remains is the purpose.

What changes is the form.

## Section 3 — Auxiliary wheels do not spread by themselves

The briefing session ends.

Participants take the documents home.

Beautifully printed materials.

A photo-based collection of examples.

Memos with red sticky notes attached.

“This much happens often.”

‘Up to the point of taking documents home, yes.’

“But nothing happens if it stops there.”

The documents go into bags.

They are placed on a meeting-room shelf.

The data is put into a shared folder.

That alone does not move any auxiliary wheels.

“The real work starts after they take it home.”

‘Yes.’

— Who will they talk to?

— Where will they test it?

— Who will first move their hands?

— If they start small, which place will they choose?

— If it fails, who will fix it?

— Who in that town will hold responsibility?

If those things are not decided, it does not spread.

— Auxiliary wheels that spread do not spread through observation alone.

— They do not spread just because documents are taken home.

— If it ends with “That was a good talk,” it stops there.

— Only when the receiving town makes a small version adapted to its own conditions can it be said to have begun spreading.

‘What is the receiving town’s first step?’

“Probably to test it small.”

— Not the whole shopping street, but one bus stop first.

— Not the entire park, but one bench.

— Not the whole residential district, but one small candidate plot for a rain garden.

— Not the entire watershed, but one riverside open space.

— Not the entire port, but one water-temperature memo used in a morning meeting.

‘It receives it in a small form.’

“Right.”

If a town tries to change everything at once, resistance becomes large.

There are not enough people.

The budget becomes heavy.

But if it receives it small, it can test it.

It can test it and fix the parts that do not fit.

It can show the fixed version to the next person.

That repetition becomes the beginning of spreading.

‘Part 5 Episode 7 depicts the work of the receiving side.’

“Right.”

I placed two logs side by side.

“I want to write the latter as the better auxiliary wheel.”

‘Spreading is surprisingly quiet work.’

“It isn’t flashy, but it may be the most important part.”

## Afterword

Part 5 Episode 7 depicted “until another town receives the spread auxiliary wheels.”

In Part 5 Episode 6, we arranged the idea that spreading auxiliary wheels does not mean increasing the same form, but raising the same purpose in a different form.

Episode 7 continues from there by looking at what the town on the receiving side does.

The main points that emerged in this episode are the following three.

### 1. Spreading requires editing by the receiving side

Mist, rain gardens, retention basins, water-temperature charts, and ways of reading shade on park benches.

All of them were created according to the conditions of the original town.

Street width, wind direction, soil quality, rainfall patterns, river shape, port fish species, and the movement of residents.

They functioned in that town because those conditions existed.

To carry them to another town, they cannot simply be copied.

They have to be edited according to that town’s own conditions.

The receiving town is not just a spectator.

It is an editor that translates the method into its own town.

### 2. The receiving town begins by knowing the differences

The first thing a receiving town should look at is the difference between itself and the original town.

The street width is different.

The soil is different.

The way rain falls is different.

The shape of the river is different.

The age structure of residents is different.

The number of people who can manage it is different.

The way fish approach the port is different.

Those differences are not reasons for failure.

They are the entrance to editing.

Because the differences are visible, the town can think about what to keep and what to change.

Mist may become shade.

A rain garden may be placed at the edge of a schoolyard.

The idea of a retention basin may become use restrictions for a riverside open space.

A water-temperature chart may become a document for a morning port meeting.

The principle may remain the same, but the form changes.

### 3. Auxiliary wheels become broader through quiet work

Auxiliary wheels do not spread merely because people attend a briefing session.

They do not spread merely because documents are taken home.

If it ends with “That was a good talk,” it stops there.

After bringing the documents home, who will they talk to?

Where will they test it?

Who will first move their hands?

If they begin small, which place will they choose?

If it fails, who will repair it?

Who in that town will take responsibility?

Only when those things are decided does it become the receiving town’s auxiliary wheel.

There is no need to change the entire town at once.

Start with one bus stop.

One bench.

One small candidate plot for a rain garden.

One riverside open space.

One water-temperature memo used in the port’s morning meeting.

Receive it small, test it small, and repair the parts that do not fit.

That quiet work is what makes auxiliary wheels broader.

Part 5 Episode 7 changed auxiliary wheels from “one town’s success” into “another town’s learning.”

Auxiliary wheels do not begin to spread when they are handed over, but when the receiving town begins editing them into its own form.
