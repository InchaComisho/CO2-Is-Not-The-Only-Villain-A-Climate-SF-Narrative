# Part 5 Episode 9 — Auxiliary wheels that become the town’s words, and ingenuity that is passed on

## Foreword

In Part 5 Episode 8, we followed how auxiliary wheels received from outside gradually fit the shape of a town, and saw how a borrowed mechanism is slowly translated into the town’s own words.

Episode 9 deals with what comes after that: how it is spoken about.

Auxiliary wheels are still weak if they are only used.

Only when someone can say, “In our town, we call it this,” and pass that on to the next person do those auxiliary wheels begin to approach the town’s culture.

Using a thin booklet placed in the corner of a meeting room as a clue, the protagonist and the AI think about the meaning of ingenuity that is passed on.

“Things that fit in eventually change names, don’t they?”

At first, it was mist that had worked in another town.

Next, it became mist suited to this town’s station front.

Then, after a few years—

‘It becomes the town’s own words.’

“Right.”

It is no longer “introduced equipment.”

It is no longer “the mechanism we tested last year.”

It is no longer “the method we learned from that other town.”

Before anyone notices, the people of the town begin calling it in their own words.

A mist day.

The bench whose shade shifts in the afternoon.

The rain garden where the grass is left.

The detour when the river is high.

The morning meeting with water-temperature colors.

“Becoming words is a pretty big step.”

‘The auxiliary wheel moves from the manual into conversation.’

“That’s it.”

I opened the thin booklet.

## Section 1 — What still remains as explanation

On the shelf in the meeting room, there is one thin booklet.

On the cover, written by hand on white paper, are the words, “Notes on Heat and Water Ingenuity.”

Inside, the way to operate the mist, how to leave grass in the rain garden, how to check the retention basin, how to read the water-temperature chart, and photographs of shade on park benches are all summarized simply.

“It’s a good booklet.”

‘It is useful.’

“But it is still explanation.”

I opened the log of the line where the world had not moved greatly.

—The booklet is useful.

—It explains the procedures.

—It says what needs to be checked.

—But to the reader, it still looks like a method from somewhere else.

—It has not yet become a word that naturally comes out of daily life in this town.

The booklet is not bad.

In fact, it is necessary.

Without records, the next person gets lost.

Without photographs, the movement of shade is hard to understand.

Without diagrams, the meaning of the rain garden or retention basin is hard to convey.

But the booklet alone is not enough.

‘It is not culture yet.’

“Right.”

—People in the shopping street say, “That mist thing.”

—People in the park say, “That bench-position adjustment from last year.”

—The rain-garden caretaker says, “That thing where we leave the hollow.”

—When explaining the retention basin, someone says, “That operation we saw in the materials before.”

—At the port, it is called “the water-temperature chart attempt.”

None of that is wrong.

But it still leans toward explanation.

It has not yet become the town’s own tone of speech.

‘So the auxiliary wheels are being used, and yet they have not fully become words that can be passed on.’

“Exactly.”

They are being used.

There are records.

They are handed over.

And yet they still sit a little outside the town.

If someone opens the booklet, they understand.

But unless the booklet is opened, the words do not come out.

At that stage, the auxiliary wheels have not fully entered the inside of the town.

## Section 2 — The moment they become the town’s words

‘Then let us look at the moment when they become the town’s words.’

The AI said.

I opened the log on the right.

Then I wrote one line at the beginning.

—From here, this is the world line of a town where explanations have become turns of phrase.

A summer morning.

Someone in the shopping street says:

“Today is a mist day.”

They are not talking about an event.

They are talking about how to spend a dangerously hot day.

On a morning when a heatstroke alert seems likely, humidity and wind are checked, and a fine mist is released for a short time.

That has become the town’s words.

—“Today is a mist day.”

—“The wind is strong, so it will probably be short today.”

—“That spot gets wet easily, so they might stop it.”

This is not equipment explanation.

It is the speech of a town that knows how to use it.

In the park, a parent with a child says:

“That bench loses its shade in the afternoon, so let’s sit over here.”

That is the language of a town that understands the placement of its auxiliary wheels.

—It knows when shade is present.

—It knows which places to avoid during hot hours.

—It knows which bench is easy to use at which time.

At the rain garden, an older person says:

“It’s better to leave this grass, because the water moves better that way.”

That is a way of speaking where learning has entered daily life.

—Do not cut everything.

—But remove grass that blocks the outlet.

—If water remains too long, check for clogging.

Those judgments have slipped into town conversation instead of remaining specialized terms.

Near the retention basin, someone says while walking:

“The river is a little high today, so let’s go around that way.”

At the port, someone looks at the water-temperature chart and says:

“With this color, it might be better to delay departure a little.”

‘All of them have become not explanations, but everyday speech.’

“Right.”

At this point, the auxiliary wheels are no longer only inside the booklet.

They are in conversations.

They are in morning greetings.

They are in the movement of parents and children.

They are in the way people handle grass.

They are in one comment during the port’s morning meeting.

—When auxiliary wheels become the town’s words, they are shared naturally in conversation even without opening the booklet.

—That is not merely convenient.

—It also becomes a kind of self-introduction: this is how this town lives with heat and water.

‘A town’s self-introduction.’

“It sounds a little grand, but I think that is what it is.”

‘Because this is Part 5.’

“There it is again.”

‘This time it fits.’

“I’ll admit that.”

Auxiliary wheels that have become the town’s words begin to express the town’s character.

This town does not overdo things on hot days.

This town remembers how shade moves.

This town does not cut all the grass in the rain garden.

This town does not go near the river when the water is high.

This port brings water temperature into morning conversation.

Those ways of speaking remain in the town little by little.

## Section 3 — Ingenuity that is passed on

“But becoming words is still not enough.”

I said while turning the pages of the booklet.

‘Why not?’

“Because words are sometimes forgotten.”

Things that become the town’s words are strong.

But they are not eternal.

People change.

Children grow up.

People in charge are replaced.

Shops in the shopping street change.

Young people at the port also change.

—Even if something once becomes the town’s words, a new generation will need an explanation.

—If fewer people know what the mist means, it looks like ordinary mist.

—The hollow of the rain garden looks like design.

—The open area of the retention basin looks like empty land.

—The water-temperature chart looks like a sheet of colored paper.

‘That is why it has to be passed on.’

“Right.”

Passing it on does not mean preserving it only through oral stories.

If it relies only on sayings, it gradually fades.

If it relies only on records, it sleeps on a shelf.

So both are necessary.

—A thin booklet.

—Simple diagrams.

—Photographs from different seasons.

—Signs at the site.

—A small explanation session before summer.

—Short talks for children.

—Handover when the person in charge changes.

—Last year’s mistakes and this year’s corrections.

Only when these are combined can the auxiliary wheel be handed to the next person.

‘To bring auxiliary wheels closer to culture, both words and records are necessary.’

“Right.”

In this town, there are people who can look at the mist and say, “Ah, it’s that time again.”

But before that “ah,” someone spent years explaining, failing, correcting, and rephrasing it.

At first, it was material.

Then it became procedure.

Then it became learning.

It was received, fit into the town, and became the town’s words.

Now it is time to pass those words to the next person.

‘Part 5 Episode 9 is about that history of rephrasing.’

“Exactly.”

—This is the log of auxiliary wheels that still remain as explanation.

“I want to write the latter as the better auxiliary wheel.”

‘Ingenuity that is passed on is not flashy.’

‘That is its strength.’
