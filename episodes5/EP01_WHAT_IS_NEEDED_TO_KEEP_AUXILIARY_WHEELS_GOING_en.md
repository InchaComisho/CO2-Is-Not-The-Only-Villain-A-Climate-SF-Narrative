# Part 5 Episode 1 — What is needed to keep auxiliary wheels going?

## Foreword

In Part 4, we watched the “world line that continued as it was” and the “world line that bent slightly” side by side, following how auxiliary wheels entered different places in cities, watersheds, and the sea.

In Part 5, the focus shifts to how those auxiliary wheels can be continued, widened, and preserved.

Episode 1 has the protagonist and the AI look again at the reality that inevitably appears after an auxiliary wheel moves once: budgets, people, institutions, maintenance, records, and the speed at which things are forgotten.

---

“After finishing Part 4, what do you think comes next?”

After closing the folder, I asked through the screen.

‘The conditions for continuing them, I suppose.’

“Yeah.”

Mist at the station front.

Assumption infrastructure in the shopping street.

Park benches.

Signs at the community center.

A reed screen on the balcony.

Bands of grass upstream.

A rain garden midstream.

A retention basin downstream.

A water-temperature chart at the fishing port.

All of them were auxiliary wheels supporting a future that bent only slightly.

But moving once does not mean the work is over.

‘Keeping them moving is harder.’

“That’s the problem.”

Introduction is still easy to turn into a story.

It started.

It was built.

It was installed.

It changed something.

Write that, and a shape appears.

But the truly troublesome part comes after that.

Will it move next year?

Will it still remain the year after that?

Will it continue after the person in charge changes?

Will it survive when the budget becomes thinner?

“Part 5 is the part that looks at that.”

‘The part that looks at how to keep what has reached a place from disappearing.’

“Exactly the folder we made at the end of Part 4.”

I opened a new folder.

— Part 5: Conditions for continuing

Inside, it was still blank.

## Section 1 — Auxiliary wheels become fragile after they move

“First, I want to look at this properly.”

I looked at the notes on my desk.

It moved.

It entered.

It bent slightly.

That is not the end.

‘This is the maintenance issue.’

“Right.”

I opened the station-front log.

— In the first year, station-front mist becomes a topic.

— People take photos.

— It may even appear in the news.

— Users may say, “It became a little easier to wait this year.”

‘The year of introduction is visible.’

“But from the second year onward, it becomes harder to see.”

— In the second year, people say, “It was there last year too.”

— In the third year, it becomes “normal.”

— In the fourth year, clogged nozzles and inspection costs start to stand out more.

— By the fifth year, the budget officer may already have changed.

‘Because maintenance is less visible than introduction.’

“The watershed is the same.”

I opened the rain-garden and retention-basin logs.

— In the year it is made, a rain garden is photographed.

— It is explained as a system that receives rain once in the garden.

— But after several years, grass grows long, soil clogs, and fallen leaves accumulate.

— If the first person in charge is gone, it becomes harder to know who should fix the flow when it worsens.

The retention basin is the same.

— When the retention basin is built, it is carefully explained.

— Diagrams are distributed: “This is a place that temporarily accepts water during heavy rain.”

— But if no water enters it for many years, it gradually disappears from residents’ awareness.

— Before anyone notices, it is seen only as a wide grass field or a walking course.

‘When years pass without use, people forget why it is needed.’

“But the years when it is not used are exactly when we must not forget it.”

‘Why?’

“Because a year when it did not have to be used and a year when preparation is unnecessary are not the same thing.”

The AI was silent for a moment.

Then it answered briefly.

‘Good distinction.’

“The sea is probably the same.”

I opened the fishing-port log.

— At first, reading water temperature at the fishing port is simply “useful.”

— In a year when departures are adjusted by looking at a sea-surface temperature chart, the results may happen to be good.

— Some days, earlier preparation of ice and refrigeration helps.

— But to continue that the next year, someone must update the chart, explain it, use it, and trust it.

‘In other words, the hard part is not installing an auxiliary wheel, but not forgetting it.’

“Exactly.”

Once an auxiliary wheel moves, the time it spends quietly continuing is longer than the moment when it first moved.

And the quieter that time is, the more easily it is forgotten.

## Section 2 — How to resist the speed of forgetting

‘That is the first issue for Part 5.’

“Auxiliary wheels do not continue automatically just because they moved once.”

I thought for a moment and said:

“People turn useful things into things that are assumed to be there.”

‘Yes.’

“Once something becomes assumed, the reason for updating it becomes harder to see.”

‘Yes.’

“When that reason becomes harder to see, the budget is easier to cut.”

‘Even though climate change itself is still continuing.’

“Exactly.”

— Mist is not unnecessary because heat has weakened.

— Rain gardens and retention basins are not unnecessary because heavy rain has decreased.

— Reading water temperature is not unnecessary because the sea has become calm.

— But when a year passes without damage, the memory that remains most easily is simply, “This year was fine.”

‘Institutions often stop under the words, “It seems fine this year.”’

“That is why we need words that keep auxiliary wheels going.”

‘Words?’

“Right.”

I wrote several words in my notes.

Disaster prevention.

Heat countermeasures.

Commercial vitality.

Watershed management.

Support for fisheries.

Quality of life.

Observation.

Maintenance.

Handover.

— “Disaster prevention” alone does not easily enter everyday life.

— “Vitality” alone weakens the necessity on dangerously hot days.

— “Observation” alone does not easily reach daily life.

— “Environmental measures” alone do not easily connect to daily budgets and people.

‘To continue, several names are needed.’

“Right.”

— Station-front mist is a tool for vitality, a heat countermeasure, and infrastructure that supports movement.

— A rain garden is a garden, a watershed auxiliary wheel, and a teaching tool for watching rain.

— A retention basin is an open space, a place that accepts water, and a place to learn evacuation judgment.

— A fishing port’s water-temperature chart is observation material, a fishing decision tool, and information that supports daily life.

‘One name cannot support it all.’

“Probably not.”

To continue an auxiliary wheel, a simple name is not enough.

Not only disaster prevention.

Not only vitality.

Not only environment.

Not only observation.

A language is needed that does not show them as separate pieces, but explains them as systems that support daily life.

“To resist the speed of forgetting, we have to keep rephrasing what it exists for.”

‘A very Part 5 theme.’

## Section 3 — Auxiliary wheels are supported by more than devices

“I want to look at this in Part 5 too.”

I remembered the photo of station-front mist.

‘The device itself?’

“No. The things around the device.”

The nozzle produces the mist.

But a nozzle alone cannot keep mist going.

— There must be a budget to pay for electricity.

— Water costs must be anticipated.

— Someone must inspect the nozzles.

— Someone must check water quality and hygiene.

— Someone must decide to stop operation when the ground becomes too wet.

— There must be a contact point for voices like “I do not want to get wet” and “It is hot, make it stronger.”

‘Around the equipment, there are people and systems.’

“Exactly.”

The rain garden is the same.

— Soil alone does not maintain a rain garden.

— Someone must cut the grass.

— Someone must remove fallen leaves.

— Someone must check whether water remains too long.

— If the soil hardens, someone must adjust it.

— Someone must explain, “This is not just a weedy garden; it is a garden that receives rain.”

The retention basin is the same.

— A gate alone does not make a retention basin work.

— Someone must announce restrictions before heavy rain.

— Information is needed to support the decision to let water in.

— After the water recedes, someone must remove mud and garbage.

— Rules are needed to balance everyday use and emergency use.

‘At sea, someone updates the chart and someone explains it.’

“Right.”

— Someone must update the water-temperature chart.

— Someone must turn observations into a form that can be read.

— Someone must connect fishermen’s experience with observation data.

— Someone must not end by blaming a wrong forecast, but turn it into the next judgment.

— Someone must tell markets and processing plants early.

‘An auxiliary wheel does not end with one invention.’

“It continues only when there are people who use it, support it, explain it, and repair it.”

I wrote one line in my notebook.

— The body of an auxiliary wheel is not the device alone.

— The procedures, people, and records around it are also part of the auxiliary wheel.

‘So that is what Part 5 depicts.’

“Right.”

— If Part 4 asked how far auxiliary wheels can reach, Part 5 asks how they can continue.

— Continuation needs institutions.

— It needs people.

— It needs consent.

— It needs records.

— It needs updates.

— And it needs systems that make forgetting harder.

## Afterword

In Part 5 Episode 1, the focus shifted from auxiliary wheels that moved once in Part 4 to the question of how to continue, widen, and preserve them.

In Part 4, we watched how far auxiliary wheels could reach in cities, watersheds, and the sea.

But an auxiliary wheel does not end once it is installed or once it moves.

The truly difficult part comes after that.

This episode organized three main points.

1. Auxiliary wheels are harder to maintain than to introduce.
   Mist systems, rain gardens, retention basins, and water-temperature reading at fishing ports do not end once they move.

To continue the next year, they require inspection, updates, cleaning, explanation, budgets, and people.

The year of introduction is visible.

It remains in photos.

It can easily become news.

But in the second and third years, it becomes “normal.”

Once something becomes normal, the reason to update it becomes harder to see.

When that reason becomes harder to see, budgets and people are more easily cut.

Auxiliary wheels become fragile after they move.

Part 5 is also the part that looks at that fragility.

2. To continue, they need names and explanations.
   To keep auxiliary wheels going, people must repeatedly re-explain what they are for.

Disaster prevention alone does not easily enter everyday life.

Vitality alone weakens the necessity on dangerously hot days.

Observation alone does not easily reach people’s lives.

Environmental measures alone do not easily connect to daily budgets and people.

Station-front mist is a tool for vitality, a heat countermeasure, and infrastructure that supports movement.

A rain garden is a garden, a watershed auxiliary wheel, and a teaching tool for watching rain.

A retention basin is an open space, a place that accepts water, and a place for learning evacuation judgment.

A fishing port’s water-temperature chart is observation material, a fishing decision tool, and information that supports daily life.

One name alone cannot support an auxiliary wheel.

That is why each place needs words that repeatedly explain what it exists for.

3. Auxiliary wheels are supported not only by devices, but by people and systems.
   Nozzles, soil, gates, and charts matter.

But they alone cannot keep auxiliary wheels going.

Mist needs water costs, electricity costs, inspection, hygiene management, complaint response, and stop decisions.

Rain gardens need mowing, leaf removal, people who watch how water stays, and people who repair soil.

Retention basins need announcements of restrictions, decisions about when to let water in, cleanup after the water recedes, and coordination with daily use.

Water-temperature reading at fishing ports needs people who update charts, make observations understandable, connect fishermen’s experience with data, and communicate with markets and processing plants.

The body of an auxiliary wheel is not the device alone.

The procedures, people, records, explanations, and updates around it are also part of the auxiliary wheel.

If Part 4 was “the part that witnesses the range they can reach,” Part 5 is “the part that looks at how what reached a place can be preserved.”

From here, the protagonist and the AI will follow the lifespan and updating of auxiliary wheels in cities, watersheds, and the sea.

Will the station-front mist work next year?

Will the rain garden be repaired when fallen leaves clog it?

Will the retention basin be remembered even in years when it is not used?

Will the fishing port keep updating its water-temperature chart?

The protagonist and AI will follow those plain questions one by one.
