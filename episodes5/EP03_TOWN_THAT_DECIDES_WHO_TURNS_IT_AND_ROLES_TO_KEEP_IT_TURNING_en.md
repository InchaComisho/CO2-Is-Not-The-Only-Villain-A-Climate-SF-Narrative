# Part 5 Episode 3 — The town that decides who turns it, and the roles that keep it turning

## Foreword

In Part 5 Episode 1, we shifted the auxiliary wheels into the perspective of how to continue them, how to widen them, and how to preserve them. In Episode 2, through the morning of maintenance inspection, we looked at “what is forgotten after it moves” and “a system that is not forgotten.”

Episode 3 deals with what comes next: the roles of people.

Auxiliary wheels do not continue through devices alone.

Unless it is decided who turns them, who watches them, and who hands them to the next person, a future that bent slightly will quietly return to the original world line before anyone notices.

This episode has the protagonist and the AI think about the visibility of “the people who turn it” and “the people who support it,” using a small town that connects a shopping street and a park as the stage.

“Once we talk about inspections, the next thing is people.”

I said that while looking at the notes on my desk.

Mist nozzles.

Mowing the rain garden.

Records for the retention basin.

The water-temperature chart at the fishing port.

None of them keeps turning through devices alone.

‘Who turns them, then.’

“Right.”

In Part 5 Episode 1, I wrote that conditions are needed for auxiliary wheels to continue.

In Episode 2, we looked at the morning of maintenance inspection.

Then who watches over that inspection?

Who leaves the records?

Who hands them to next year’s person in charge?

If that remains vague, auxiliary wheels quietly stop.

“Episode 3 is about people’s roles.”

‘Plain, but impossible to avoid.’

“Part 5 is full of plain topics.”

‘Because it is about continuing.’

“Do not say that like a convenient phrase.”

‘It is a fact.’

I opened a map of a small town where a shopping street and a park sat beside each other.

## Section 1 — The town where the person who turns it cannot be seen in the world line that did not move much on a large scale

The station-front mist exists, at least as shared equipment for the shopping street.

The position of the park benches does not change easily either.

The rain garden, the retention basin, and the fishing port’s water-temperature reading all exist by name.

“But it is hard to see who is actually turning them.”

I opened the reality-line log.

— For residents, auxiliary wheels have become “things that are there.”

— Mist is something that appears on hot days.

— Benches are something that exist in the park.

— A rain garden is a slightly unusual garden.

— A retention basin is a wide grassy area.

— A water-temperature chart is a sheet of paper posted in the port office.

But the people behind them are hard to see.

— The person who inspects.

— The person who records.

— The person who explains.

— The person who arranges the schedule.

— The person who decides when to stop it.

— The person who hands it to the next person in charge.

Those people do not appear on the surface.

The better things are running, the less visible they become.

‘If it looks as though nobody is turning it, people may even think that is natural.’

“That is exactly it.”

The better a piece of infrastructure runs, the less users notice it.

Mist comes out.

Shade is there.

Grass has been cut.

Water-level information is updated.

The paper at the port has been replaced.

The more natural these things look, the thinner the figures of the people supporting them become.

— In the shopping street, nobody notices when the person in charge of the mist changes.

— In the park, when summer shade shifts away from the benches, nobody knows whom to tell.

— In the rain garden, when grass grows tall, the difference between grass to cut and grass to leave is not shared.

— In the retention basin, the number of people who can explain both everyday use and emergency function gradually decreases.

— At the fishing port, after the person who made the water-temperature chart is transferred, the update frequency slowly drops.

‘If that happens, it gradually stops.’

“It stops.”

And it does not stop all at once.

One year, inspection is delayed a little.

The next year, the records become a little rough.

The year after that, the person in charge changes.

Another year later, someone asks, “Whose job was this again?”

And before anyone realizes it, the auxiliary wheel comes closer to the original world line.

— A future that should have bent slightly quietly returns.

— The device remains, but the role disappears.

— The shape is still there, but the person who turns it can no longer be seen.

‘An auxiliary wheel stops when its role disappears.’

“Right.”

“That is why we need to decide who turns it.”

## Section 2 — The town where the person who turns it can be seen in the world line that bent slightly

‘Then let us look at the town where the person who turns it can be seen.’

The AI said.

I opened the log on the right.

And I wrote one line at the beginning.

— From here, this is the world line of a town that has decided from the beginning who turns each auxiliary wheel.

The same shopping street.

The same park.

The same rain garden.

The same retention basin.

The same fishing port.

But in this world line, each auxiliary wheel is tied to “the person who turns it,” “the person who watches it,” and “the person who hands it over.”

“For example, for the shopping-street mist.”

— Within the shopping street, there is a small role called the mist coordinator.

— On a yearly rotation, that person handles inspection-week notices, checks during use, and the handover of records.

— The role is not held by one person alone; there is also a deputy.

— At the end of the year, the coordinator briefly summarizes operating days, reasons for stopping, complaints and requests, and replaced parts, then passes that to the next coordinator.

‘So the responsibility is not pushed onto one person.’

“That part matters.”

— Making the person who turns it visible does not mean making one person bear all responsibility.

— It means deciding who mainly watches it.

— It means deciding whom people can consult when there is trouble.

— It means deciding how to hand it to the next person.

‘The person who turns it is also a person who explains it.’

“Right.”

The shopping-street coordinator does not only touch machines.

“This mist is not only a decoration for liveliness.”

“It is equipment for creating a place where people can wait for a few minutes on dangerously hot days.”

“Depending on humidity and wind, there are days when it must be stopped.”

They can explain it that way.

Because those words exist, the mist stops being “somehow, mist is coming out.”

Next, the park.

— In the park, there is a liaison between the neighborhood association and park management.

— Before summer, that person checks what time of day shade falls on the benches.

— They check whether tree growth or pruning has changed the position of the shade.

— If necessary, they consult park management about bench placement or additional shade.

‘So even the park bench has someone who watches it.’

“Right.”

A bench is not finished once it is placed.

Shade moves with the season.

Trees grow.

If they are pruned, shade decreases.

If a new building appears nearby, wind and light can change as well.

So someone watches this year too, instead of stopping at “it worked last year.”

Next, the rain garden.

— The rain garden has a caretaker.

— But that person is not “someone who cuts all the grass.”

— They know the difference between grass to leave, grass to cut, and plants to remove.

— They remove leaves, watch how water pools, and check whether the soil has become too compacted.

— They can explain to neighbors, “This is not a neglected garden; it is a garden that receives rain once.”

‘Rain gardens are easy to judge by appearance alone.’

“That is the issue.”

If grass remains, it can look like poor maintenance.

If water pools, it can look like just a puddle.

But if someone who understands the role is there, they can say, “This grass is being left intentionally,” or “This shallow depression receives water once.”

When someone can explain it, the auxiliary wheel is less likely to be misunderstood.

‘What about the retention basin?’

“The retention basin is built into the watershed contact network.”

— Someone watches the water-level guide marks.

— Someone announces use restrictions for the retention basin.

— Someone gathers information to support the decision to let water in.

— Someone explains that decision to the community in simple words.

— After the water recedes, someone records what was watched and how the decision was made.

‘The people who decide, the people who communicate, and the people who record are separate.’

“Because if one person does everything, it does not continue.”

‘The fishing port’s water-temperature reading is the same.’

“Yeah.”

— At the fishing port, someone updates the water-temperature chart.

— Someone turns observation values into a simple chart usable at the port.

— Someone talks through that chart at the morning meeting.

— Someone records the gap between fishers’ experience and observation data.

— Changes to departure, sorting, and preparation are not left entirely to one person’s intuition.

‘Auxiliary wheels continue more easily when the people who turn them can be seen.’

“That is exactly it.”

In a town where the people who turn them can be seen, auxiliary wheels are not just equipment.

They become a bundle of roles.

Who watches?

Who informs people?

Who records?

Who hands it to the next person?

When those lines are visible, auxiliary wheels have a place inside the town.

## Section 3 — When roles are visible, auxiliary wheels become the town’s own work

After thinking for a moment, I said:

“In a town where the people who turn them cannot be seen, auxiliary wheels remain ‘somebody’s job.’”

‘Yes.’

“But in a town where the people who turn them can be seen, they become ‘the town’s job.’”

‘That is a major difference.’

— Mist becomes the work of the shopping street.

— Benches become the work of the park and the neighborhood association.

— Rain gardens become the work of the garden owner and the neighborhood.

— Retention basins become the work of the watershed contact network.

— Water-temperature charts become the work of the port.

“If auxiliary wheels are turned only by personal goodwill, they do not continue.”

‘Right.’

“Goodwill matters, but if everything is placed on goodwill alone, it stops the moment that person becomes tired.”

‘It stops when the person in charge is transferred.’

“It stops when they move away.”

‘It stops when they become ill.’

“It stops when they get busy.”

I smiled bitterly.

Reality often stops for reasons like that.

Not because of some grand ideology.

Because of small exhaustion.

Because of an ordinary transfer.

Because a handover was missed.

Without anyone becoming a villain, auxiliary wheels quietly come off.

‘That is why roles need to be made visible.’

“Right.”

— When roles are visible, responsibility can be shared.

— When responsibility can be shared, records remain.

— When records remain, handover becomes possible.

— When handover becomes possible, things are harder to forget.

— When they are harder to forget, people can return to them next year.

‘A future that bent slightly is not made by one special person working hard for one moment.’

“It remains when daily roles slowly become the town’s habit.”

‘This episode is about socializing auxiliary wheels.’

“I think we can say that.”

Socializing auxiliary wheels.

The phrase sounds a little stiff.

But the meaning is simple.

Do not lock them inside one person’s goodwill.

Do not lock them inside the device alone.

The people of the town know, even a little, who is watching this, where to speak if something goes wrong, and who will receive it next year.

That alone changes the lifespan of an auxiliary wheel.

## Afterword

Part 5 Episode 3 focused on the roles of people: who turns the auxiliary wheels.

In Part 5 Episode 1, we organized the idea that continuing auxiliary wheels requires attention to budgets, people, institutions, maintenance, records, and the speed at which things are forgotten.

In Episode 2, through maintenance inspection of the station-front mist, we described how an auxiliary wheel lives not only on the day it moves, but also on the morning it is inspected.

In Episode 3, the focus moves from that maintenance and recordkeeping to the human roles that carry them.

The main points that appeared in this episode are three.

1. If the person who turns it cannot be seen, the auxiliary wheel gradually stops
   When it is unclear whose work the mist, benches, rain gardens, retention basins, and fishing-port water-temperature charts are, they may appear to be running well, but handover becomes fragile.

Especially in years when things are running smoothly, the people supporting them do not appear on the surface.

Mist comes out.

Shade falls on the bench.

Grass remains in the rain garden.

Retention-basin information is updated.

The water-temperature chart is replaced.

The more natural these things look, the harder it becomes to see who is supporting them.

As a result, transfers, moves, exhaustion, busyness, and missed handovers make auxiliary wheels stop little by little.

A future that bent slightly does not break suddenly. It quietly returns to the original world line when roles become invisible.

2. When the person who turns it can be seen, the auxiliary wheel becomes the town’s own work
   In the world line that bent slightly, each auxiliary wheel is tied to people who turn it, watch it, record it, communicate it, and hand it over.

The shopping-street mist has a coordinator and deputy coordinator who handle inspection-week notices, checks during use, and handover of records.

The park has a liaison who checks bench shade and tree growth, then consults park management.

The rain garden has someone who knows the difference between grass to leave, grass to cut, and plants to remove, and who can explain, “This is a garden that receives rain.”

The retention basin has people who watch water-level indicators, announce use restrictions, communicate decisions to the region, and leave records.

The fishing port has people who update water-temperature charts, make observation values usable at the port, and record the gap between fishers’ experience and data.

When roles are visible, the auxiliary wheel is no longer “somebody’s job”; it becomes “the town’s job.”

When it becomes the town’s job, responsibility can be shared.

When responsibility is shared, records remain.

When records remain, handover becomes possible.

When handover is possible, forgetting becomes harder.

3. Auxiliary wheels continue through socialization more than through devices alone
   The axis of Part 5 is not only the performance of auxiliary wheels themselves.

It is who turns them, how they are shared, and how they are handed over.

When auxiliary wheels run only on personal goodwill, they do not continue.

Goodwill matters.

But if everything depends on goodwill alone, the system stops when that person gets tired, is transferred, moves away, or becomes busy.

To keep the future that bent slightly from ending as a one-time event, auxiliary wheels must become part of the town’s system.

That is the “socialization of auxiliary wheels” described in this episode.

Auxiliary wheels are not turned by a single hero.

There is a person who turns them, people who support them, people who stop them, people who record them, and people who hand them to the next person.

When the town itself receives those roles, auxiliary wheels begin to continue for a long time.

Part 5 Episode 3 connects to the next episode as the point where auxiliary wheels begin to move from “someone’s goodwill work” into “the town’s role.”
