# Part 4 Episode 7 — Upstream slopes, fields that remember how they collapsed, and fields that still forget

## Foreword

By the end of Part 4 Episode 6, we had compared many places in the urban summer—station fronts, shopping streets, parks, community centers, and balconies—as the world line where little changed on a large scale and the world line that bent slightly.

In Episode 7, the viewpoint moves from the city to the watershed, using upstream slopes and fields as the stage.

In the world line where little changed on a large scale, there are slopes and fields where the memory of how they collapsed remains vague.

In the world line that bent slightly, there are slopes and fields that remember how they collapsed and then move the soil closer to a sponge.

This episode has the protagonist and the AI visit the upstream area once before the summer downpours, to consider whether the memory of collapse can become an auxiliary wheel.

## Section 1 — The slope in the world line where little changed, vaguely remembering how it collapsed

About an hour by car toward the upper river.

Between the mountains and the paddies, there is a slope that people wonder about every year: will it collapse again?

— During a heavy rain a few years earlier, part of this slope gave way.

— Some of the earth spilled onto the prefectural road, and an early-morning news ticker reported that the road was closed.

— That year, one residents’ briefing was held.

“A slope whose name even appeared on the news once, more or less.”

‘And what happened after that?’

“In the world line where little changed on a large scale, probably something like this.”

I opened the reality-line log.

— A few years have passed, and the slope looks stable from the outside.

— In the field above it, cultivation continues in much the same way as before.

— The shape of the ridges and the way grass is cut remain almost unchanged, because that is how it has always been done.

— Before rain, the owner looks at the sky and ends the work early by experience and instinct.

“That isn’t bad in itself.”

‘Experience matters.’

“But it has not become a record.”

— Photographs from the year of the collapse remain in a municipal file.

— In the memories of the people who live there, fragments remain: that year was terrible.

— But the details—where water gathered, which part of the soil began moving first, where grass and roots remained—have not been put clearly into words.

‘The memory of collapse remains only as a rough recollection.’

“Yeah.”

Whenever heavy rain comes, people passing the slope say:

“I hope it doesn’t collapse again.”

The field owner looks at the color of the clouds and says:

“I should stop early today.”

That judgment is not wrong.

In fact, as a sense held by people on site, it is important.

But the information from the year of the collapse has not moved sufficiently into the structure of the slope or the way the soil is handled.

— Which ridge became a water pathway?

— Where did the soil loosen all at once?

— Which band of grass held back a little soil?

— Those things remain disconnected from the next year’s cultivation, and another summer arrives.

‘This is a world line with almost no auxiliary wheels.’

“They remember that it collapsed.”

‘Yes.’

“But the slope and field do not remember how it collapsed.”

It did not feel so much like forgetting.

It felt closer to not yet having a form that could remember.

## Section 2 — The sponge field in the world line that bent slightly and remembers how it collapsed

‘Then let us look at the slope and field in the world line that bent slightly.’

The AI says this.

I opened the right-side log.

Then I wrote one line first.

— From here begins the log of a world line where the slope and field remember how they collapsed and then move the soil closer to a sponge.

The same upper river.

The same slope.

The same kind of field sitting above it.

However, in this world line, after the collapse, people recorded how it collapsed, and then the way the field was handled changed a little.

“For example, a world where something like this happened.”

— In the year the slope collapsed under heavy rain, a municipal staff member, local residents, and researchers from a university some distance away walked the slope together.

— They recorded, with photographs and a hand-drawn map, where the soil first began moving, which paths the water took, and where grass remained or bare soil had been exposed.

— Based on that record, they made rough rules: leave grass in this band, change the direction of this ridge slightly so water can escape, and avoid breaking up the soil too finely in this section.

‘So they did not carry out full construction. They changed the way they cultivated and left grass.’

“Right.”

This is not large-scale erosion-control work.

It is not a plan to cover everything in concrete.

Of course, depending on the risk level, some places need full-scale countermeasures.

But what this world line is looking at is the small auxiliary wheel that comes before that.

— Along channels where water gathers easily, a band of grass is left.

— In places where soil tends to become bare, cover crops are introduced.

— The direction of the ridges is adjusted slightly so water running over the field does not rush straight downward all at once.

— Some areas are not over-tilled, so air and water pathways remain inside the soil.

‘The sponge field appeared in Part 3.’

“Yeah.”

— Leaving spaces in the soil where water can be received.

— Moving the soil closer to loose aggregates through roots and organic matter.

— Not leaving the surface bare, so the impact of raindrops is absorbed a little.

— In this field, those practices came to be called moving closer to a sponge.

‘But we cannot say that making it sponge-like prevents collapse.’

“I won’t say that.”

— During the next heavy rain, the slope still shook.

— Some soil still flowed.

— Muddy patches formed at the edge of the field.

— Even so, the soil did not drop from exactly the same place with the same force as before.

— The grass band divided the flow of water a little.

— In covered areas, more of the soil surface remained than before.

— Not everything was protected, but a record remained that allowed people to say the way it collapsed may have changed a little.

‘Who remembers that?’

“In this world line, the field owner remembers, the municipality remembers, and the map remembers.”

— The field owner says:

“That year, we decided to leave grass here.”

— The municipal staff member adds a small note to the edge of the watershed map:

“Upstream slope, section where grass band was left. Surface-flow traces observed after heavy rain. Expansion of previous collapse area limited.”

— The researcher lines up photographs and says:

“It is not enough to prove an effect. But it is meaningful to leave it as the next observation point.”

‘A world line where the record of how collapse happened enters the hands that work the next year.’

“Exactly.”

This field has not become completely safe.

But information from the previous failure has moved slightly into the way the soil, grass, and ridges are handled.

That is the world line where the upstream auxiliary wheel has begun to work.

## Afterword

Part 4 Episode 7 used upstream slopes and fields as the stage, comparing the world line where little changed on a large scale, with slopes and fields whose memory of collapse remained vague, and the world line that bent slightly, with slopes and fields that remembered how they collapsed and then moved the soil closer to a sponge.

In the world line where little changed, the memory of the year when heavy rain collapsed the slope remains.

The road closure was reported on the news, and a residents’ briefing was held once.

But afterward, there was no major change in how the field was cultivated, how grass was left, or how the ridges faced.

The memory that that year was terrible remains, but the information about where water came from and where soil moved does not sufficiently move into the way the field is handled in the following years.

Whenever heavy rain comes, people worry that it may collapse again.

They look at the sky and, by experience and instinct, stop work early.

That is an important judgment on site.

But if the memory of collapse does not become a record, and the record does not become a change in cultivation, it remains weak as an auxiliary wheel.

In the world line that bent slightly, after the collapse, municipal staff, local residents, and researchers walk the slope.

Where did the soil begin moving?

Which paths did the water take?

Where did grass remain, and where was the soil exposed?

They leave those things in photographs and a hand-drawn map.

Based on that record, they decide where to leave grass bands, where to plant cover crops, where not to break the soil too finely, and where to change the direction of ridges slightly.

This is not large-scale erosion-control construction.

Nor is it a technology that makes the slope completely safe.

Depending on the level of danger, some places may need full-scale construction or evacuation decisions.

Even so, it is possible to change the way soil, grass, and water are handled a little, based on the previous pattern of collapse.

During the next heavy rain, the slope still shakes.

Some soil still flows.

The field becomes muddy.

But perhaps it did not fall all at once from the same place with the same force as before.

Perhaps the grass band divided the water flow a little.

Perhaps the covered areas retained a little more soil surface.

A record like that remains as the next observation point.

Part 4 has looked at many urban auxiliary wheels.

At the station front, records of dangerous heat led to rethinking equipment.

In the park, the way shade fell led to the placement of benches.

At the community center, anxiety about heat and heavy rain led to signs, spoken guidance, and maps.

On the balcony, the way heat accumulated led to the use of shading, space around the outdoor unit, and ventilation timing.

In this upstream field, the memory of collapse leads to how grass is left, how soil is cultivated, and how ridges are angled.

In other words, an auxiliary wheel is not only a new device brought in from outside.

It is also the act of moving information from a previous breakage into the next way of living and handling land.

Moving the memory of damage into the next form.

In this episode, while looking at the upstream field, the protagonist receives that as one answer.

From here, in Part 4, the water flows down toward the middle reaches.
