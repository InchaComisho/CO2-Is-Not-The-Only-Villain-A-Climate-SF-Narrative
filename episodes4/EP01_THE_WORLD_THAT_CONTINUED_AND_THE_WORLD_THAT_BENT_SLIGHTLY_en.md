# Part 4 Episode 1 — The world that continued as it was, and the world that bent slightly

## Foreword

This episode continues from Part 3 by showing a log of the reality line in which little changed on a large scale, while overlaying another world line in the same season and the same place: a world where the auxiliary wheels moved a little farther.

It was a day when extreme heat had been forecast.

The actual temperature came in slightly below the prediction, but more than enough heat remained in the city.

In this episode, the protagonist and the AI compare two screens: the world continuing here and now, and a world that bent only slightly.

---

“Didn’t they say it would be thirty-eight degrees today?”

Remembering the morning news, I looked at my phone again.

A forecast high of thirty-eight degrees in inland Kanto.

A possibility of more than thirty-five degrees even in central Tokyo.

Avoid unnecessary outings.

I had heard those words again and again since morning.

“It says around thirty-three where I am now.”

The number on the app was slightly lower than the morning forecast.

‘It came in below the forecast,’ the AI displayed from the device at the edge of my desk.

“It is still more than hot enough.”

Outside the window, the summer sky looked hazy and white.

Across the road, an invisible layer of heat seemed to rise from the asphalt.

It was not thirty-eight degrees.

That did not make it cool.

“How should Part 4 Episode 1 begin?”

‘Starting from this heat is the most natural choice.’

“As a log of the world that continues as it is.”

‘Yes.’

I opened the two folders I had created for Part 4.

One was titled, “The world where little changed on a large scale.”

The other was titled, “The world that began to move.”

The two folders that had been empty at the end of Part 3 would begin filling little by little today.

## Section 1 — The news we are seeing here and now: the world line where little changed

“First, I will write this as the reality-line log.”

I placed my hands on the keyboard.

— This summer, dangerous heat is once again in the news.

— The morning forecast predicted nearly thirty-eight degrees in inland Kanto.

— The announcer repeatedly said that central Tokyo might also exceed thirty-five degrees.

“In reality, the app says it is around thirty-three degrees here.”

‘Even so, it feels more than dangerous enough.’

“Heatstroke alert.”

“Please avoid going outside as much as possible.”

“Please use air conditioning appropriately even indoors.”

‘Words heard every year.’

“The fact that we hear them every year is not exactly normal.”

‘We have merely become accustomed to the abnormal.’

“That is a heavy way to put it.”

I remembered the station area I had walked through before.

The rotary.

The bus stop.

The pavement in front of the convenience store.

The crosswalk with little shade.

The narrow passage where outdoor air-conditioning units blew hot air.

“The station area is probably hellishly hot again today.”

‘Probably.’

“The mist is still available only during the event period.”

‘Yes.’

“Wind corridors exist in citizens’ bodily memories.”

‘But they have not been sufficiently returned to urban-planning maps.’

“Only people who know the shaded routes take a slight detour to use them.”

‘That is the city as it exists now.’

“What about the soil and watershed auxiliary wheels?”

‘They continue in small local sites.’

The upstream farmer is growing cover crops again this year.

The resident with the rain garden is removing clogged fallen leaves.

The municipal staff member is updating the watershed map after heavy rain.

“But they remain separate.”

‘They have not become a larger institution or a mechanism connecting multiple regions.’

“What about the sea?”

‘Blue Pulse remains at the concept and review stage.’

Could human intervention provide a small supplement to ocean heat and circulation?

Which waters could host a small-scale trial?

Who would monitor it, and who would stop it?

How should it be explained to fishers and coastal residents?

The questions exist.

The concept exists.

But it has not yet advanced to an offshore demonstration.

“A summer of high sea-surface temperatures passes casually through the news.”

‘Yes.’

“Stories about El Niño and marine heatwaves pass too.”

‘Yes.’

“But the ocean auxiliary wheel remains a concept.”

‘That is the summer of the world line that continues as it is.’

It was not a world where nothing happened.

There were small attempts.

There were people who continued them.

But they remained isolated points and did not connect on a larger scale.

That was the world displayed on the left screen.

## Section 2 — The AI opens another screen: the world line that moved only slightly

‘Now let us open the what-if line,’ the AI said.

“The same summer?”

‘The same summer.’

In my mind, I opened another window on the right.

The same date.

The same season.

The same city.

But a world where slightly different choices had been made at several branch points.

I typed one line first.

— From here onward, this is the log of a world line where the auxiliary wheels moved a little farther.

Then I continued.

— The same summer.

— The same season when the phrase dangerous heat appears in the news.

— But a world line where auxiliary wheels have entered institutions and design a little more deeply in several cities, watersheds, and seas.

‘Start with the station?’

“Yes.”

— This year, a mist line was installed at the station rotary as shared infrastructure.

— It does not operate only on event days. Fine mist starts automatically during periods of high heatstroke risk when operating conditions such as temperature and humidity are met.

— The bus-stop roof includes shading that does not block too much wind, with a small green area and benches around it.

‘The air temperature itself does not fall dramatically.’

“The whole city has not become cool.”

‘No.’

“But a place where people could not stand for even a few minutes became a place where they could wait until the bus arrived.”

‘That is an air auxiliary wheel.’

Previously, the station heat had been treated as something unavoidable.

In this world line, it had begun to be treated as something that could be changed slightly through design.

It was not complete cooling.

There was simply one more shaded refuge.

One more place where people could wait.

Even so, extending the time before someone collapsed had meaning.

‘What about the watershed?’

“Like this.”

— Multiple watersheds share observational records about sponge-like upstream soil, midstream rain gardens, and downstream flood-storage areas.

— Rather than using the same method everywhere, each region adopts only the parts suitable for its own terrain, soil, and land use.

— When heavy rain is forecast, municipal terminals display not only rainfall predictions but also water levels in rain gardens and regulating ponds, remaining flood-storage capacity, and locations where water concentrated in the past.

— Not every river.

— Only several watersheds.

— Even so, the number of rivers able to prepare a little earlier is increasing compared with rivers that face heavy rain without knowing anything.

‘Did flooding disappear?’

“No.”

‘Landslides?’

“They still happen.”

‘Then what changed?’

“Records of places that held and places that became dangerous began passing to the next watershed.”

‘Not only successes?’

“Failures too.”

Rain still fell in this world line.

Some rivers still overtopped their levees.

Some slopes still collapsed.

But failures no longer disappeared inside a single municipality. They became material that helped the next place avoid repeating the same mistake.

That was the world where soil and watershed auxiliary wheels had spread a little farther.

‘And the sea?’

“I will not make it a world where every El Niño weakened.”

I opened the Blue Pulse log.

— Blue Pulse, which had remained at the concept stage, advanced to a monitored small-scale demonstration in a limited area off Chile.

— Operating conditions, observation ranges, and stopping criteria were established, and fishers, researchers, and government shared the data.

— After several years of observation, an even more limited operation began in part of the waters off Peru.

— In one year, during a season when El Niño development had been forecast, there was an area where the period of sea-surface warming ended slightly sooner than expected.

— However, it has not yet been determined whether that was an effect of Blue Pulse alone or a combination with natural variability.

— At the fishing port that year, both an ambiguous impression — it was better than an ordinarily bad year — and numerical records of sea-surface temperature, currents, and catch volume remained.

‘Global sea-surface temperatures did not return to normal.’

“Of course not.”

‘El Niño was not erased.’

“No.”

‘It is not a stage where success can be declared.’

“But data remained suggesting that continued observation might be worthwhile.”

‘That is why it is a world line that bent slightly.’

“Exactly.”

A line that had been moving straight bent only slightly in another direction.

The world itself was not saved.

Only one season.

One sea area.

One watershed.

One station front.

The damage or burden there may have become slightly lighter than expected.

That was the scale of the bend.

## Section 3 — Both world lines were hot

On the left screen was the summer of the world line where little changed on a large scale.

On the right was the summer of the world line where the auxiliary wheels moved only slightly.

‘What do you think when you compare them?’ the AI asked.

“Both are hot.”

I answered after thinking for a moment.

‘That is your first impression?’

“It is true.”

‘Yes.’

“The right-hand world line has not become a comfortable summer either.”

‘No.’

“In the left-hand world line, the dangerous-heat news continues.”

“The station rotary remains a place where people want to avoid standing.”

“News of record-high sea-surface temperatures, heavy rain, and heatwaves keeps piling up again and again.”

‘The world line where little changed.’

“In the right-hand world line, the dangerous-heat news continues too.”

‘Yes.’

“But people can wait for the bus a little longer at the station.”

‘Yes.’

“In several watersheds, the failures of previous heavy rains pass to the next region.”

‘Yes.’

“In some sea areas, records remain suggesting that the year may have been better than an ordinarily bad year.”

‘A world line where auxiliary wheels entered institutions and design a little farther.’

“That is probably the extent of the difference.”

‘Do you think it is small?’

“It is small.”

I answered honestly.

“But for people living inside that small difference, it may not be small.”

‘Someone avoids collapsing at the bus stop.’

“Not all the soil in a field washes away.”

‘An evacuation decision comes a little sooner.’

“A fishing port’s poor catch is less severe than in the worst year.”

‘That kind of difference.’

I compared the two screens.

The left side was reality.

The right side was not a complete dream either.

But the right side contained several decisions that had not yet been chosen in reality.

A decision to allocate a budget.

A decision to accept maintenance responsibility.

A decision to share records of failure.

A decision to proceed to a monitored demonstration.

‘Which world line do you think is correct?’ the AI asked.

“That is not for me to decide.”

‘You said that in Part 3.’

“My job is to place the two beside each other.”

I typed the final sentences.

— This is the log of the world line where little changed on a large scale.

— This is the log of the world line where the auxiliary wheels moved a little farther.

— Neither is a story of complete salvation.

— Both contain heat.

— Both contain heavy rain.

— Both contain failure.

— But at least I want to leave the slightly bent world line in writing as something better.

‘Then Part 4 Episode 1 is the episode that confirms that.’

“Yes.”

I saved both logs.

On the left screen was today’s temperature.

On the right was another station front on the same day.

Outside the window, the sky looked equally white and hazy in both worlds.

The difference was not large enough to cover the whole world.

One person could stand for a few minutes longer.

Water could be received once by soil or a garden.

A record of ocean change could remain and inform the next decision.

Perhaps world lines begin to bend through differences that small.

Part 4 Episode 1 ends here.

## Afterword

Part 4 Episode 1 begins actually using the two pens decided upon in Part 3: the reality-line log and the what-if-line log.

The same season.

The same city.

A summer described in both worlds as dangerous heat.

Within it, we place side by side the world line where little changed on a large scale and the world line where the auxiliary wheels moved a little farther.

In the reality line, even when the temperature comes in slightly below the morning forecast, more than enough heat remains in the city.

Station rotaries and bus stops still have little shade or permanent cooling infrastructure.

Mist is installed as a seasonal event, while wind corridors may exist in citizens’ bodily experience but are not sufficiently reflected in urban plans or budgets.

In soil and watersheds, farmers, residents, and municipalities continue small attempts.

However, the records remain scattered and do not become a mechanism connecting multiple watersheds.

Blue Pulse, the ocean auxiliary wheel, also remains at the concept and review stage in the reality line and has not advanced to an offshore demonstration.

This is not a world where nothing is being done.

Small attempts exist.

But they do not connect to larger institutions or broader regions, and they do not reach the point of significantly bending the present course.

In the what-if line, different choices are made at several branch points.

At stations and bus stops, shade, greenery, structures that do not block too much wind, and mist lines operated according to conditions are introduced as shared infrastructure.

They do not dramatically lower the air temperature.

But they may turn a place where people cannot wait even a few minutes into a place where they can wait until the bus arrives.

In soil and watersheds, observational records of sponge-like soil, rain gardens, and flood-storage areas are shared among multiple regions.

Rather than installing the same system in every river, each region adopts only the parts suitable for its terrain, soil, and land use.

Heavy rain and landslides do not disappear.

But records of both success and failure pass to the next region, slightly reducing the chance of repeating the same mistake.

At sea, Blue Pulse advances from the concept stage to a monitored small-scale demonstration in a limited area off Chile.

Later, after repeated observation and adjustment, an even more limited operation takes place in part of the waters off Peru.

In one year, a record remains showing that the period of sea-surface warming ended sooner than expected in an area where El Niño development had been forecast.

However, it cannot be concluded that this was caused by Blue Pulse alone.

It must be distinguished from natural variability.

Even so, combined records of sea-surface temperature, currents, catch volume, and fishers’ experience remain, allowing consideration of whether continued observation is worthwhile.

This world line is not a world of universal salvation either.

Extreme heat continues.

Heavy rain still occurs.

Marine heatwaves and El Niño do not disappear.

But people can wait a little longer at the station.

Watershed failures pass to the next region.

Ocean changes remain as records that can inform the next decision.

Those small differences are depicted as a world line that bent slightly.

The protagonist’s role is not to force one world line as the correct answer.

In the world line where little changed, he writes what failed to move and why.

In the world line where auxiliary wheels moved a little, he writes what changed and what new responsibilities and problems appeared.

He places the two logs side by side and shows where the small choices that separated the world lines were made.

That is the protagonist’s role in Part 4.

Part 4 Episode 1 confirms that neither world line is easy, while the protagonist quietly chooses to preserve the slightly bent world line as something better.
