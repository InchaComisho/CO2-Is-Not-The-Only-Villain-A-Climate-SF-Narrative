# Part 4 Episode 2 — Summer at the station front, and summer at a station front that bent slightly

## Foreword

In Part 4 Episode 1, we placed the world line that continued as it was and the world line that bent slightly side by side in the air of the same summer day.

In Episode 2, the stage narrows to one point: the station-front rotary.

In the world line where little changed on a large scale, it is the usual station front in summer.

In the world line that bent slightly, auxiliary wheels have become one of the assumptions built into the city.

This episode has the protagonist and the AI compare the two from the distance of everyday life.

---

“Just walking to the station takes a little resolve today.”

The moment I opened the front door, it felt as if my face had struck a layer of heat.

The air was already heavy in the morning.

The number I had seen in yesterday’s forecast flickered at the edge of my mind.

“Around thirty-three degrees here.”

I checked the temperature on my phone.

“But the station front will probably feel hotter than that.”

‘There is less shade, and more heat from pavement, buildings, and vehicles,’ the AI replied briefly from the device.

“Let’s make Part 4 Episode 2 about summer at the station front.”

‘A good subject.’

I opened the two logs for Part 4.

The same station.

The same time.

The same summer described as dangerous heat.

Into that, I placed two station fronts that differed only slightly.

---

## Section 1 — The station front in the world line where little changed

There was almost no shade on the road to the station.

Apartment walls.

A flat parking lot.

A convenience-store sign.

Rows of vending machines.

The road reflecting the sun looked hot before I even stepped on it.

“The usual summer.”

‘Yes.’

I wrote in the reality-line log.

— There has been no major change at the station-front rotary this year either.

— The bus-stop roof is a single thin sheet of metal.

— It is the minimum structure needed to keep off rain, rather than something designed to protect people from summer heat.

There was shade beneath the roof.

But the shade itself held heat.

Even sitting on the bench, hot air curled around from behind my back.

“The mist is not running today either.”

I remembered the mist hung for last year’s shopping-street event.

Children had run beneath the spray, and passersby had slowed their steps for a moment.

But now there was nothing.

— The place where fine mist drifted only during the event has returned to being an ordinary hot space.

— Even when the morning news repeats the phrase dangerous heat, the equipment at the station front does not automatically begin operating in response.

‘There are many people.’

“Yes.”

Even before noon, people had accumulated around the rotary.

People waiting for buses.

People meeting someone.

People standing still while looking at their phones.

Trying to fit beneath the roof’s shadow, they lined up inside a narrow strip of shade.

— Each person endures the heat through individual measures: cold drinks, hats, parasols, towels, and portable fans.

— The rotary itself remains a place that says, protect your own body yourself, leaving almost everything to the individual.

‘This is the station front in the world line where little changed.’

“Yes.”

No one was completely unprepared.

Citizens were making efforts.

They carried drinks.

They tried to avoid the hottest hours.

But the space itself had barely changed.

I stepped into the thin shade under the roof and breathed slightly through my shoulders.

“I came into the city, but the city does not help much.”

‘A system for helping is not built in as an assumption.’

“So it feels like enduring with only personal auxiliary wheels.”

‘That is one way to put it.’

## Section 2 — The station front in the world line that bent slightly

‘Now let us look at the station front in the world line that bent slightly,’ the AI said.

I opened the log on the right.

Then I wrote one line first.

— From here begins the record of a world line where auxiliary wheels moved slightly farther and became one of the assumptions of the station front.

The same summer.

The same time.

The same station-front rotary.

But in this world line, when heatstroke risk rises, the station front itself moves slightly to the side that supports people.

“For example, like this.”

I imagined another station front.

— The bus-stop roof has been updated with enough thickness and width to consider not only rain protection, but also shielding summer sunlight.

— Solar panels are installed on the roof, and beneath them is a structure that stores less heat, with eaves extending shade into the surrounding area.

— A small planting strip and fixed-pipe mist equipment are built into part of the roof and the edge of the rotary.

‘A world line where mist became equipment rather than an event.’

“Yes.”

— The mist does not spray unconditionally simply because a heatstroke alert has been issued.

— It runs gently only during periods when fine droplets are likely to evaporate sufficiently, while monitoring temperature, humidity, wind speed, and how people are using the area.

— It is not equipment meant to soak people. It supplements shade and airflow by using the cooling effect created when water evaporates and draws heat from its surroundings.

‘Simply adding moisture to the air is not enough.’

“If humidity is too high, or there is no wind, it needs to stop.”

‘Hygiene management and nozzle inspection are also necessary.’

“It is not magic fog.”

— The mist is not a flashy performance.

— It lowers the temperature of the air moving through the shade by a small amount in a limited area.

— Even that creates one more space where a person can think, I can stay here for a few minutes, instead of waiting in direct sunlight.

‘What about the wind?’

“This would also be a world where wind paths were reconsidered in the plans.”

— Several years earlier, a small renovation reviewed vehicle and pedestrian movement through the rotary.

— At that time, a condition was set: no high wall or sealed waiting room would be placed in this direction, so that wind entering from the surroundings would not be completely blocked.

— Places where vehicle exhaust heat tends to accumulate were separated slightly from places where people wait, and the layout was adjusted so wind could pass through shade, planting, and mist.

‘They did not create the wind itself.’

“They only made it harder to block the wind that already existed.”

‘But that too is design.’

“So design is not only about what to build, but also what not to block.”

Even in this world line, the station front was not a cool place.

The asphalt was hot.

Cars still moved through it.

The outdoor temperature was high.

If the bus was late, waiting was still difficult.

Even so.

— The time before a person feels, I cannot stand here any longer, is extended slightly.

— People can move from a place without shade to a place with shade and wind.

— There is a bench where someone feeling unwell can sit, and a place to refill cold water.

— Even when an individual could not prepare everything personally, the station-front space itself has a minimum set of auxiliary wheels.

“At a station front like that, I might feel that the city is enduring the heat with us, at least a little.”

‘The space does not treat heat as only an individual problem.’

“So that is the difference in the bent world line.”

## Section 3 — What the protagonist says after placing the two station fronts side by side

I placed the two station fronts side by side in my mind.

On the left.

A thin roof built only to keep off rain.

Mist hung only during events.

A rotary with nothing there on ordinary days.

People enduring the heat with drinks, hats, and parasols.

On the right.

A broad roof that blocks sunlight.

Fixed mist equipment operated according to conditions.

Planting and benches.

A layout that separates exhaust heat from waiting areas and makes it harder to block wind.

‘How does it look?’ the AI asked.

“Both are station fronts used by people who live here.”

I put it into words slowly.

“At the station front on the left, people protect their own bodies with hats, towels, and cold drinks.”

‘Yes.’

“They still need those things at the station front on the right.”

‘Auxiliary wheels do not make personal heat precautions unnecessary.’

“But on the right, the roof, wind, mist, and benches also support people a little.”

‘The space shares some of the burden, not only the individual.’

“That is the difference.”

It was not a dramatic difference.

The maximum temperature did not fall by five degrees.

Extremely hot days did not disappear.

But the boundary of daily life moved slightly.

— In this story, auxiliary wheels are not magic that turns extreme heat into a comfortable summer.

— But they can shift the boundary from I cannot stand here any longer to I can somehow stay here this long.

‘At a station front in summer, that difference affects daily choices directly.’

“Whether to wait for the bus.”

‘Yes.’

“Whether to change the time you leave home.”

‘Yes.’

“Whether to walk one station or use public transport.”

‘Yes.’

“Whether an older person or a child can go outside alone.”

‘The presence or absence of auxiliary wheels at the station front affects all of those decisions.’

I looked at the two rotaries on the screen.

“I do not intend to declare which station front is the one correct answer.”

‘You place them side by side as two logs.’

“This is the log of a station front where almost no auxiliary wheels were introduced.”

‘Yes.’

“And this is the log of a station front where auxiliary wheels entered slightly as an assumption of the city.”

— Both are station fronts that could exist in reality.

— Neither is perfect.

— Neither is made only of despair.

— The difference is whether dangerous heat is carried only by individuals.

— Or whether the city’s space carries a small part of it too.

‘Part 4 Episode 2 is an episode for seeing that difference.’

“It works as an entrance into the urban summer.”

I returned my eyes to the real bus stop.

Under the thin roof, an older woman moved a folding fan.

A short distance away, a student pressed a cold plastic bottle to his neck.

It was not that no one was thinking.

Everyone was doing what they could.

And precisely because of that.

Could the city add one small auxiliary wheel to those efforts?

The station front on the right was born from that question.

Part 4 Episode 2 ends here.

## Afterword

Part 4 Episode 2 narrowed the setting to one place, the station-front rotary, and placed side by side the summer of the world line where little changed and the summer of the world line that bent slightly, through the viewpoint of the protagonist and the AI.

At the station front in the world line where little changed, the bus-stop roof remains a minimum structure mainly intended to keep off rain.

Mist installed for an event does not exist during ordinary days.

Even when the morning news reports dangerous heat and heatstroke alerts, that information does not automatically cause the station-front space to change.

Users endure the heat with measures they prepare themselves: drinks, hats, parasols, towels, and portable fans.

Citizens are not doing nothing.

Rather, each person is making as many efforts as possible.

But the station-front space still leaves almost all heat response to individuals.

At the station front in the world line that bent slightly, the space itself also carries some of the heat burden.

The bus stop has a roof designed to block solar radiation.

Solar panels, small planting areas, and fixed-pipe mist equipment are built into the roof and its surroundings.

However, the mist does not spray unconditionally in response to a heatstroke alert.

It operates only when temperature, humidity, wind speed, and other conditions allow droplets to evaporate sufficiently and provide cooling.

When humidity is too high, or wind is weak and droplets would linger, operation is reduced.

Nozzle cleaning, water quality, hygiene management, and maintenance costs are also necessary.

The mist is not used alone to cool the entire station front. It is one part of a heat countermeasure that combines shade, wind, planting, benches, and places to obtain water.

The wind path does not require rebuilding the whole city on a massive scale either.

When the rotary is renovated, structures that completely block wind are avoided.

Places where vehicle exhaust heat accumulates are separated slightly from places where people wait.

Walls and equipment are arranged so existing wind can pass beneath the shade.

Such small design changes are accumulated.

Even in this world line, the station front does not become a cool place.

An extremely hot day remains an extremely hot day.

Users still need individual measures such as drinks and parasols.

Even so, there is a possibility of changing a place where people cannot stand for even a few minutes into a place where they can wait until the bus arrives.

The greatest difference described in this episode is not the number or luxury of the facilities.

Does the individual alone carry dangerous heat?

Or does the city’s space carry a small part of it too?

That is the difference.

Auxiliary wheels are not magic that erases extreme heat.

But they can shift the boundary from I cannot remain here any longer to I can somehow remain here this long.

That difference directly affects everyday decisions: whether someone can wait for a bus, whether they must change the time they go out, and whether older people or children can move safely.

The protagonist does not impose either of the two station fronts as the one correct answer.

A station front where almost no auxiliary wheels were introduced.

A station front where auxiliary wheels entered slightly as an assumption of the city.

He places the two side by side and records how the burden differs for the people who live there.

In this way, Part 4 will examine specific places that make up the urban summer through two world lines: the world where little changed on a large scale and the world that bent slightly.

The next episode shifts the viewpoint to those who actually maintain the station-front auxiliary wheels — the people who manage the equipment, bear the costs, and decide when to stop it — and follows the difference between building something and continuing it.
